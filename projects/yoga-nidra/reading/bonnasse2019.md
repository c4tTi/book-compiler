## In one paragraph
The publisher calls this "the most complete work to date" on yoga nidra; treat that as a marketing line. It is a 542-page French manual that combines a treatise with a practice library.
- **The library:** 108 short exercises written as guided meditations and drawn from yoga, Vedānta, tantra and Buddhism. Each can be practised **on its own**, sitting or lying, at any time of day or night. They are also meant to be **"conjugated"**: combined, by a method the book explains, into one's own yoga nidra sessions, with "an infinity" of possible combinations.
- **The theory:** the book places yoga nidra in its traditional, mythological and philosophical setting, drawing on ancient texts, "modern research" and the author's own experience.
- **Readers:** it is written for yoga and meditation practitioners. It is also the main study text of the author's 120-hour yoga nidra teacher training, so in practice it is a **teacher's manual**.
- **The promise is spiritual, not clinical:**
  - The book claims that cultivating deep relaxation, "attention without tension", discernment, letting go and detachment leads to effortless acceptance of whatever happens.
  - It also claims to lead beyond suffering, and to recognition of the unchanging Self that pervades and transcends waking, dreaming and deep sleep.
  - Death is then no longer feared as an end, but felt as a reminder of the joy of being alive.

## Core ideas
Sources for this section: the publisher's text, Le Roy's posted passage, the *Esprit Yoga* carte blanche and the school's pages. Each bullet says which it comes from.
- **Modularity is the method** (publisher). Practices are units. You learn them singly, then assemble sessions to suit yourself or others. This is the 2015 book's "115 practices to inspire and create your sessions" promoted from the last section of Part III to the organising principle of a whole book.
- **True meditation is not a doing** (excerpt). Its essence is our natural state. It is neither repeating a formula nor scanning the body from end to end, though he concedes most of us have to pass through such techniques. At most, it is resting as the empty, living space of awareness, the pure "I am", without identifying with its contents.
- **Don't try to stay awake; recognise what doesn't sleep** (excerpt).
  - Watching oneself fall asleep is called one of the best frames for this recognition.
  - He says outright that any **effort** to keep awareness going into sleep fails. He rejects, as a mistaken definition he says is common, the idea of keeping one part of the mind awake while the rest sleeps, because the ego doing the trying is a thought and vanishes when the body falls asleep.
  - The point is to recognise that the awareness of falling asleep does not itself fall asleep. In the same way, the awareness of pain does not hurt, and the awareness of red is not red.
  - This is a notable move away from the 2015 book's ethos of vigilance (see "What's distinctive").
- **Nothing to produce or attain** (excerpt). Yoga nidra does not try to introduce, build or reach anything. It only invites recognition of what is already present (quote 1 below). The waking "person" is seen to be as unstable as a dream: it appears with waking and birth, and disappears in deep sleep and death.
- **Joyful acceptance** (*Esprit Yoga*, July 2019, published with "dernier ouvrage paru" pointing to this book). Real acceptance is a full "yes" with no "but". Trying to change oneself is still a refusal. Instead, inquire into who is not accepting. The "me" that resists turns out to be a sensation, emotion or thought, seen by an unaffected light. This matches the back cover's promise of "accepting without effort" all of life's events.
- **The three states and the Self** (publisher). Practices explore "all the structures of being and states of consciousness" so that the Self underlying waking, dream and deep sleep can reveal itself. This continues the 2015 book's Māṇḍūkya frame; the training studies the Māṇḍūkya and Gauḍapāda's *asparśa-yoga*.
- **Death reframed** (publisher). The "sleep of yoga" changes how death is seen: no longer an anxious end, but a constant reminder of an unsuspected joy in living. The training includes preparation for sleep and death.
- **A mythological frame** (publisher: "contexte … mythologique"). The book places yoga nidra in its myth, presumably the goddess Yoganidrā of the Purāṇas. Bonnasse later developed this into a whole book, *Yoganidrā, la légende de la Déesse* (Almora, 2023, 608 pp.), and a devotional *Śrī Yoganidrā Stotram* (2020). How much goddess material the 2019 book contains is not known.
- **Sources across traditions** (publisher, school). Vedānta and tantra sit next to Buddhist meditation (Vipassana, Zazen, Dzogchen in the training). Medieval yoga texts appear too: his site gives French translations, unattributed and presumably his own, of *Yogatārāvalī* 25–26 (yoganidrā as a concept-free, blissful sleep in the "fourth") and *Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra* 75 (awareness at the onset of sleep). Whether these verses appear in the book is likely but not verified.

## The practice as this book teaches it
**Caution:** I have not seen a single practice or the combination method. This section separates what is **confirmed**, what is **reported** by the school or a reader, and what is my **inference** from the 2015 book.

**The unit and the assembly (confirmed, publisher):**
- **Unit:** one of 108 exercises, written as a guided meditation.
- **Posture:** sitting or lying.
- **When:** any time of day or night.
- **Use:** alone, or "conjugated" with others into a session by a stated method.
- **Length:** not stated. The training distinguishes "short sessions & long sessions". In 2015 a full session ran "an hour or more".
- **Language:** the excerpt addresses the reader directly as *vous*. It capitalises *Conscience*, *Soi* and *Saveur*, and is contemplative and non-dual. I have not seen the script voice of the practices themselves (directive or invitational).

**Practice families named by others (reported):**
- The one Goodreads reviewer (2020) singles out three practices: **"the dissolution of the 5 elements", "listening to the inner sound" and "the joy of being"**.
- The training programme adds:
  - breath work, sounds, images and silence, yantra, archetypes, "visions", "the animal";
  - dream and night practices;
  - death preparation;
  - the "in-between" moments of the *Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra*;
  - meditation with and without an object.

**Stage by stage: the likely grammar.** The 2015 book already offered its micro-practices for building sessions around a nine-phase protocol. The 2019 training still teaches "structure & stages of a session; the Heart". The table below uses the slots from `05` so you can line it up with other books.

**Sankalpa.** Not seen for 2019. Two clues:
- the 2015 method was a breath-held resolve, heard again at the end;
- the 2019 training files resolve and intention under "variations".

Together they *suggest*, but do not prove, that sankalpa is now optional or variable. The excerpt's "nothing to attain" stance also sits uneasily with a goal-type resolve. Check this in the book.

**Sleep guidance.** This is the clearest thing known about the 2019 book:
- falling asleep is welcomed as a field of observation;
- effortful wakefulness is rejected as the ego trying;
- the student is pointed to the awareness that is never asleep.

Compare Miller's "awareness is present anyway" position (`08` §3). It is closer to that than to Satyananda's "stay awake".

## What's distinctive
- **Probably the most explicitly modular single-author book in the set.** Lusk offers short, modular scripts and Verma separate full scripts. Satyananda gives fixed scripts. Nidrā Shakti [@dinsmoretuli2022] gives 108 *complete* practices from more than 30 facilitators; a dozen of them (the Kerala teaching practices) share one nine-component structure. Bonnasse instead offers 108 standalone exercises meant as building blocks, plus a method for assembling them. The research brief for this summary calls it "the closest French counterpart to the Nidra Shakti treasury". That is fair on scale and on the 108 number, but the logic differs: a combinable toolkit here, an anthology of finished sessions there.
- **It came first.** Bonnasse's 108-practice book (2019) predates Nidrā Shakti's 108-practice Treasury (2024) by five years. Both use the mālā number, and both later turn to the goddess Yoganidrā (Bonnasse 2023; Dinsmore-Tuli 2024). I found no sign that either influenced the other.
- **A changed stance on falling asleep, compared with his own 2015 book.** In 2015, and in the 2016 English blurb on his site, yoga nidra put body and mind to sleep "while keeping the awareness alert", with much talk of vigilance and effort. The 2019 passage rejects the "one part of the mind stays awake" definition as mistaken and says that effort fails. This moves him toward the "state, not technique" pole of debates 2 and 3 (`08`), without adopting Dinsmore-Tuli's "sleep is fine" ethic.
- **Day and night by design.** Every practice can be done at any hour, sitting or lying. The sleep threshold, the night and the moment of waking stay central, as in 2015.
- **A textbook used in training.** It is the main study text of a 120-hour teacher training (Rishikesh and Pyrenees) that the school says the FFEY validates. Compare Miller's book, which is required reading for iRest Level 1 (`02`).
- **An unusually wide set of sources.** Advaita (the Inchegeri line), Kashmir Śaiva and Śākta tantra, the Himalayan tradition, Nātha yoga and Buddhist meditation, with medieval sources such as the *Yogatārāvalī* and the *Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra*.

## Strengths and limitations
**Strengths**
- **Scale.** It is the largest single-author yoga nidra practice book in French that I can identify. I did not survey every French title, so "largest" is a probability, not a measured fact.
- **A modular architecture.** It is designed for teachers who want to build sessions, not just read scripts aloud.
- **Grounded in texts.** A bibliography of about 9–14 pages and a glossary. The author translates Sanskrit himself (Devī Gītā 2021).
- **Tested in teaching.** It is currently the core text of a 120-hour training. I did not find when that started.
- **A more forgiving view of sleep than his 2015 book.** It answers the "I always fall asleep" problem philosophically, without abandoning the contemplative aim.
- **Spiritual rather than clinical claims** (from what I could see), so it largely avoids the health overclaiming criticised in `06` §6.3.

**Limitations / critiques**
- **No independent critical review found.** The reception I could find:
  - José Le Roy's blog post is by a self-described friend. He had only "begun reading" it, calls it "very complete", and mostly reproduces Bonnasse's text.
  - Zuihô, editor of *Livres Bouddhistes*, calls it a "Bible du Yoga-Nidrâ de 542 pages", "intelligent, érudit, pratique". I saw only the excerpt that the author chose to quote on his own site; the original page is gone.
  - Goodreads has 2 ratings (both 5★). The one text review (Sept 2020) calls it a very good tool for teaching yoga nidra.
  - The publisher's "media" tab lists only the author's own *Esprit Yoga* carte blanche.
- **Hard to get and only in French.** Print is out of stock, the ebook pagination differs from print, and there is no translation.
- **The combination method is unseen.** "An infinity of combinations" is attractive, but without clear rules it can produce incoherent sessions. Whether the book gives sequencing rules, timings and contraindications is unknown. The training's talk of "creativity" suggests a lot is left to the teacher.
- **Heavily non-dual and spiritual.** Talk of the ego as bound to vanish, and the capitalised Self and Consciousness, will not suit secular, clinical or rest-first readers without translation (`08` §4).
- **Safety is unknown.** Death contemplation, dissolution of the elements and the "in-between" practices (fainting, sexual ecstasy) need trauma-aware handling. I saw no evidence either way about safety guidance (`07` §7.1–7.2). The 2015 book's breath-retention sankalpa also needs a no-hold option if it is kept.
- **Unknown handling of history and research.** The publisher says the book uses "modern research", but I could not check how.
  - The 2015 book had a shaky Satyananda chronology, and its English edition did not credit Boyes [@boyes1973].
  - It also stated that Swami Rama "proved" a conscious delta state, which is anecdote, not proof (`06` §6.1, §6.3).
  - Assume these may recur until you have checked.
- **"The most complete work to date" is marketing.** It was arguable in 2019. Nidrā Shakti (704 pp., 2024) is now larger.

## For your own book
**Take**
- **Copy the architecture, not the content.** Build a **practice library plus a combining grammar**:
  - Write each practice as a self-contained unit with a fixed header: slot (`05`: settling, intention, body, breath, feeling, image, awareness, return), duration, posture (lying or sitting), best time (bedtime, night waking, morning, daytime), prerequisites and cautions.
  - Then give 3–4 tested "recipes" (e.g. 10-minute bedtime, 25-minute classic, 45-minute deep, a seated version).
  - This is what Bonnasse promises. You can make it more transparent and safer by stating the sequencing rules outright. It also gives a middle answer to debate 7 (`08`): one core sequence plus interchangeable parts.
- **Tag by time of day.** His insistence that every practice works "at any moment of the day and night", together with his 2015 night exercises, argues for a bedtime, night-waking and morning section, with every practice marked by when it suits.
- **Use his falling-asleep reframe for debate 3.** Neither scold sleepers nor drop the contemplative aim. Invite readers to notice the onset of sleep, and to recognise that what notices it was not asleep. Ground it in *Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra* 75 from a scholarly translation, not via Bonnasse. Say plainly that dozing off still has value (`06` §6.4, "sleep-onset training").
- **Study two practice families readers single out: dissolution of the elements and inner sound.**
  - Trace dissolution of the elements to *bhūtaśuddhi* and the *tattva* tradition.
  - Trace inner sound to *nāda* (Haṭhapradīpikā ch. 4, via Mallinson & Singleton [@mallinson2017]).
  - Then write your own versions. Both suit a "deepening" chapter.
- **A history lead.** The French translation of *Yogatārāvalī* 25–26 on his site (presumably his own) is a good primary witness that medieval *yoganidrā* meant a concept-free absorption in the "fourth". This supports the Birch & Hargreaves reading [@birch2015; @bhattacharyayt]. Quote from a scholarly edition, not his paraphrase.
- **French context.** If your book discusses Francophone yoga nidra, this is the current reference manual and a training textbook. Cite it with the 2015 book [@bonnasse2015fr], his 2023 goddess book, and Boyes [@boyes1973] as landmarks of French yoga nidra.

**Skip or treat critically**
- **Don't reuse "108".** The symbolic count is now taken twice (Bonnasse 2019, Dinsmore-Tuli 2024). Choose a number your structure actually needs.
- **Don't promise "infinite combinations".** Give rules instead: which slots are required, which are optional, maximum lengths, and what never to combine (e.g. no death contemplation in a 10-minute bedtime practice; no breath retention for anxious, pregnant or cardiac students).
- **Frame the non-dual metaphysics as one option.** If your readers include secular or clinical students, offer the "recognise awareness" language as one way of putting it, alongside rest-based and body-based language (`08` §4).
- **Death and dissolution practices** need an explicit opt-out, grief and trauma notes, and should not be the default (`07`).
- **Don't use it as a source for history or research** until you have read those chapters and checked them against `01` and `06`. Treat any "ancient" or "proven" claim as a claim.
- **Practicalities.** If you quote it, cite the 542-page print edition (BnF) and check the page numbers yourself. Borrow nothing verbatim: it is in copyright, untranslated, and any English rendering would be your own translation.
- **Is it worth buying?** If you read French and are designing modular scripts, yes: it is the closest model of what you may be building. If not, the 2015/2017 book gives you the protocol, and this summary gives you the architecture.
