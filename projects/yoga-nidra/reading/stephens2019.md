## In one paragraph
A mainstream, science-forward sleep manual from a well-known yoga teacher-trainer, whom the publisher copy bills as a "reformed insomniac". Part 1, "Foundations", covers sleep physiology, sleep disorders and their treatments, and a yogic reading of all this through the Yoga Sūtra and the kośas. Part 2, "Practices", gives condition- and age-specific sequences: a basic sleep sequence, and sequences for hyperarousal, for depression and lethargy, for young and old, for sleep apnea, and for chair yoga. Yoga nidra is one tool among many. It appears as the closing relaxation of the anti-hyperarousal sequence, not as the book's method.

## Core ideas
As inferred from the TOC, chapter list and publisher copy, not from the text:
- Insomnia is mainly a **hyperarousal** problem, so the down-regulating practices (including nidra) are grouped in the hyperarousal chapter.
- Sleep science comes first: the two-process model, sleep architecture and the ICSD categories frame everything that follows. The three Foundations chapters take about 3 of the audiobook's 8.7 hours.
- Yoga is presented **alongside, and partly as an alternative to**, drugs and CBT-I. The book discusses CBT-I seriously, but going by the Italian TOC it also gives a sub-section to doubts about it.
- Different problems call for different practices: calming for hyperarousal, energizing for depression/lethargy, and breath and tongue work for apnea.
- The yogic frame is classical-eclectic (eight limbs, kośas, kleśas, guṇas), not tantric or lineage-specific.
- Self-assessment comes before prescription (the sleep-assessment appendix).

## What's distinctive
- Within this project it is the **sleep-medicine-literate yoga book**: two-process model, ICSD, CBT-I and apnea are all covered. The dedicated nidra books (Satyananda, Miller, Desai, Moore) do not give insomnia this clinical framing.
- It places nidra **inside a hyperarousal protocol** instead of treating it as a stand-alone practice. That is a useful model if your book has a sleep chapter.
- Compared with **Sanfilippo** (*Yoga Therapy for Insomnia and Sleep Recovery*, rejected because yoga nidra is one tool among many), Stephens has the same profile. Nidra is even more peripheral here: one step in one chapter. As a nidra source it fails for the same reason. Its only value is as sleep-science and CBT-I framing, hence the proposed `02` §F entry above rather than a place among the nidra books.
- Measured against `06`:
  - **§6.3:** whether the book repeats the "1 hour of yoga nidra = 4 hours of sleep" lore or claims delta waves during nidra is **unknown**. The publisher copy makes no nidra-specific claims.
  - **The 65% statistic:** the headline claim that most US adults are sleep-deprived ("over 65 percent", in publisher copy) has no source on the page. For comparison, the CDC's 2014 BRFSS analysis (MMWR 65(6)) found that 65.2% of adults reported a *healthy* sleep duration (7 hours or more), which means about 35% slept less than 7 hours. The blurb's figure is roughly the mirror image of that. It may be a misreading of the CDC number, or it may come from a broader, poll-based definition such as "feel they don't get enough sleep". Don't reuse it without seeing Stephens' endnote source.
  - **Alzheimer's/plaque clearance:** a popular and still-developing research area, so treat any strong claim there with care.
  - **The sleep-specific evidence for nidra next to CBT-I** is thin. Only two items in `06` compare yoga nidra with CBT-I: Datta 2021 (§6.2: one RCT, n=41, both groups improved) and Dutta 2026 (§6.0: 6 RCTs, n=244, comparators include CBT-I, PMR and music, moderate–high risk of bias). Singh 2026 (§6.0, insomnia/poor sleep, 5 usable studies) found pooled effects that were large but not statistically significant, with **very low** GRADE certainty. NCCIH (§6.5) notes stronger evidence for CBT than for relaxation techniques. So any sleep chapter should present nidra as a complement to CBT-I, not a rival. Read how Stephens handles that comparison in Ch. 3.

## Strengths and limitations
- Strengths: broad and well-organized clinical scaffolding (sleep stages, disorders, drugs, CBT-I, hygiene, lifespan, apnea); matching practice to the problem; an experienced sequencing author. Endorsers include Richard Miller, Bernie Clark, Loren Fishman MD and a former *Yoga Journal* editor, and the Seattle Book Review praised its friendly style and comprehensive practices (publisher page). The author-read audiobook, available through library loan, is an accessible way to hear the frame.
- Limitations / critiques: for a yoga nidra book it is thin, with nidra a minor component and no stated nidra lineage. I found no independent critical review, only publisher-curated blurbs. The quality of its evidence handling cannot be judged without reading the notes and bibliography. The marketing tone overreaches compared with the cautious line recommended in `06` and `08` §11: one endorsement (Levine) says the practices can cure insomnia.

## For your own book
- Take (from the ToC structure, not the text): the **architecture** of a sleep chapter: (1) a short two-process explanation of why you can't sleep; (2) hyperarousal as the target; (3) a sequence of calming breath, then supine release, then yoga nidra as the last step. Also take the idea of a pre-practice **self-assessment**.
- Take: place nidra **next to CBT-I**, not against it, and say so with the modest claim from `08` §11: "about as good as other relaxation". The `06` evidence above (Datta 2021, Dutta 2026, Singh 2026) does not support saying nidra matches or beats CBT-I. That also fits `08` §3: falling asleep is a legitimate goal when the purpose is sleep.
- Idea to test (**not from this book.** This is my own suggestion, prompted only by where the Italian ToC places nidra. I do not know how Stephens ends his practice, so don't attribute it to him): a short sleep-onset nidra, done in bed after a calming breath practice, that ends without a return to waking. Compare it with the waking-return ending of the Satyananda and Miller formats. Check Step 3 first: if Stephens already does this, cite him; if he doesn't, it is your own idea.
- Take: an explicit distinction between "nidra for sleep onset" (falling asleep is fine) and "nidra as self-inquiry" (stay awake), the two uses Miller's blurb runs together.
- Skip or treat critically: the 65% statistic (see above) and any claims on Alzheimer's or plaque clearance until you have checked the primary sources. Don't cite this book for how to teach nidra. Use Satyananda, Miller or Moore for that. If you borrow a copy through Libby, check Ch. 5 Step 3 for the stages used, any sankalpa, and any "1 hour = 4 hours" or delta-wave claims (`06` §6.3).
