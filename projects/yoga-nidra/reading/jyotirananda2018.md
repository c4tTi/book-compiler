## In one paragraph
A short, practical Spanish book by a Barcelona couple who trained at Satyananda's Bihar School in the 1990s and later built their own brand, "Yoga Nidra Sistema Darshan", shortened to "Nidra Darshan". They present it as an improved, "Western, pragmatic, eclectic and experiential" version of Satyananda's yoga nidra. It keeps the same basic structure (for the long version), but every instruction is rewritten in an "indirect language aimed at the subconscious". That language comes from neuro-linguistic programming (NLP) and, as the book's own glossary admits, from the tools of Ericksonian hypnosis. The sankalpa is redesigned as an NLP-built "personal affirmation", and the practice comes in three lengths on an enclosed CD. The second half of the title carries the main argument. The **whole practice is said to be mindfulness**: an "open" meditation (as opposed to a concentration meditation) done with five attitudes, in which nothing counts as a distraction. The book is for complete beginners and for people who already practise any body-mind or meditative method. It promises deep relaxation, stress control and self-knowledge. With practice, it also promises the ability to stay in the in-between state of sleep and waking, where you can "plant" affirmations in the subconscious to reach personal goals. The authors' website stresses that it is a practical book, not a teacher-training manual. The indirect-language technique itself "is not explained in the book" and is taught only in their paid courses.

## Core ideas
- **The whole practice is mindfulness, not a blend.** The book defines mindfulness as an "open" meditation focused on the present (pp. 21–22). It contrasts "concentration meditation", where you hold one or two objects and return to them, with "open meditation", where any sensation, thought or experience is allowed (pp. 15–16). Nidra Darshan is presented as a guided open meditation, and therefore as mindfulness "with all its verifiable benefits" (p. 17; ch. 6).
- **Nothing is a distraction.** Whatever arises is treated as part of the practice and used to go further into present experience. The authors call this the method's originality and the reason it is easier than other meditation (p. 18). Memories or thoughts about the future are met by noticing their felt effect *now* (pp. 17, 21).
- **Five practice attitudes,** the authors' own selection: love (for oneself, others and whatever is in consciousness), compassion and self-compassion, openness, curiosity ("a little like an explorer"), and acceptance. After several of them the book adds that accepting something does not mean you may not want to change it later (pp. 21–22, 25). They note that each mindfulness author lists different attitudes. The best-known list, Kabat-Zinn's, is different (non-judging, patience, beginner's mind, trust, non-striving, acceptance, letting go) 🔎 (from general knowledge, not checked for this summary).
- **Mindfulness as secularised Vipassana, with a commercial side.** The book calls mindfulness a Western adaptation of Buddhist Vipassana, built to be "aseptic" and scientific and perhaps also shaped by commercial interests, which they call "perfectly legitimate" (citing a *Time* cover). Its value for stress control stands anyway. The research on it is declared out of scope (p. 22).
- **Indirect language instead of commands.** Relaxation is never named or ordered ("never or almost never" in the book, p. 5; "never" on the website). It is meant to arise from the guidance itself. The glossary defines "indirect language" as language addressed to the subconscious, where much of the message lies in *how* things are said, and says it "uses the tools of Ericksonian hypnosis" (p. 16). The NLP-derived technique is applied in the book's scripts but deliberately not described (p. 18).
- **Never presuppose the outcome.** On their website the authors explain this as the core difference from "the original" and from most yoga nidra on offer. The guide never says "forget your worries", "make your mind blank", "relax the whole body" or "now you will see a light and calm down". The experience belongs to the practitioner, and the teacher only accompanies it. The sample's script fragments follow this: "however it is", "if you want", questions rather than predictions.
- **The sankalpa becomes an NLP "personal affirmation".** Ch. 10 promises a method to "install" goals and affirmations in the subconscious, combined with "a good action plan" (p. 12). The website describes the method: the affirmation comes out of a reflective process using the NLP "logical levels" (environment, behaviour, capabilities, beliefs and values, identity, spiritual/transpersonal), is kept **secret**, and is repeated in the "alpha state" that the practice induces. The website contrasts this with the "simple repetition of a phrase three times" in the original.
- **The in-between state as a window to the subconscious.** The publisher's description says that with practice you learn to stay between sleep and waking, contact your inner world and intuitions, and plant affirmations. Ch. 3 lists the uses: stress control (many illnesses are "considered psychosomatic"), personal goals, self-knowledge, concentration, progress in meditation of any kind, and mindfulness (pp. 11–12).
- **Pragmatism over tradition.** The authors describe their approach as Western: "practical, pragmatic, eclectic and experiential" (p. 10). The practice comes in several lengths. If it does not suit you, which they say is true for a fairly small percentage of people, they advise trying another meditation technique (p. 6). Chapter 13 states that the method is not psychotherapy.
- **An "improved evolution" of Satyananda's yoga nidra**, in the authors' "experiential opinion" (p. 16). The book calls the improvement "verifiable" (p. 17), and the website calls Satyananda the "main compiler" of yoga nidra. The three claimed improvements are NLP indirect language, adaptable lengths (2/15/35 minutes) and a better affirmation design (p. 18).

**Language and voice.** The book addresses the reader with informal *tú*. The fragments use short sentences, ellipses and repetition. They are **not purely invitational**: they still use attention verbs in the imperative ("feel", "notice", "simply notice"), but they soften them with "if you want" and "however it is", and they **ask rather than predict**. The website's list of banned phrases (predicting relaxation, emptying the mind, forgetting worries) is the most concrete style rule in the material. Compared with `07` §7.4, it sits between Satyananda's directive voice and the fully permission-giving style of Total Yoga Nidra. Note that the authors avoid *outcome* suggestions but openly use *indirect* ones (Ericksonian, p. 16).

**Mindfulness parallels drawn** (what the brief asked for): the practice as open monitoring rather than concentration; "no distractions"; present-moment noticing of past and future thoughts; the five attitudes, applied in the same way in nidra (p. 25); a teacher who presupposes nothing ("the experience depends entirely on the practitioner") [W]; and the body scan, extended to a "scan through consciousness" [W]. Concentration practices are called useful and compatible but different (p. 25).

**Sankalpa.** See stage 2. Further points: personal goals are tied to an action plan, not just to repetition (p. 12). The affirmation is secret. The website's "logical levels" checklist is a set of questions: where, when and with whom; what I do; how I do it; why; who I am; what for or for whom.

**Sleep.** The only direct guidance is a section title, "Estado anímico y sueño" (mood and sleep, p. 66), which I have not read 🔎. The aim stated in the description is to *stay* in the state between sleep and waking. I saw no rule against falling asleep, and no reassurance about it either.

**Scripts.** The sample has five fragments (pp. 7, 10, 13, 19, 23). The table of contents gives 2, 3 and 5 pages respectively to the 2-, 15- and 35-minute practices (pp. 73–82). Five large-type pages seem too few for a full 35-minute script, so these may be outlines or partial texts 🔎. The audio is on the CD.

## What's distinctive
- **The only book in this set that openly grounds its guiding language in NLP and Ericksonian hypnosis.** Satyananda and his successors insist the method is not hypnosis. These Satyananda-trained authors write in their glossary that their indirect language uses the tools of Ericksonian hypnosis (p. 16). That makes them a valuable witness for debate `08` §10 and `03` §3.12.
- **A clear example of yoga nidra re-explained in secular mindfulness vocabulary**, by teachers trained in the Bihar lineage, for a Spanish and Catalan market. The authors' website even tells booksellers to shelve the book under "meditation + mindfulness" or relaxation, because those sell better than "yoga", and complains that "physical yoga" is a contradiction in terms. This is direct evidence for the rebranding described in `08` §9 and `11`.
- **The sankalpa recast as NLP goal-setting** (logical levels, secrecy, an action plan). It is the most explicitly goal-directed sankalpa in the set, more so than Satyananda's and the opposite of Miller's "heartfelt desire" or Dinsmore-Tuli's optional sankalpa (`08` §8).
- **A three-length design down to 2 minutes**, explicitly so that teachers and therapists can add it to other activities. The training is marketed to therapists, Pilates and dance teachers and others who want to "expand their offer".
- **A rule against presupposing outcomes**, with lists of banned phrases (website) and a "nothing is a distraction" principle (book). It is a concrete, teachable script-writing rule.
- **One of very few Spanish-language yoga nidra books** in the project, alongside Sesma 2022 (`11` §11.4). It is the only one tied to a Catalan centre and teacher-training business.
- **What it does not offer:** history (beyond a short "Origin" chapter I have not read), research, trauma or safety guidance beyond "not psychotherapy" and a "Warning" section, or a full manual for teachers.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Short, plain and accessible. The definitions of concentration, meditation, relaxation, concentration versus open meditation, and "plot visualisation" (pp. 15–17) are clear enough to reuse as a model for your own glossary.
  - A strong practical principle: **don't presuppose the result; nothing is a distraction**. It removes beginners' sense of failure.
  - Candid in places. Some people won't benefit and should try something else (p. 6). The method is not psychotherapy (ch. 13). Mindfulness has commercial drivers (p. 22). The indirect language is Ericksonian hypnosis (p. 16).
  - Practical scope for a short book: room, light, music, seated and floor postures, recordings versus a live reader, three lengths on CD.
  - The image lists are gentle and multisensory, a safer default than dramatic images.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **Reader reception is split between strong praise and strong criticism.** On Amazon.es: 20 ratings, average 4.0/5 (64% five stars, 15% four stars, 0% two and three stars, 21% one star). The critical reviews are all marked "verified purchase". One reader calls the book "a kind of leaflet" praising the method, and says the CD meditations are worse than free ones online (Jan 2018). Another calls it a "plagiarism" of ordinary yoga nidra, complains of large print, and says the audio doesn't relax (June 2018). A third calls it "like an advertising leaflet" that keeps praising "their" Darshan system, with very thin yoga nidra content (Feb 2020). Positive reviews praise the CD, the NLP affirmation method and its suitability for beginners; two of the earliest five-star reviews (Jan–Feb 2018) are not marked as verified purchases. Goodreads has one 2-star rating and no text reviews. **I found no press, journal or long-form blog review** (see Confidence on search limits).
  - **The key ingredient is withheld.** The book applies the NLP indirect-language technique but does not explain it (p. 18). The website confirms that it is taught only in the paid instructor course (40 h, about €390–500). As a source for *how* to write such language, the book gives you examples, not the method.
  - **Claims of superiority without evidence.** The method is called a "verifiably" improved yoga nidra (p. 17), easier and more effective than other systems (ch. 11 title) and "one of the most effective" relaxation techniques (website). No comparative data are given; research is declared out of scope (p. 22). Compendium `06` §6.0 finds yoga nidra *as a whole* not clearly better than other active relaxation methods. There is no study of this variant.
  - **NLP and "alpha state" lore.** The mechanism offered combines "installing" information in a subconscious made receptive in the "alpha state" with NLP techniques. A review of 63 NLP studies (Witkowski 2010, *Polish Psychological Bulletin* 41(2), abstract) found mostly non-supportive results and concluded that they "contradict the claim of an empirical basis of NLP". The "alpha opens the subconscious" idea is self-help lore. The EEG literature on yoga nidra reports more alpha and theta but no such mechanism (`06` §6.1, `03` §3.2).
  - **The mindfulness label is stretched.** A fully guided script with chosen images and a goal-directed affirmation is closer to guided imagery and suggestion than to open monitoring. Mainstream mindfulness programmes emphasise non-striving, while this book is built around achieving personal goals. The authors do not address this tension in the pages I read.
  - **The "never say relax" principle is not new.** Janakananda, Satyananda's disciple since 1968, already writes that "you are not asked to relax in yoga nidra" (see `reading/janakananda2021.md`). It is probably older lineage teaching, not a Darshan innovation as the website presents it. Check Satyananda 1976's own scripts to see how often "relax" actually appears there 🔎.
  - **Lineage handled reverently and without context.** Satyananda and Niranjananda appear among their teachers in the acknowledgements, and the website stresses they were "direct disciples" who met Satyananda personally. Neither the sample nor the website mentions the 2014–16 Royal Commission findings (`08` §6). Whether the book's "Origin" chapter does is unknown 🔎.
  - **Small inconsistencies.** The book says the Darshan centre was founded in 2008 (p. 17); the website says 2004. The book says "never or almost never" about the word "relax"; the website says "never". The structure is called "identical" (2018) and later "almost identical" (2022) to Bihar's.
  - **Thin and dated format.** 104 large-type pages, a CD in 2017, no e-book and no online audio that I could find.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The "no presupposition" rule as a script-writing checklist.** Their banned phrases (predicting relaxation, emptying the mind, forgetting worries, announcing what the listener will see or feel) give you a concrete test for your own scripts, and they fit the invitational side of `07` §7.4. Write your own examples. Their "however it is" and "if you want" are common phrasings, but avoid copying their sentences.
  - **"Nothing is a distraction."** One of the best one-line framings for beginners who think they are "doing it wrong". It pairs well with Miller's "welcoming" and helps with the falling-asleep guilt in `08` §3.
  - **Questions instead of commands** ("how are you breathing now?") as a deliberate option at selected stages. Contrast it in your book with Satyananda's brisk naming, and explain when each works: questions for arrival and breath, brisk naming for the rotation.
  - **The concentration-versus-open-meditation distinction** (pp. 15–17). It gives you a clean way to explain how yoga nidra relates to mindfulness. Yoga nidra arguably *moves between* the two: concentration in the rotation, open noticing in the breath and *chidākāśa* stages. That is a more defensible position than "it is all mindfulness".
  - **Gentle, multisensory image lists, each image said twice.** A low-risk alternative to dramatic rapid images, consistent with the lineage's own caution about frightening images (see `satyananda1976.md`).
  - **A tiered-length design (2 / 15 / 35 minutes)** with a "skeleton" that survives in the short versions. Combine it with Satyananda's rule of changing length by adding or removing whole stages.
  - **The authors' own admission of Ericksonian roots**, as a source for an honest "is it hypnosis?" section (`03` §3.12, `08` §10). Here Satyananda-trained teachers say openly what the lineage denies.
  - **The rebranding story** (shelving advice, "physical yoga" polemic, training sold to therapists to "expand their offer") as a small case study for your culture and market chapter (`08` §9, `11`).
  - **Reflective prompts before the sankalpa.** A sequence of questions from surroundings and behaviour up to values, identity and meaning is a useful journaling exercise *before* practice. Present it as reflection, not as a way to "program" the subconscious.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **"Installing" goals in the subconscious, the "alpha state" as an open door, and NLP as the mechanism.** None of this is supported (Witkowski 2010; `06` §6.3, "reprograms the subconscious" is a metaphor). If you mention it, mark it as a claim.
  - **"Verifiably more effective / easier / the most effective."** Don't echo superiority claims for any branded variant, including your own. The honest benchmark is in `06` §6.0.
  - **"The whole practice is mindfulness."** Don't adopt it uncritically: a goal-directed affirmation and guided images sit uneasily with non-striving. If you combine yoga nidra and mindfulness, explain where they meet and where they differ.
  - **Indirect suggestion without disclosure.** If you use indirect or Ericksonian phrasing, tell readers what you are doing and why. Covert persuasion techniques raise the teacher-power issues in `07` §7.7, especially for a practice done in a receptive state.
  - **Brand-building and withholding the method.** "Sistema Darshan" holds back its central technique for a paid course. If your book teaches a method, teach it in full. Readers notice when they don't get it, as the Amazon reviews show.
  - **"Never say relax" as an innovation.** Present it as a long-standing lineage principle (Janakananda) with a reason (attention moves, relaxation follows), not as anyone's invention.
  - **Psychosomatic generalisations** ("many illnesses are considered psychosomatic", p. 11). Too broad to repeat without sources.
  - **Lineage claims without context.** If you cite these authors as Satyananda-trained, handle the lineage history as you decide in `08` §6.
