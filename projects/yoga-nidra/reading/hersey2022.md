## In one paragraph
*Rest Is Resistance* is the manifesto of The Nap Ministry, by the author her US publisher calls the "originator" of the movement to understand the liberatory power of rest. In lyrical, sermon-like prose, Hersey argues that **grind culture**, her name for white supremacy and capitalism working together, treats human bodies as machines. Resting, napping, daydreaming and slowing down therefore become acts of refusal, reparation and spiritual reconnection. The roots are Black American history (plantation labour, Jim Crow, the Great Migration) and her own family's "legacy of exhaustion", with her grandmother Ora as muse. It is written for the tired who still hope, explicitly for everyone, but centred on Black experience. It offers practices (per the publisher and reviewers) but, in the pages I sampled, no structured method, and it promises no health outcome. It offers a change of consciousness, which she calls "deprogramming", and an invitation to collective rest.

**How much concerns yoga nidra: none explicitly, as far as I could check.** Neither "yoga" nor "yoga nidra" appears in the sample I read. A search of Hersey's blog posts finds "yoga" once (yoga mats at a 2018 performance) and "nidra" never. Her About page mentions yoga only as yoga mats and as yoga studios used as venues. None of the reviews mentions yoga. I could not search Parts One to Four, so a passing mention cannot be ruled out. What is relevant is the **case for rest** (most of the book) and a handful of guided, imagery-based rest moments.

## Structure
The ebook's contents list, as shown in the publisher's sample reader: Preface, Introduction (which sets out the four tenets), **Part One: REST!**, **Part Two: DREAM!**, **Part Three: RESIST!**, **Part Four: IMAGINE!**, then back matter including Notes and "The Nap Ministry Library". *South Side Weekly* reports one tenet per part.

The navigation shows no chapter titles inside the four parts. The Introduction opens with a one-paragraph blessing wishing the reader rest. Its main text, as far as the sample runs, does three things:
- it states the **four tenets of The Nap Ministry**;
- it explains that they came to her in dreams;
- it discusses Tenet 1 in full and begins Tenet 2.

*South Side Weekly* reports that each of the four parts focuses on one of the four tenets. I did not see the parts, so this mapping is the reviewer's, not mine.

## Core ideas
- **Grind culture.** White supremacy and capitalism together reduce people to machines and tie worth to output. The counter-claim, repeated like a mantra: you are enough now. Hersey insists we do not only suffer grind culture, we enact it in daily behaviour, which is why change takes "deprogramming".
- **The four tenets** (paraphrased):
  1. rest resists capitalism and white supremacy because it disrupts them;
  2. the body is a site of liberation;
  3. naps are a portal to imagine, invent and heal;
  4. the **DreamSpace** (her coinage) has been stolen and will be reclaimed through rest.
- **Rest is wider than sleep:** naps, daydreaming, slowing down, meditation, prayer, even intense laughter (*BookPage*). One blog post is titled "Rest is anything that connects your mind and body" (2022).
- **History as motive.** Rest honours ancestors whose labour and sleep were stolen. The sample states without argument that capitalism began on plantations; the full book has Notes I did not see. In the sample she calls sleep deprivation a public-health and spiritual issue, and ties the deepest exhaustion to descendants of plantation labour. Her blog goes further and calls rest a racial justice issue (Jan 2021) and a social justice and public health issue (Feb 2022).
- **Guilt, shame and grief are part of the practice.** Guilt and shame when resting are evidence of conditioning. Grieving that conditioning is a first step, and rest "supports our grieving by allowing space".
- **A lifelong unravelling, not a hack.** Rest is slow, embodied and ancient work, never a trend or a quick fix. Readers should rest a little every day before intellectualising the idea.
- **Collective, not individual.** Rest is a matter of community care: no one leaves grind culture alone. She rejects commodified self-care that is packaged and sold, such as candles, shakes and crystals (*BookPage*, which also quotes her phrase "shallow wellness work"). The Nap Ministry says it has "never identified" itself as part of the wellness industry (blog, Jan 2021).
- **The honest objection.** She calls the question "How can I rest, if I have to pay bills?" the heart of the work. Her answer is to refuse the work-or-rest binary and to stay adaptable and subversive, not to offer financial solutions.
- **Testimony as authority.** The Preface says she needs no outside verification or "complicated theories" for what rest did for her. The argument rests on personal and family stories and on spiritual knowing (dreams, Ancestors, Spirit).

## The practice as this book teaches it
This is not a yoga nidra method. **In the sample I read** there are no stages, no body rotation, no breath technique beyond a single line telling the reader to breathe deeply, and no sankalpa ritual. Parts One to Four were not seen; reviewers report journaling questions, meditations and practical methods there (*South Side Weekly*, *BookPage*), and the UK publisher says the book explains her "methods". What the sample offers:
- **Short guided imagery passages.** Early in the Introduction a few lines lead the reader through a mini-visualisation, close to a script fragment. In my paraphrase: imagine a world without oppression; linger and picture softness; breathe deeply; picture a world centred on justice; remain here; you have arrived in your DreamSpace.

  The language is short, imperative-but-tender lines with heavy repetition, closing on a collective blessing that we rest together. The content is social and political imagery, not the body.
- **Affirmation in place of a resolve.** She asks readers to keep telling themselves that they are enough now. This works much like a sankalpa. Its aim is to undo the urge to achieve, not to achieve something.
- **Ora's "resting her eyes".** The founding image is her grandmother sitting upright on the couch with closed eyes every day. The Preface gives the length both as thirty minutes and as thirty minutes to an hour, and Hersey also calls it meditating. The child Hersey thought Ora was asleep; Ora said it was not sleep but resting and listening for God. This is close to the yoga nidra idea of rest that is not sleep (see quote 1).
- **Daily minimum, lifelong arc.** Rest for at least a few minutes each day (quote 2). Expect years of unlearning.
- **Practical elements reported in the later parts** (not seen by me): journaling questions and meditations to use while adding naps and quiet time (*South Side Weekly*), and boundary-setting imperatives (*South Side Weekly*). Practices include prayer, daydreaming, sleep and laughter (*BookPage*).
- **Hersey's live format** (not in the sample, but the model behind the book): the **Collective Napping Experience**. Mats, blankets and pillows, a curated soundtrack, a rest altar, "poetic meditations" and a post-nap talk, all guided by Hersey (Nap Ministry *About* page). Sessions run about 30–40 minutes, open with a meditation and end with group discussion (Wikipedia, citing *Bon Appétit* 2019 and the *Detroit Free Press* 2019).

## What's distinctive
- **The only book on this list that makes rest a political and theological claim** grounded in Black American history: slavery, Jim Crow, reparations, womanism. Among the yoga nidra books:
  - Stanley (2021) speaks of ancestral and collective reclamation of rest, inside a tantric yoga nidra frame. Our summary of *Radiant Rest* notes that she does not cite Hersey.
  - Brody (2017) frames rest as feminist empowerment, five years earlier.
  - Dinsmore-Tuli & Tuli (2020) call rest a radical social act and cite The Nap Ministry (p. 49).

  *Rest Is Resistance* is the source those framings point to (or parallel). It is not a yoga text.
- **The DreamSpace** concept: dreaming and daydreaming as a stolen inheritance to be reclaimed. This gives the hypnagogic, dreamy edge of yoga nidra a social meaning that no yoga nidra book offers.
- **Rest as communal ritual** (collective naps with altar and post-rest talk), not private self-improvement.
- **An insider critique of the wellness market.** It meets the religious-studies critique of yoga as a consumer product (`08` §9, Jain 2014) from the opposite direction.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - The clearest, most quotable statement of the social and spiritual case for rest.
  - Moving family testimony: grandmother Ora, a great-grandmother keeping watch against the Klan, and a father who died at 55 after working two full-time jobs (*PW*).
  - Takes guilt, grief and economic fear seriously instead of promising quick relaxation.
  - Beautiful, incantatory prose (*BookPage*: "exquisitely beautiful", to be read "with a pen in hand").
  - Hugely influential: an instant *NYT* bestseller (author's blog). On Goodreads it averages 4.03 from about 13,500 ratings (2 Oct 2026).
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **Repetitive.** *PW* says the author "sometimes struggles to find new ways to expand on her central point". The sample bears this out: a four-sentence passage of the Preface recurs almost word for word a few paragraphs later.
  - **Anti-evidential by design.** The Preface rejects the need for outside verification or theory. Health and history claims (sleep deprivation and race; capitalism's origin on plantations) appear in the sample without argument. Check them in the Notes and in the historical and epidemiological literature before repeating them.
  - **Not a manual.** There are few concrete methods, and the economic dilemma is named rather than solved.
  - **US-specific frame.** Its force comes from Black American history. Transposing it to other audiences needs care and attribution.
  - **Critiques of the movement, not the book.** Trey Washington's essay "Rest is Not Resistance, and That is OK" (*Scalawag*, 28 Mar 2024) argues that framing rest as a choice puts the burden on individuals, that the choice to rest is a luxury few can afford, and that rest is better seen as a tool and an outcome of resistance. The essay does not name Hersey or the book; it answers the slogan. I found no published review that raises corporate co-option of the slogan, though Hersey's own blog complains of uses that strip out its Black-liberation history (see below).
  - I found no long critical review of the book itself. Kirkus and the *NYT* were inaccessible.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **Cite Hersey by name for the social and political case for rest.** Credit "rest is resistance" and "rest as reparations" to her and The Nap Ministry. She dates the "Rest is Resistance" framework to 2016. This is the citation `02` already recommends.
  - **The guilt-and-deprogramming insight** belongs in your chapter on why students cannot rest: restlessness or guilt in *śavāsana* is conditioning, not failure. It fits your "is falling asleep failure?" discussion (`08` §3) and a rest-oriented rather than goal-oriented stance on sankalpa (`08` §8).
  - **Ora's "resting her eyes"** is a powerful, non-Indian image of awake, listening rest. Use it, attributed, to show that rest that is not sleep has many cultural homes.
  - **The communal format**: shared props, an altar, a soundtrack, a short talk afterwards. It is a model for group yoga nidra sessions and for integration after practice.
  - **Script style.** The short lines, collective "we" and repeated invitation to remain in rest are a useful reference for invitational voice (`08` §5). Notice how imagery of a just world can replace a personal resolve.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Don't use the slogan as marketing.** Hersey says The Nap Ministry was never part of the wellness industry. She calls anyone who promotes rest-as-resistance without its Black-liberation history an "agent of grind culture" (blog, Feb 2022). If you invoke it, give the full context, or don't invoke it.
  - **Don't cite it for health or physiological claims.** It is testimony and theology. For outcomes, use `06` and keep to modest claims (`08` §11).
  - **Don't present Hersey as a yoga nidra teacher**, or the tenets as a yoga nidra framework. Don't claim she shaped Stanley or Brody without evidence: Brody's book came first, and *Radiant Rest* does not cite her.
  - **Check contested history** (capitalism's plantation origins) before repeating it.
  - **Race and sleep.** `06` has no entry on racial sleep disparities. If you repeat the claim, cite primary sleep-epidemiology sources (for example a review of racial and ethnic sleep disparities) rather than Hersey. This is a gap in the compendium for `06`.
  - **Positionality.** If you are not writing from Black American experience, quote and credit rather than adopt her voice. The same care applies to yoga nidra's Indian sources (`08` §9).
