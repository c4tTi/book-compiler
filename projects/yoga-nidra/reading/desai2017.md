## In one paragraph
This is the textbook of the Amrit school of yoga nidra (later branded I AM). It argues three things. First, yoga nidra is a practice of **non-doing**: you consciously ride the brain's natural descent toward sleep, and change happens "beyond the mind" instead of through effort. Second, the root of most suffering is **identification**, meaning we mistake the contents of awareness (body, thoughts, stored samskaras) for the self. Third, a guided descent through the five **koshas** and the brainwave bands (beta → alpha → theta → delta → turiya) quiets those contents until the "container" of awareness becomes obvious. On the way back up, a planted **Intention** (sankalpa) rewrites the stored pattern at its "seed" level. The book is built around **Six Tools**: Realization, Integration, Dis-identification, Intention, Relaxation and Restoration (PDF pp. 14–15). Section Two applies them to stress, health, depression and anxiety, insomnia, addiction and trauma, backed by a large literature review. Most of that literature is general meditation, MBSR or TM research, and the author says so herself. Section Three gives practical setup advice and the one script. The promise is large: better sleep and health, freedom from habits, and ultimately Self-Realization.

## Core ideas
1. **Non-doing.** Yoga nidra techniques are active methods whose job is to tip you into a state where nothing is being done. Desai compares relaxation to floating: it happens only when you stop struggling (PDF pp. 11–12, 330). Change made from this state is said to originate beyond the mind, not through it (PDF p. 12).
2. **Container and contents.** The Self is the unchanging witness and background, like a sky, a pot or a cinema screen. Suffering comes from identifying with whatever passes through it (Ch. 2, PDF pp. 24–32). The kleshas describe how this forgetting happens: avidya, asmita, raga/dvesha, abhinivesha (Ch. 3). Yoga nidra "solves" the problem experientially: when the brainwaves slow, the contents fall quiet and the container is noticed. She calls each session a small taste of enlightenment (PDF p. 32) and a rehearsal for death that can help in hospice (PDF pp. 93–94, 239).
3. **Brainwave map.** Beta (13–100 Hz, gamma 40–70 as a subset) = waking; alpha (8–12) = relaxed waking into dreaming, with serotonin, immunity and first access to the subconscious, which she places in the Prana body (PDF pp. 50–51); theta (4–7) = deep sleep, unconscious patterns, emotional integration; delta (0.5–3.9) = most restorative, with growth hormone release, cortisol reduction and possible out-of-body experiences (PDF pp. 56–59). Beyond these lies **turiya**, the ultimate purpose of the practice (PDF pp. 21, 60). She says most people reach this gap of nothingness within their first few sessions, unlike seated meditators (PDF p. 56). For awake delta she cites Swami Rama, via a 1983 *Yoga Journal* article (PDF pp. 57–58).
4. **Koshas, from ice to ether.** Bliss = ether, Wisdom = air, Mental = steam, Energy = water, Physical = ice. Ice needs hammer and chisel; water takes any shape it is given, and Intention uses this principle (PDF p. 66; the same image appears at PDF p. 20). The Bliss (Causal) body holds *chitta*, the "hard drive" of samskaras, including imprints from past lives (PDF p. 68). The Wisdom body is clouded by fears and attachments (*vasanas*) and is strengthened by witnessing (PDF pp. 69–71). The Mental body is quieted by closing the eyes (PDF p. 71). The Energy body is what makes the Amrit method distinctive (Quote 1, PDF p. 74).
5. **Prana-first method.** Mind and prana compete for the same fuel. Quiet the mind and freed prana grows from a biological maintenance force into a healing and then "evolutionary" force that returns to Source (PDF pp. 64–65, 76). Her image is the lost boy who drops the reins so the horse can find the way home (PDF p. 77). Signs of moving energy include tingling, shaking, jerking, lights and visions. She insists that the benefit does not depend on having them and warns against making experiences the goal (PDF pp. 77–78).
6. **Integration (detox).** Emotions are energy moving through a "tube". Resistance blocks them, and in deep states the blocks release without your needing to know their story (PDF pp. 81–83). After-effects such as moodiness, headache, nausea or "Shakti flu" (flu-like symptoms within about 24 h) are framed as clearing, a "healing crisis" (PDF pp. 83–85). Her colleague Eric Walrabenstein supplies the shower metaphor: the shower reveals dirt, it does not cause it (PDF p. 85).
7. **Karma and samskaras.** Karma is not what happens to you but how you meet it: external event + internal reaction = outcome (PDF pp. 99–100). Samskaras are grooves deepened by repetition. Most are useful, and she gives "99.9%" (PDF p. 102). An unhelpful samskara distorts every kosha, from perception down to the body (PDF pp. 105–108). The aim is to work at the root rather than the leaves (PDF p. 109).
8. **Dis-identification and "burning" karma.** Neither feed an urge nor fight it. Withdraw attention (pratyahara), live through the burning discomfort, and the groove heals over. Her worked example is ice cream (PDF pp. 114–116). For entrenched addictions, total abstinence may be needed (PDF p. 117). The sunglasses demonstration, also from Walrabenstein, shows how adopted beliefs filter what we see (PDF pp. 118–120). She presents yoga nidra as an adjunct to therapy because you do not need to know what the "glasses" are (PDF p. 121).
9. **Intention as the method's practical core** (Chs. 10–11):
   - **Goal vs. intention.** An intention is a direction without attachment to an outcome, so detours are not failures (PDF pp. 126–127).
   - **Why plant it in yoga nidra.** Waking affirmations only layer a new thought over an old samskara. In yoga nidra the Intention is placed **after** resting as awareness, in "neutral gear", where no competing voices are active (PDF pp. 128–129).
   - **Is an Intention necessary?** No. Spirit does the work anyway, and a specific Intention is a tool for patterns you can name (PDF p. 134).
   - **Primary vs. secondary.** A primary Intention states who you are, either as formless Source or as its embodied expression (PDF p. 135). Secondary Intentions clear the path and target **attitudes, habits and external stressors**, each with sample pairs of pattern and intention (PDF pp. 135–137). Primary Intentions change less often than secondary ones (PDF p. 164). Her own first primary Intention was to be the fullest expression of herself; it later became standing in the power of Divine Presence (PDF p. 164).
   - **Five qualities:** it resonates, is in the present, is positive, is concise, and is about you (PDF pp. 137–141). Two of her glosses are unusual. "Positive" means it **feels** positive: wording that names sadness or struggle is acceptable, although "not" should be avoided (PDF p. 139). For daily use she shortens an Intention to one word or an image (PDF p. 140).
   - **When to use it in a session:** if the recording does not prompt it, bring it to mind just after settling with eyes closed, again at the deepest point, and again at the end while lying on your side (PDF pp. 141–142).
   - **The school's core Intention:** to be at peace with oneself as one is and with the world as it is (PDF p. 93; it is also the last of the script's general affirmations, PDF p. 341).
10. **Intention in daily life.** Ch. 11 is a protocol in four steps. Observe the pattern (*swadhyaya*; Portia Nelson's "hole in the sidewalk" poem, PDF pp. 149–151). Make space before reacting with a stock delaying phrase (PDF p. 152). Choose the new "seed" using *pratipaksha bhavana*, *satya* and the body's felt "click" (PDF pp. 153–154). Rehearse the new behaviour inside yoga nidra and then reinforce it while awake (PDF pp. 156–157). A long case story about her friend Sally carries the chapter (PDF pp. 147–158).
11. **Yoga nidra vs. hypnosis.** Both work on the dense levels through the subtle ones. Hypnosis optimises the body/mind, while yoga nidra aims to transcend it. Physiologically, she says, hypnosis suspends left anterior executive function (citing Gruzelier 1998), whereas yoga nidra makes both hemispheres work as one, which she says PET scans show; she gives **no citation** for this (PDF pp. 129–130).
12. **Third eye.** The eyebrow centre is linked to the hypothalamus, pituitary and pineal glands. Melatonin is "likely" the yogis' amrita (PDF p. 131). Long passages quoted from Amrit Desai's 2015 Immersion Manual present the third eye as the gateway through which Intentions are actualised (PDF pp. 131–134).
13. **Ida/pingala model of mood.** Too much pingala (control, doing) leads to anxiety and burnout. Too much ida (collapse) leads to depression. Balance is the middle path, and equal flow through both nostrils after practice is offered as its sign (PDF pp. 175, 241–256). She offers this explicitly as her own suggestion (PDF p. 243).
14. **Habit ladder.** Tendency ("nice to"), habit ("want to"), compulsion ("need to"), addiction ("have to"); each step moves further from conscious choice (PDF pp. 279–286). Habits are framed as ways of managing how we feel (PDF pp. 272–273).
15. **Trauma as undischarged energy.** She draws on Peter Levine: trauma is an incomplete stress response held as an energy knot (*granthi*). Yoga nidra discharges it a little at a time, without revisiting the story (PDF pp. 297–300, 306).

## The practice as this book teaches it
**The model (Ch. 7, PDF pp. 86–97; Figure 8).** A session follows the koshas **inward and then back out**. Each technique has a "first half", deliberate and willed, and a **"second half"** in which you stop and absorb its energetic effect. She calls this pause essential (PDF pp. 65, 87). The stages:
- **Physical body: from thinking to feeling.** Asana or deliberate tension and release, then techniques that make energy easy to feel, such as Bumble Bee Breath or palming the face (PDF pp. 86–87).
- **Energy body: speeding up the flow.** Pranayama; each breath technique is said to have its own therapeutic effect. She claims sheetali balances blood sugar and the three-part yogic breath is the strongest relaxation breath (PDF p. 87).
- **Energy body: steering energy with attention.** "61 Points" through marma points and meridians; contrasts such as heavy/light and cold/heat, said to give control over the involuntary nervous system (PDF pp. 88–89). Two anecdotes about Amrit Desai support this. Researchers at the Institute of Noetic Sciences timed him reaching deep meditation in a minute or less; on *The Mike Douglas Show* he raised his heart rate to about 180 and produced different blood pressures in each arm (PDF pp. 88–89). Neither is referenced.
- **Mental body:** internalisation, concentration (61 Points, breath, scanning) and healing visualisation (PDF pp. 89–90).
- **Wisdom body: fostering the witness.** Pairs of opposite words (pleasure/pain) received with equal openness, and **rapid images** watched without engaging (PDF pp. 90–91).
- **Bliss body and beyond: resting in awareness.** Deepest theta/delta, the thinnest veil, then turiya. Some first-timers feel as if they had died (PDF pp. 91–94).
- **Return path:** at the **Bliss body, plant the Intention** (PDF pp. 94–96). At the **Wisdom body, invoke Presence**: masters, guides and deceased family members may be felt (PDF p. 96). Through the **Mental, Energy and Physical bodies, externalise** (PDF pp. 96–97). Allow about 15 minutes before being fully functional again (PDF p. 97).

In Ch. 14 she describes the same arc in mindfulness terms: muscle contraction, then breath, then "body rotations and visualizations", then resting as awareness (PDF p. 219). This is the only mention of body rotation in the book.

**Logistics (Ch. 20, PDF pp. 324–332):**
- **When:** on waking; at bedtime (it drops you into alpha and then into sleep); and in the late afternoon slump, when she says it balances blood sugar (PDF pp. 324–325). In Ch. 17 she adds that some people find bedtime practice energising and should practise earlier in the day (PDF p. 267).
- **Guided or self-led:** it is best guided, by a person or a CD, because guiding yourself is a form of doing. 61 Points and breathing techniques can be done alone (PDF p. 325). Even advanced practitioners should keep a guided practice for the therapeutic value of the techniques (PDF p. 326).
- **Length:** beginners start at about 20 min; sessions usually run up to 45 min; 12–15 min versions fit the end of a yoga class; aim for at least 20 (PDF pp. 326–327). If you cannot settle, do yoga poses or stretches first (PDF p. 326). She distinguishes **"surfing"**, bobbing in and out of hearing the voice, from a true sense of being complete (PDF pp. 326–327).
- **Posture and props:**
  - Lying on the back is ideal. Use plenty of height under the knees and only enough under the head to keep the chin parallel to the floor; a thick pillow is said to tell the brain to stay awake (PDF pp. 325, 327).
  - Pad sensitive spots. Cover with a blanket, because body temperature drops. Nothing should touch the body, not even a foot against the wall (PDF pp. 327–328).
  - In **pregnancy** after three months, or at the first discomfort, lie on the left side. Otherwise she recommends right-side lying, which she says activates ida (PDF p. 327).
  - Sitting against a wall or in a chair is fine (PDF p. 328).
  - For snoring or drowsiness, raise one hand (then both) or prop up the upper body. If you have **low blood pressure or feel dizzy** afterwards, elevate the upper body (PDF pp. 328–329). She mentions squeezing a snoring student's toe (PDF p. 328).
- **Sleep:** falling asleep at first is natural. If you are exhausted, let it happen. When ready to train wakefulness, repeat a resolve to stay awake three times beforehand (PDF p. 329). If you come back when called, you were not asleep, and the body is said to register the words anyway (PDF p. 329).
- **Stillness, listening, disturbances:** move slowly if you must (PDF pp. 329–330). Listening is meant to happen through the body, not the thinking mind. For hearing loss, sit near the guide, face them and keep the eyes softly open to lip-read (PDF p. 330). Use disturbances, outer or inner, to go deeper (PDF pp. 331–332).
- **Amrit vs. "concentration" styles:** concentration is a stepping stone to be surrendered. When the mind returns, simply pick up the guidance wherever it has reached (PDF pp. 330–331).
- **Notes for guides:** drop into a meditative state yourself, feel the words, and keep the marked pauses. She recommends further training before guiding others (PDF p. 332).

What this means for comparisons:
- **No body rotation and no 61 Points appear in the printed script**, although Ch. 7 and Ch. 20 name 61 Points as a core technique. **There is no counted breath.**
- **No Intention is set at the start of the script.** It is repeated three times at the deepest point and recalled once more on the right side at the end. Ch. 10 separately advises a third placement at the start if a recording does not prompt one (PDF pp. 141–142).
- **The script has no closing affirmation in the Satyananda style ("I am practising…").**
- **Pacing is given only as relative pauses, never in minutes.** The only fixed counts are: up to 7 bumblebee breaths, 8–10 breaths of silence at the deepest point, and no more than 3 affirmations.
- **Register.** The voice is directive (imperatives throughout) and devotional (higher power, grace, Presence, Spirit, guides). It promises results: the text says Intentions planted here come true without effort, and that something deep has shifted whatever you noticed. This is far from the invitational, choice-giving style of her 2026 facilitation blog post [author, 2026; earlier pass].

**Cautions and safety in the book, gathered in one place:**
- tension technique: cardiac disease, hypertension, glaucoma, pressure in the head (PDF p. 333);
- dizziness or low blood pressure afterwards: elevate the upper body (PDF p. 328);
- pregnancy: left-side lying (PDF p. 327);
- possible after-effects (emotions, headache, nausea, "Shakti flu") and permission to come out at any time by squeezing the hands, feeling the soles of the feet, or opening the eyes softly (PDF pp. 83–84);
- for trauma: practise with eyes open and/or seated against a wall; watch memories "as a movie" from the watcher's seat; ground through the soles of the feet or by touching thumb to index finger; repeat a safety Intention; anchor in the breath and the present (Ch. 19, "Recommendations for Practice", PDF p. 319);
- spend extra time on breath work with trauma (PDF p. 307);
- yoga nidra is an adjunct, not a replacement for treatment, medication or psychotherapy (PDF pp. 81, 284, 293); oddly, she also says that for habits, depending on severity, it can be used on its own (PDF p. 287).

**What the book never addresses:** screening or contraindications for psychosis, dissociative disorders or epilepsy. Nothing cautions against the optional out-of-body stage for dissociation-prone or traumatised students, even though Ch. 19 describes dissociation and out-of-body feelings as part of the freeze response (PDF p. 301). Nothing discusses consent before touching a student.

## Claims about effects, and how well they are supported
Desai does say plainly that **most of the benefits she lists come from research on meditation in general**, and she treats yoga nidra as one kind of meditation (Quote 2, PDF p. 196). Even so, the condition chapters repeatedly say that yoga nidra releases a chemical or thickens a brain region when the cited study was of something else. Three groups of evidence:

**(a) Studies she presents as specific to yoga nidra** (Ch. 13, PDF pp. 202–206; Ch. 14, PDF pp. 210–212; Ch. 18, PDF p. 288; Ch. 19, PDF pp. 312–313):
- **Kjaer et al. 2002** (dopamine PET, raclopride). Reported as a dopamine increase of up to 65% (PDF pp. 202, 291) and used to suggest yoga nidra as an aid for Parkinson's disease, for withdrawal and for ADD/ADHD (PDF pp. 202, 292). Compendium `06` §6.3: one n = 8 study, with a speech-listening control that felt just as relaxing.
- **Lou et al. 1999** (PET): she gives theta +11% and alpha −2%, plus symmetric bilateral activation, partly via the Scandinavian school's own article (Nilsson) (PDF pp. 210–212). **Parker et al. 2013** supplies her definition (PDF p. 210). **Mangalteertham**, cited from Satyananda's book, supplies the beta → alpha → delta claim (PDF p. 210).

- **Indian studies:** Kumar 2004, 2005 and 2006; Pandya & Kumar 2007; Srivastava 2012 (menstrual disorders); Amita 2009 (blood glucose); Praktikui 2006 (guilt) (PDF pp. 203–205).
- **Older relaxation studies:** Cooper & Aygen 1979 (cholesterol) and Lekh Raj Bali 1979 (hypertension). Both are presented as yoga nidra, but the cited titles say only "a relaxation technique" and "relaxation"; I did not check what was taught (PDF p. 203).
- **Amrit-method data:** Nicola Vague's four-week Melbourne case study (n = 9, self-rated percentages, no control; a "working paper") and Desai's own case study at a cancer centre (n = 7, cited as "raw data"). She reports an average drop in resting heart rate of 8.5 beats per minute and says more study is needed (PDF pp. 203–204).
- **Military endorsement claims:** an Army Surgeon General endorsement for chronic pain (2010) and about 33 VA and military sites, both sourced from press articles (PDF pp. 205, 312).

- **Brain structure:** Lazar 2005 and Hölzel 2011. The Hölzel MBSR hippocampus figure is reproduced (PDF p. 215). In Ch. 16 its eight-week MBSR finding is restated as the result of eight weeks of yoga nidra (PDF p. 247).

- **Pain and cognition:** Zeidan 2010 and 2011, Levy 2011, Mrazek 2013 (PDF pp. 222–223, 266).
- **Specific symptoms:** Carmody 2006 (hot flashes) and Keefer & Blanchard 2001 (IBS) (PDF p. 223); Lush 2009 and Sephton 2007 (fibromyalgia) (PDF p. 224).
- **Reviews and comparisons:** the Arias 2006 review (PDF p. 256). Öst & Breitholtz 2000 (applied relaxation vs. cognitive therapy for GAD) leads her to suggest that relaxation methods like yoga nidra may beat psychotherapy in the long term (PDF p. 257).
- **TM literature:** Glaser 1992 (DHEA), Jevning 1978 and MacLean 1997 (growth hormone), Walton (cortisol), Elias 2000 (GABA), the Alexander 1994 meta-analysis (addiction), Rosenthal 2011 (5 veterans) (PDF pp. 198–200, 294, 313).

**Mismatches I noticed between claims and the sources cited:**
- Lagopoulos 2009, an acute EEG study of meditation, is cited for theta and delta declining with age (PDF p. 56, note 10).

## What's distinctive (vs. Satyananda and Miller)
- **Built on functions, with the koshas as the map.** The book is organised around six named jobs the practice does, and it explains the order of a session as a trip through the koshas and back. Satyananda's *Yoga Nidra* is organised by stages (sankalpa, rotation, breath, opposites, visualisation, sankalpa). Miller's iRest uses ten steps that begin with Intention and an **Inner Resource**. Desai explains *why* each element sits where it does more thoroughly than either.
- **Energy first.** Desai uses deliberate tension and release, bumblebee breath, the three-part breath, heavy/light and third-eye resting where Satyananda uses a fixed rotation and counted breath. Each technique is followed by an absorption pause, "the second half" (PDF p. 87). This pause is the method's signature and the most transferable idea in the book.
- **Intention comes late, from the "return path".** The deep Intention is planted on the way back up, at the Bliss body (PDF pp. 94–96, 129), and recalled again on the right side. Compare Satyananda's resolve at start and end, and Miller's intention and heartfelt desire set at the start. Desai also adds a **devotional return**, with Presence, guides and deceased relatives (PDF pp. 96, 341–342), which neither of the others has.
- **Intention as a craft for daily life.** Primary and secondary intentions; the three target areas; five qualities; the "feels positive" rule that allows negative words; shrinking it to one word for use while awake; and a full behaviour-change protocol in Ch. 11. Satyananda's resolve and Miller's heartfelt desire are both much thinner on technique.
- **Register.** Satyananda is directive and technical. Miller is invitational, welcomes whatever arises, and offers choice. Desai's printed script is directive and devotional and promises outcomes. Her trauma advice (Ch. 19) contains some iRest-like safety tools: eyes open, sitting up, grounding, a safe watching position.
- **Relation to Miller.** The book never discusses iRest as a method but **borrows its research base**: most of the "studies specific to Yoga Nidra" in Chs. 13, 18 and 19 are iRest studies.
- **Sleep.** Desai trains wakefulness, like Satyananda, but forgives sleep: let it happen if you are exhausted, and the body is said to take in the guidance anyway. She also explicitly allows bedtime use to help you fall asleep (PDF pp. 324, 329). This is closer to Dinsmore-Tuli than to the 1964 Satyananda line.

### Where Desai stands on the compendium's debates (`08`)
- **§1 Ancient or modern:** the word "Ancient" is in every section title. The history is unsourced (PDF pp. 20–22).
- **§2 Technique or state:** both. Techniques are platforms for diving into a state of non-doing (PDF pp. 12, 331).
- **§3 Falling asleep:** stay awake as a trained skill; early sleep is natural; sleep is acceptable when exhausted (PDF p. 329).
- **§5 Directive or invitational:** directive script (PDF pp. 333–343).
- **§6 Misconduct:** not mentioned.
- **§8 Sankalpa:** central (the Fourth Tool), but explicitly **optional** (PDF p. 134).
- **§10 Hypnosis:** related but different (PDF pp. 129–130). The Western relaxation roots of her techniques (progressive tension-release, autosuggestion) are not discussed.
- **§11 What to promise:** large promises: sleep equivalence, the neurochemical lists, healing anecdotes, disease prevention.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - **A rationale for every stage.** The kosha route in and out, "the second half", and the late Intention form a coherent, teachable logic (Ch. 7).
  - **Excellent teaching metaphors:** container and contents (lobster pot, sky, screen), ice → water → steam → ether, the boy who drops the reins, the tube, the sunglasses, samskaras as grooves (Grand Canyon), seeds and weather, the habit ladder, the toe-touch visualisation demo (PDF p. 228).
  - **The intention material is genuinely practical** (Chs. 10–11) and includes good worked examples for attitudes, habits and stressors.
  - **Sensible practice logistics:** props, chin height, warmth, side-lying in pregnancy, the raised hand, "surfing" vs. completion, disturbances, hearing loss, short class versions (Ch. 20).
  - **It plays down experience-chasing**: energy and psychic experiences are not the point (PDF p. 78). It frames emotional after-effects and gives a short **emotional-integration practice** (PDF pp. 271–272).
  - **Useful trauma tools** in Ch. 19: grounding, eyes open, seated, the movie-watcher stance, intentions about social support.
  - **It admits the evidence is borrowed** (PDF p. 196) and calls its own case data preliminary (PDF p. 204).
  - **Reception (Goodreads, earlier pass; not re-checked):** warm overall, about 4.25 from 265 ratings, and readers use it as a reference.
- **Limitations:**
  - **Theory far outweighs practice.** Practice begins at PDF p. 324 of 343, with one script that the book calls only a toe-dip. Goodreads reviewers (earlier pass) made the same complaint, and the full read confirms it.
  - **Evidence is pooled and inflated** (see the section above): meditation, MBSR and TM findings restated as yoga nidra effects; mismatched citations; popular sources; the Eysenck material; dopamine-based suggestions for Parkinson's and ADHD; cancer-prevention hints. A 2021 Goodreads reviewer's complaint that science and pseudoscience are presented as equally factual is fair.
  - **Metaphysics is stated as fact:** past lives, prana as the Holy Spirit, out-of-body travel as healing, guides and deceased relatives, third-eye manifestation.
  - **Unreferenced feats of the lineage founder** (the IONS timing, the TV demonstration, the particle generator) work as authority claims.
  - **Promotion:** the back matter and Ch. 20 point to paid Amrit trainings (PDF pp. 332, 345–346). The Foreword writer also helped with the book (PDF p. 6).
  - **Safety gaps:** no screening; an optional out-of-body stage; touching a student (the toe squeeze) without discussing consent. The trauma chapter's claim that flashbacks are "rare" is unsupported.
  - **Silence on the lineage's 1994 scandal.**
  - **Editing:** loose transliteration (*Ana*, *Mana*, and one "andamayakosha", PDF pp. 66, 68), small errors ("third law"), and typos. A 2020 Goodreads reviewer also wanted tighter editing.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **"The second half."** After every active technique, give an explicit absorption pause. It is a simple, credited structural principle that explains why yoga nidra is more than a string of relaxation exercises.
  - **A rationale box for each stage**, as Desai does, but add what the evidence actually shows (cite `06`). Readers praise her for the *why*; you can match that without the inflation.
  - **A function vocabulary** (settle, feel, witness, integrate, intend, restore). Build your own, or credit the Six Tools to Desai/Amrit if you borrow them.
  - **The ice/water kosha image**, labelled as a metaphor and credited, to explain why a sankalpa is planted when the mind is quiet.
  - **The intention craft:** goal vs. direction; primary vs. secondary; attitudes, habits, stressors; five qualities; the "feels positive" rule; one word for daily use; the four-step daily-life protocol (observe, make space, choose the opposite or the truth, rehearse in nidra). Paraphrase and credit. Pair it with her 2026 trauma-aware sankalpa advice [author, earlier pass].
  - **Late placement of the Intention** as a real alternative to discuss next to Satyananda's start-and-end resolve and iRest's opening intention.
  - **Energising alternatives to rotation:** tension and release (with her cardiac/hypertension/glaucoma caution), bumblebee breath, the three-part breath, heavy/light. Note that tension-release is Western progressive relaxation (Jacobson), even though she presents it as Amrit.
  - **Logistics checklist** from Ch. 20, including "surfing" vs. completion and the use of disturbances.
  - **Short emotional-integration practice** (PDF pp. 271–272) and the **habit ladder** (PDF pp. 279–286) as material for a habits chapter.
  - **Trauma options** (PDF p. 319), set out more systematically, with an Inner Resource first (`07`).
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Sleep equivalence, neurochemical lists, "65% dopamine", "11 hours / 17 CDs", Parkinson's/ADHD/cancer hints.** Leave them out, or quote one as an example of a myth.
  - **Pooled evidence.** Keep yoga nidra studies apart from MBSR, TM and IBMT studies, and keep iRest studies labelled as iRest.
  - **Brainwave ladder as mechanism.** Use it only as labelled shorthand. The EEG literature mostly shows theta changes, not delta with awareness [@kachera2025; @zaccaro2021].
  - **Metaphysical claims as fact** (past lives, out-of-body healing, guides, manifestation). Present them as lineage teaching ("in this tradition…") if you use them at all.
  - **The optional out-of-body stage**, unless you add screening and an opt-out; it conflicts with trauma-sensitive practice.
  - **Founder anecdotes as evidence** (the TV blood-pressure demonstration, the particle generator).
  - **The theory-to-practice ratio.** Put a short practice early and give several scripts.
  - **Authority and lineage.** Describe Amrit/I AM history as the school's self-description, and decide how to handle 1994 (`08` §6).
