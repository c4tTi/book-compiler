## In one paragraph
A short, warm, mass-market German guide that presents yoga nidra as a trainable skill for calming an overloaded nervous system, written by a GP turned health coach and yoga teacher. It promises better sleep, clearer thinking, less anxiety and more calm, and, distinctively, support for **women's hormonal balance**. The book has three parts. Chapter 1 explains what yoga nidra is "between science and mysticism": Patañjali's eight limbs, the "levels of consciousness", the nervous system and stress, and a selection of studies. Chapter 2 prepares the practice: setting, props, preparatory breathing and asanas, troubleshooting, and habit formation. Chapter 3, which the author calls the heart of the book, offers **eight short guided practices ("Flows")**, each for a mood or need. Each has a full script and an audio version narrated by the author via QR code. The reader is told to start with the audio and use the scripts later to guide themselves. The book speaks to complete beginners but says it also suits experienced practitioners and teachers. Its central promise: you can learn to produce deep relaxation yourself, instead of relying on alcohol, social media or TV, and so take responsibility for your own health ("Selbstwirksamkeit", "Selbstregulation").

## Core ideas
- **Deep relaxation is a skill you can train**, like a muscle or an asana. Self-generated relaxation is presented as the sustainable alternative to the pseudo-relaxation of alcohol, social media and TV. Yoga nidra is "the ultimate form of doing nothing".
- **Yoga nidra sits inside classical yoga.** After a short paragraph on each of Patañjali's eight limbs (Yama and Niyama share one subsection; about two pages in all), she says yoga nidra combines three of them: *pratyāhāra*, *dhāraṇā* and *dhyāna*. Brief tastes of *samādhi* can happen in the transitions between states of consciousness.
- **"Between science and mysticism".** The book wants both: studies that "support centuries-old wisdom", and a mystical layer in which yoga nidra is linked to the sleeping goddess or Shakti, the creative energy the practitioner opens to.
- **A state between waking and sleep that reaches the "subconscious".** In this state "old patterns and blockages" can be released and the whole system regenerates. This is the classic Satyananda-derived promise, stated without qualification in the part I read.
- **Chronic stress harms the whole body, "down to the cellular level"**; the essence of health is self-regulation of the nervous system and self-efficacy. Yoga nidra is offered as one "piece of the puzzle" for this, not as a cure (author, podcast and interview).
- **Women's hormones.** Stress raises cortisol, which can disturb oestrogen and progesterone and so the cycle (irregular bleeding, PMS, period pain). The book says yoga nidra may ease symptoms also in endometriosis and PCOS, cites one trial on hormone levels, and speculates about a pituitary and endorphin mechanism (passage read aloud by the author).
- **A practice for every mood.** Eight short practices are each tied to a need: quick relaxation, grounding, sleep, deep relaxation, clarity and creativity, calm and acceptance, hormone balance, and "boundless unfolding".
- **Habit beats knowledge.** Knowing about yoga nidra does not make you a practitioner; a routine does. Chapter 2 gives habit-building tips and short practices that fit a full day.
- **Comfort and permission.** Build a "Yoga-Nidra-Kokon" (cocoon) with props; there are "no rules" about posture (side-lying in pregnancy, sitting on a train with headphones, even standing); eyes may stay open with a lowered gaze; falling asleep is allowed (interview).
- **Audio first, then self-guidance.** Beginners follow the recordings; later the printed scripts serve as a "red thread" to guide oneself.

## The practice as this book teaches it
*How sure each point is:* **[H]** = heard: the book's own "Yoga Nidra für schnelle Entspannung", guided in full by the author in her podcast #117 and introduced there as the practice from the book; **[A]** = the author's own description of how the book's practices are built (interview, March 2025); **[S]** = seen in the publisher's sample; **[R]** = reported by readers. I did not read or hear the other seven practices.

**General template of the book's practices [A]:** take time to get comfortable ("check, check again"), become still; "in many practices" set a **sankalpa**, described as an intention planted like a small seed; then a **fairly quick body scan**; then **visualisations, sensations or a mantra**; breath awareness is included. She calls these "building blocks" that can but need not appear. The practices are "a mix of modern stories with old patterns": some she likes to teach, some she was once guided through, all adapted to her style. None is very long; she says she left out the hour-plus "deep" practices that can stir people up. She also says she chose words to avoid triggering anyone.

**The book's short practice, "Yoga Nidra für schnelle Entspannung" [H]** (about 12 minutes as recorded):

|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Arrival | Arrive where you are, lying **or sitting**; feel the support; close the eyes **or rest the gaze** on a point on the floor or the tip of the nose | 0:45 | The seated and eyes-open options are modern accessibility additions; Satyananda assumes eyes closed in śavāsana |
| 2. Deepening breath with cue words | Sink into the support with each exhale; three rounds of "inhale fresh energy / vitality / fullness, exhale let go" | 0:30 | Affirmation-style breath cues, closer to wellness meditation than to any lineage |

- **Not in this practice:** no sankalpa, no "I am practising yoga nidra" or "stay awake" instruction, no pairs of opposites, no visualisation. It is a relaxation and body-scan practice that works as an entry point. The author's description [A] says the longer practices add sankalpa, imagery, sensations or mantra.
- **Voice [H, S]:** informal *du* throughout. The introduction starts in formal *Sie* and then explicitly switches to *du*, because the practice works on "deeper levels" that respond better to a friendly *du*. The guidance uses gentle imperatives ("Spüre", "Lass", "Nimm wahr") softened with options and "vielleicht" (perhaps) and "wenn du magst" (if you like), plus a no-effort line ("you don't have to do anything"). The rotation is a terse list of body parts with two to three seconds per body part. Overall: **gently directive with permission language**, between Satyananda's commands and iRest's invitations.
- **Posture [S, A]:** śavāsana is the default, but sitting or standing is fine "if that works better"; side-lying is recommended in late pregnancy; padding under the sacrum and head and bent knees are suggested; eyes may stay open for people who panic when they close them.
- **Length [H, A, R]:** the practice I heard is about 12 minutes. In interview she says a practice can run anywhere from 10 to 60 minutes, that 20–30 minutes is often pleasant, that about 10 minutes is enough for "quick relaxation" and 20–30 for deeper practice, and that she is "a big fan of small portions" because a 30–40-minute plan creates resistance. She says no practice in the book is very long, and one reader returned the book because it has "mainly short meditations". Exact lengths of the eight recordings: not seen.
- **Sankalpa [A]:** optional ("can be done, need not") but "very common". She calls it an intention, a theme and an "anchor" for the practice, planted "like a small seed". It can be a single word or a whole sentence; her examples are "Ich lasse los" (I let go) and "I let go of everything I no longer need". Take whatever resonates or comes to mind first, without much thinking, or use one the guide suggests. Then feel it in the body. This is close to Satyananda's form (short, present tense) but without his insistence that it is required or that it will come true; I heard no promise of results. I could not see the book's wording, where in the sequence it comes, or whether it is repeated at the end. The quick practice I heard has none.
- **Sleep [A, author's podcast notes]:** falling asleep is allowed. Most guidance, she says, asks you to stay alert and follow the voice as an "anchor", but if you drift off "you needed it" and it is still regenerative; she has often fallen asleep in yoga nidra herself, and she has recorded practices meant to let you glide into sleep afterwards (the book's "guter Schlaf" practice, p. 103, is presumably one). She also describes a **hypnagogic** in-between state where you are not sure whether you were asleep, and agrees with an interviewer that it is "comparable" to hypnosis (debate 10). Her 2023 podcast notes say that if you fall asleep you needed sleep, if you come out alert you needed the pause, and if you come out tired but awake you need rest. This is the **"sleep is fine"** position in debate 3. In the sample she describes her first yoga nidra as feeling "as if I had slept for hours", and in a 2021 podcast she called a practice as effective as a power nap. I saw no "one hour equals four hours of sleep" claim, but these remarks are its softer relatives: the power-nap equivalence is likewise untested, and 06 §6.3 notes that no study compares yoga nidra with napping.

**The book's explanations of how it works (what I could see):**
- **Nervous system:** the subtitle and a 13-page section ("Das Nervensystem – Schlüssel zur Stressbewältigung", pp. 40–52) carry this framing, but **I did not read that section**. In her podcast and interviews she talks about self-regulation of the nervous system, stress hormones, and "shutting down" the nervous system as the point "where healing happens". Her earlier podcast on the vagus nerve (#99, Oct 2023, which I transcribed) shows how she handles the autonomic story. She explains the autonomic nervous system and the vagus as the main parasympathetic nerve, gives a short account of **Porges' polyvagal theory** (dorsal vagal freeze, ventral vagal social safety, sympathetic fight or flight), calls it a vivid "explanatory model" useful in therapy, but says it "has not gone uncriticised" because complex processes cannot be reduced to one nerve and it needs better research. She is openly sceptical of the commercial vagus-nerve hype (devices, oils) and recommends simple non-device options (humming, gargling, singing, slow breathing). If the book's chapter follows this line, it is more careful than most popular nervous-system writing. For your own book, note that the critique is now stronger than "not uncriticised": physiologists argue that the theory's basic premises are untenable (Grossman 2023; Grossman et al. 2026, 39 authors). The safe, well-supported claim is the general one: relaxation practices shift the balance towards parasympathetic activity (06 §6.4).
- **Brain activity:** the publisher says the book explains how yoga nidra "influences brain activity", and the author confirms that the book discusses brainwaves and the nervous system in more depth than her interviews. In one interview the host (a GP) brought in brainwaves: beta in conversation, alpha in front of the TV. She agreed, added theta and delta for the deeper states, "where yoga nidra ultimately leads", and said EEG studies of yoga nidra show people moving back and forth between states and drifting into a sleep-like state while still "half listening". The back-and-forth and the sleep-like drift match the compendium's summary (06 §6.1: alpha/theta, "local sleep"); a reliable delta state with awareness is not supported (06 §6.3). Check the book's wording in "Die Ebenen des Bewusstseins" (pp. 32–39).
- **Hormones:** see Core ideas. The one study I saw cited is **Rani et al. 2013** (*J Altern Complement Med* 19(12):925–929): 150 women with menstrual irregularities at one hospital in Lucknow, randomised to yoga nidra (35–40 min a day, five days a week, for six months) or a control group (the abstract does not say what, if anything, the controls did); 126 completed. TSH, FSH, LH and prolactin fell in the yoga nidra group compared with controls. The abstract reports only these hormone levels as measured outcomes, although its conclusion goes further and calls the practice helpful in dysmenorrhoea, oligomenorrhoea, menorrhagia and similar conditions. The book (as read aloud in podcast #117, about 11:03–11:56) retells the study in outline, but says the 126 women were split into two groups (150 were randomised; 126 completed) and links the hormone changes to improvements in menstrual complaints such as painful or irregular cycles, which the abstract does not report as an outcome. It then concludes that the study "confirms" yoga nidra as an effective way to support hormonal balance.
- **Research in general (interview):** she calls research on yoga nidra "manageable", a "niche within a niche", but "promising"; she mentions studies against other relaxation methods and no-treatment controls, on schoolchildren's wellbeing, on PMS and menstrual complaints, and on PTSD with iRest (she says only that "an American" developed this yoga-nidra-based method, about 29:41–29:52 in the recording; that is Richard Miller, whom she does not name). In the book podcast she calls the practice "nebenwirkungsfrei" (free of side effects); in the interview "nebenwirkungsarm" (low in side effects).
- **History and naming (interview, March 2025):** yoga nidra "has its origin in the ancient cultures", in the meditative branch of yoga, though perhaps not under that name. It was once reserved for advanced yogis who had first mastered the yamas and niyamas, concentration and breath, because the deep, long practices were thought to transform people. A "big school of yoga nidra founders" was "in the Himalaya" (her words, as far as the recording allows; she does not name Satyananda or Bihar in either interview), then iRest in the US, then Huberman's "non-sleep deep rest". She deliberately keeps the name "Yoga Nidra" rather than NSDR "to pay tribute to the tradition", and she agrees with the host that biohacking and productivity uses miss the point of a practice meant for self-knowledge (debate 9).
- **Gut and the vegetative nervous system (interview):** from her clients and retreats she reports that people whose digestion reacts to chronic stress can change their gut problems "sustainably" through repeated activation of the parasympathetic nervous system, once yoga nidra becomes a routine. This is clinical impression, not a cited study, and I do not know whether it appears in the book.

## What's distinctive
- **The only yoga nidra book in this set written by a practising medical doctor.** Mishra (1959) was a physician too, but his is a general yoga handbook with a short section on yoga nidra; Jacobson and Schultz were physicians writing on Western relaxation. Here a German GP specialist writes for a lay audience, with the medical framing that implies (stress physiology, hormones, a disclaimer that the book is no substitute for medical advice).
- **A women's-health angle.** It is the only book here with a dedicated **"Yoga Nidra für Hormonbalance"** practice and a rationale tied to the cycle, PMS, endometriosis and PCOS. Dinsmore-Tuli's cycle practices (reading files dinsmoretuli2020, dinsmoretuli2022) come from a women's-spirituality and Yoni Shakti angle; Göttinger's from a GP's endocrinology angle. This matches her coaching niche (fertility, perimenopause).
- **A self-help format around a practice library:** a mood-indexed set of eight short practices, QR-code audio, a habit-formation chapter and "hacks". It reads like a contemporary German health-coaching book that happens to be about yoga nidra, which fits its origin as a publisher's commission.
- **The newest German trade title.** Compared with the other German books in the project: Kündig and Trökes (reading files kuendig, troekes) are older mass-market titles that carry the sleep-substitute claims; Röcker (roecker) sells an "alpha-state" self-development method; Skuban (skuban2012) teaches a purpose-free practice with **no** sankalpa and no imagery. Göttinger keeps sankalpa and imagery in many practices, adds a nervous-system and hormone frame, and avoids (in what I saw) the "30 minutes = hours of sleep" promise.
- **Explicitly "between science and mysticism"**: a GP's book that also introduces the goddess/Shakti dimension of yoga nidra in its first chapter, which most clinically framed books leave out.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Clear, friendly and well organised: theory, then preparation, then practice, with the advice to start anywhere. Readers praise it as easy to understand, "spiritual but not aloof" and "undogmatic", with useful pointers to sources (Amazon.de reviews). It averaged **4.6/5 from 32 ratings on Amazon.de** on 2 Oct 2026 and ranked #47 in "Ratgeber Yoga"; it has gone to a **second printing** (2026). One reviewer met it in her yoga teacher training.
  - **Accessibility details** that many yoga nidra books skip: the "cocoon" of props, side-lying in pregnancy, padding the sacrum and head, eyes-open options for people who panic with closed eyes, a seated version on a train with headphones, and permission to fall asleep.
  - **Short, usable practices with audio** in the author's voice, plus scripts for later self-guidance. The quick practice (about 12 minutes) is a sound beginner's template.
  - **Honest in places about the research base** (interview: "a niche within a niche") and about silence being uncomfortable (stillness "is not so still"; things surface).
  - **Some awareness of trauma-sensitive wording**: she says she chose words so that nothing startles or triggers listeners.
  - **Respect for the name and the purpose.** She keeps "Yoga Nidra" instead of NSDR to credit the tradition, and she is openly sceptical of biohacking and productivity uses (interview). That is a clear, reader-friendly position on debate 9.
  - **A gentle, non-coercive sankalpa** (optional, a word or short sentence, felt in the body, no promised outcome; interview), which suits readers who dislike self-improvement pressure (debate 8).
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **No independent critical review found.** The public reception is retail reviews and friendly podcast interviews (a yoga-therapy podcast and a gut-health podcast hosted by a fellow GP). My search for journal or long-form blog reviews was limited (see Confidence).
  - **Evidence is overstated in the passages I saw.** One single-site trial with a control group whose activity the abstract does not describe, and 16% drop-out (Rani 2013), is said to "confirm" that yoga nidra is effective for hormonal balance. Falls in TSH, FSH, LH and prolactin are not in themselves "balance", and since the abstract does not say what the controls did, there is no way to tell from it whether the trial separates yoga nidra from attention and expectation (I read only the abstract, not the full text). The book also adds symptom improvement that the abstract does not report as an outcome (see above). The compendium's 2026 systematic review of menstrual disorders (Sony et al.) calls for more high-quality trials, and the broader meta-analyses rate the studies as low quality (06 §6.0). Symptom relief in **endometriosis and PCOS** is asserted without a cited trial in the passage read. The pituitary-and-endorphin mechanism is speculation.
  - **"Side-effect free" is not established.** Adverse events are poorly reported in yoga nidra trials (Dutta 2026, 06 §6.0), and meditation research documents occasional harms (Farias & Wikholm; see 07 Teaching and safety, §7.1). The author herself says some people panic when they close their eyes, and that silence can bring things up. That is a reason to give safety guidance, not to call the practice free of side effects.
  - **The history is told the traditional way.** "Eine uralte Praxis", studies that "support centuries-old wisdom", and (interviews) yoga nidra as a once "privileged" practice reserved for advanced students, with its main founding school "in the Himalaya". In the interviews she does not name Satyananda or the Bihar School, although the practice I heard follows Satyananda's template. None of this is sourced in what I saw, and it is the "yes" side of debate 1. In the sample the modern method is not distinguished from the medieval meaning of *yoganidrā* (Birch & Hargreaves). The Yoga Sūtra is dated to "about 200 BCE". That is a popular early date. Scholarly dates range from 500 BCE to 450 CE, and later dates are more commonly accepted (for example, Maas argues for about 400 CE; summary in the Wikipedia article on the Yoga Sutras).
  - **"Subconscious" and "blockages" language.** The claim that the state lets you dive "deep into the subconscious" and dissolve "old patterns and blockages" is presented as fact. The compendium treats "reprograms the subconscious" as metaphor, not mechanism (06 §6.3).
  - **The mysticism is vague.** The sample introduces the "Divine Goddess" (rendered as "göttliche Göttin") and Shakti, but in the part I read (the first two paragraphs of "Die Mystik von Yoga Nidra"; the sample breaks off after them) it does not name the goddess Yoganidrā or the *Devī Māhātmya*. Check what follows in the book.
  - **No lineage and unclear sourcing of the scripts.** The author has a 200-hour general yoga training and no stated yoga nidra training. She says the practices mix ones she likes to teach with ones she was once guided through, adapted to her style. The one I heard is a shortened Satyananda template. The book does not seem (from the sample and interviews) to explain *why* each stage exists.
  - **Short practices only.** Good for beginners and busy readers; a reader looking for longer practices returned it. There is little for experienced practitioners or teachers who want depth of method.
  - **Thin source list** (about three pages) for a book that leans on science.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **A mood or need index for the practices** ("Eine Praxis für jede Stimmung") with a short entry practice of about 10–12 minutes for busy readers. Show the length of each practice in the contents.
  - **A practical onboarding chapter** like her chapter 2: the "cocoon" of props, posture alternatives (side-lying in late pregnancy, sitting, headphones on a train), eyes-open with lowered gaze, and what to do when lying still feels uncomfortable. Add habit-formation advice: when, where, how often, and how to restart after a break.
  - **Audio by QR code in the author's voice**, with the explicit progression "follow the recording first, then guide yourself from the script".
  - **The quick practice's arc as a model short form:** arrive, breath, sounds from far to near to inside, a brief rotation, counting breaths back from 12, short rest, return. Write your own wording and rotation list.
  - **Her honest line that stillness is not always still.** It prepares readers for restlessness or emotions coming up, which is better than promising instant calm.
  - **A women's-health chapter** is an open niche, especially in German. If you write it, base it on the systematic review (Sony et al. 2026) and say clearly what is and is not known.
  - **Her sankalpa handling as a beginner default:** optional, one word or a short sentence, whatever resonates, then felt in the body, with no promise attached. Offer Satyananda's fuller form as an option for readers who want it.
  - **An explained choice of voice.** She explains her switch from *Sie* to *du*. Whatever voice you choose (debate 5), say why in one sentence.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **"Studies confirm" for single small trials, and mechanism speculation** (pituitary, endorphins). Use the 06 summary instead: better than nothing, about as good as other relaxation, mostly small trials. This is debate 11 ("What should you promise?").
  - **"Side-effect free" or "no risk"** (also debate 11). Say that it is low-risk for most people, name the exceptions (trauma, panic with closed eyes, some psychiatric conditions), and give opt-outs.
  - **Disease claims (endometriosis, PCOS) without trials.** If you mention them, frame them as stress support alongside medical care, not symptom relief.
  - **"Ancient practice" and "once secret or privileged" history.** Use "ancient roots, modern form" (debate 1), with Birch & Hargreaves, Singleton and Boyes for the modern story.
  - **Polyvagal theory as established fact.** If you explain the nervous system, use the plain sympathetic and parasympathetic account. Present polyvagal ideas, if at all, as a contested model (Grossman 2023; Grossman et al. 2026).
  - **Brainwave ladders** (alpha in front of the TV, theta and delta in deep practice). If you use them, add the 06 §6.1 caveats.
  - **"Subconscious" and "blockages".** Use them as metaphor, and say so.
  - **"Comparable to hypnosis".** She agrees with this in interview. If you raise it, explain the overlap and the difference (the practitioner chooses the resolve and stays the one who decides), as in debate 10 and 03 §3.12.
  - **The vague "Divine Goddess".** If you include the goddess, use the *Devī Māhātmya* and the scholarship (Pargiter's translation, Sarkar 2017), and keep her separate from the modern technique.
  - **Market lesson.** This is what German publishers currently commission: a doctor's voice, the nervous system, sleep, hormones, habits and audio, in 176 pages. It sells, but it is light on method, history and lineage. A German-language book with the same accessibility plus real depth on *why* each stage exists, honest evidence and the actual history would not compete with it head-on.
