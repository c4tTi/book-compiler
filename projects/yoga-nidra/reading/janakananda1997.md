## In one paragraph
This is not a book in the usual sense. It is a guided-relaxation recording with a short explanatory booklet, made by a direct Satyananda disciple who has taught yoga nidra in Scandinavia since 1970. It was first released in English in 1983 (per the school); Janakananda says he prepared his first yoga nidra tape in the 1970s. It is for anyone, beginners included: no prior knowledge is needed, you just lie still and follow the voice. There are two practices. The first is a 20-minute "Little Yoga Nidra" to learn on. The second is a 45-minute "Deep Yoga Nidra" that includes the chakras, with their seed mantras, visual symbols and Roop Verma's musical "sound symbols". Its central promise is that a fixed, systematic technique triggers relaxation by itself, without anyone telling you to relax, and then opens onto meditation and self-knowledge. According to the school, its teachers listened to this CD's deep practice in the 1999 Copenhagen PET study (Lou et al.) and the 2002 London/Copenhagen dopamine study (Kjaer et al.); neither abstract names the recording.

## Core ideas
(From Janakananda's essay "Tantra and Yoga Nidra", which twice sends the reader to the booklet for more, and from the school's companion articles.)
- **Yoga nidra is nyāsa.** The body rotation is the tantric practice of "touching" body parts, done mentally by naming them. Mantras can be "placed" on body parts in the same way. The deep practice is built on simple variations of nyāsa from beginning to end. One of its larger sections resembles *mātṛkā nyāsa*, in which the Sanskrit letters are distributed over the body. He gives dictionary definitions and Agehananda Bharati's definition of nyāsa, and a motor-homunculus figure linking each body part to its brain area.
- **Never say "relax."** By the school's account the word relaxation does not occur in the guidance, and you are never asked to feel relaxed or to imagine a part relaxing (the Swedish transcript bears this out for instructions, though the word names the practice in the short track's introduction). Keeping the mind busy with a fixed sequence "triggers" relaxation without effort. This is the school's main argument against suggestion-based and hypnotic relaxation, which it says produces an "artificial and limited state".
- **Pace keeps the mind occupied.** Attention moves on (thumb, index finger, middle finger…) before the mind has time to wander, so it "cannot hold any tension". Janakananda says the same principle runs through tantric ritual and Kriya Yoga.
- **One resolution only.** He calls the resolution good and effective, a way to steer the direction of your life. Several resolutions would scatter your energy, so make one. Because the critical mind is quiet in deep relaxation, he says, the resolution is not undermined by doubt. He wants a balance between influencing yourself (the resolution) and becoming conscious, by which he means touching and awakening, not analysing.
- **Fixed instructions, an experienced guide.** The long practice should be the same each time, so the subconscious "feels secure" (Mira). Whoever guides it needs long experience of yoga (Janakananda, citing Satyananda).
- **Layers of being.** The essay says the deep practice touches, names and vitalises every part of your being through nyāsa, and lists the dimensions it has in mind (body, feelings, images, chakras, finally identity); the school's science page adds "your breath" to what you experience. Janakananda maps this onto the five *kośas* (quoting the *Paiṅgala Upaniṣad*) and onto the chakras. The goal is the insight that no single layer defines you, and that your true identity is "the experiencing consciousness".
- **Chakras through sound and symbol.** In the deep practice the major chakras are named and given their seed mantras and visual symbols, "in accordance with the traditions of India and Europe". Roop Verma's music includes what the school calls the first recording of the chakras' traditional musical sound symbols (Nada Yoga).
- **Ancient roots.** Janakananda calls the practice tantric, says medieval Indian texts contain "exact descriptions of the content of Yoga Nidra", and credits Satyananda's "genius" with making it usable for everyone. He opens with a *Vijñānabhairava* verse about the point between waking and sleep, which he calls "more than 4000 year old".

Order in the Swedish deep practice (timings approximate, from the transcript): preparation and inner statement (0–1 min) → whole body → natural breath (2 min) → sankalpa (3 min) → rotation (4–9½ min) → heaviness, lightness, warmth, cold, pain (9½–17½ min) → joy and contentment (17½–19 min) → chakras with location, mantra and symbol (19–30 min) → scenes, inner space, rapid images, subtle-body passage (30–40 min) → witness consciousness and golden egg (40–41½ min) → sankalpa repeated → "Om tat sat" and return (41½–43½ min). This matches the PET account (body and face, then happiness, then a summer landscape, then the identity moment), with the chakras between happiness and the landscape. The chakra section was **not** among the four moments scanned (Janakananda says this himself).

Note on symbols: the 2021 successor has sections called "The mountain symbol in Yoga Nidra", "The four animals and…" and "The shape of the egg", and its Swedish blurb says shared human symbols with shamanic links are used deliberately in yoga nidra (my paraphrase of the Swedish LIBRIS/Bokinfo description). The essay lists animal symbolism among the nyāsa ways of touching the chakras, "many of which" are used in yoga nidra; it does not say this one is. The egg also appears in Satyananda's standard visualization ending (satyananda1976). In the Swedish deep practice the mountain, the animals (mouse, deer, bear, eagle) and the golden egg all occur as images [SV], so the 2021 book's symbol sections almost certainly refer to this recording.

**Little Yoga Nidra ("The Wholeness of Your Nature", about 20 min)**
- Set to "the sounds of Mother Earth". The publisher calls it "composed" by Janakananda, whereas the deep practice is "adapted", which suggests the short one is his own composition and the long one is his version of Satyananda's.
- It is the entry practice. The 2021 ebook tells readers to start with track 1 and move to track 3 once they know it and benefit from it. Mira adds that, once familiar with the short version, you can guide yourself through it mentally before taking on the deep one.
- **Content (Swedish stream [SV]):** a short spoken orientation (you will be guided into deep rest, and out wide awake at "Om tat sat"); the same preparation as the deep practice (position, note your surroundings, phone off, stay still), but **no inner statement and no resolution**; then, instead of a body-part rotation, **planes of contact**: body and floor, the lips, the eyelids, moving between them; the whole body; its stillness, and the life in it (warmth, movement, pulsing); natural breath awareness, then breath at the nostrils (the difference between in- and out-breath); then **mental alternate-nostril breathing with counting**, every fifth breath through both nostrils, starting again from one after any mistake; then a place you like for rest, yourself there in meditation, then yourself active with others; then four nature scenes (sunrise by the sea, a midsummer landscape at noon, a forest in late afternoon, a lake under a full moon at midnight); a short series of images (a red rose and its scent, other flowers, a white water lily, a crystal ball, a candle); a mirror in which your own face appears; then "Om tat sat" and a return that has you feel the body, listen to the surroundings, open the eyes and move.
- Users report doing it daily, for example the long one in the morning and the short one in the early evening.

**One stage, paraphrased (Swedish Little Yoga Nidra, opening) [SV].** After the preparation, the voice has you feel the whole body lying on the floor, its weight, and then the contact between body and floor: not the body and not the floor but the thin surface in between, felt all at once. You move to the place where the lips touch each other, back to the floor, then to where the upper and lower eyelids meet, and go back and forth among the three contact surfaces, faster towards the end, until the voice calls it an almost abstract experience. Then you enter the body itself, the whole of it from head to toes, leaving nothing out, and notice how still it has become, and that it is nonetheless full of life. The instructions are short, plain statements; nothing tells you to relax.

**Sleep (as the school teaches it; whether the booklet says exactly this is not confirmed)**
- Mira's article (*Bindu* no. 11 per IAYT): dozing off at first is normal, though the aim is to stay aware; if you miss some instructions, don't try to recall them, just rejoin the voice where it is now.
- Mira's tips, in brief: firm surface, light cover, loose clothes, head to the north; to stay awake, uncover hands and feet or prop the forearms upright.
- The 2021 successor has three sections on sleep [T].

**Voice and language style:** Directive, fixed and spare: short present-tense statements and lists of names ("thumb, index finger, middle finger"), with silences [SV]. Short sentences you repeat inwardly (I am awake, I am aware, I practise yoga nidra; my translation) recur between stages, and the inner statement at the start is a formula shared with Satyananda (janakananda2021 notes the same) [SV, P]. In the guidance itself no one is told to relax; the word relaxation does appear as a *name* for the practice in the short practice's introduction, and the short practice once invites you to pick a place where you can best unwind [SV]. Some images carry strong second-person suggestion: you are told, for example, to picture the highest mountain you can imagine and that you make contact with a bear (my translation). The school describes the listener's attitude as "a child listening to a fairy tale": actively taking part, but without effort [A]. **Length:** 20 or about 44–45 minutes. **Posture:** on the back, on the floor, completely still.

## What's distinctive
- **The recording the school says was used in the 1999 and 2002 PET studies.** The school says its teachers listened to this CD's deep practice in the Copenhagen PET study (Lou et al. 1999) and in the 2002 London/Copenhagen dopamine study (Kjaer et al.). Neither abstract names the recording (the school says the 2002 paper itself makes clear that the "meditation" was yoga nidra); check the methods sections. I know of no other commercial yoga nidra recording that the school or researchers name as the stimulus in a PET study. Other neuroimaging work on yoga nidra exists (e.g. Fialoke et al. 2024, fMRI; `06` §6.1); I did not check which recording it used. The school's account names the script content at each scan point, which lets you link specific stages to specific findings.
- **The upstream source of the NSDR "65% more dopamine" line.** The figure that Huberman/NSDR material repeats comes from Kjaer 2002, i.e. from listening to this lineage's recording (`10` §10.6; `06` §6.3).
- **An early English-language yoga nidra recording** (released in English in 1983 per the school), by a disciple who learned from Satyananda in 1968, still sold, in the same voice in four languages.
- **Nyāsa explained more fully than by Satyananda.** It has dictionary and scholarly definitions, the link between *mātṛkā nyāsa* and the deep practice, and the homunculus image. The 2021 successor's publisher blurb goes further, saying body-scanning was taken from nyāsa and has been used regrettably in shallow therapies (janakananda2021 quotes the English wording).
- **Music as part of the method.** The chakra stage is built on recorded Nada Yoga sound symbols. No other book in this project's set integrates played chakra tones into the guidance.
- **A "no-suggestion" doctrine** stated more strongly than in Satyananda: never ask anyone to relax.
- **A two-tier design:** a short self-composed practice to learn on, and a long adapted practice for depth.
- **A Scandinavian reception history**, outside the Bihar/US/UK narrative: a Danish women's-weekly health editor, the Swedish alternative-spirituality magazine *Sökaren*, Norwegian and Danish testimonies.

## Strengths and limitations
- Strengths:
  - Continuity and authority: guided by someone trained by Satyananda himself, in the same voice since the 1983 English release (per the school) through 1996/97 and 2012. What the 1970s tape contained is unknown.
  - A clear, teachable theory of why the method works without relaxation instructions: fixed sequence, pace, occupying the mind.
  - Accessible: no prior knowledge needed. The short practice works as a beginner's on-ramp.
  - A well-documented account of a lineage practice in laboratory research, with the school openly noting some limits (the chakra section was not scanned; in the 1999 study the subjects had done Kriya Yoga that morning, which the school says may have intensified the effects).
  - The school's free streams in three languages let you hear the actual practice.
- Limitations / critiques:
  - **Thin as a "book".** The text is a short booklet. The ideas live in website essays and in the 2021 successor. I found **no independent serious review** of the CD or booklet: no journal, no Yoga Journal, no long-form blog. Reception material is publisher-curated blurbs (Lisa McColley Anderson of Spirit Links, USA; Nils-Olof Jacobson in *Sökaren*; Karen Pallisgaard, health editor of the Danish weekly *Femina*; A. Thomson, a UK therapist and bookseller, whose blurb on the Swedish page reads "Detta band är en hit", i.e. "this tape is a hit", my translation) and user testimonies. On Goodreads it has 46 ratings averaging 4.28, with one written review (October 2026).
  - **Overstated antiquity** (cross-reference to `08` §1 and `01` §1.1, checked against Birch & Hargreaves [@birch2015]). Birch & Hargreaves treat the *Vijñānabhairava* as one of the tantras that preceded the 12th-century *Dattātreyayogaśāstra*; nothing there supports "more than 4000 years". For an actual date, check a scholarly edition. They also show that in medieval yoga texts *yoganidrā* names a samādhi-like state, not this guided sequence, which conflicts with the claim of "exact descriptions" of yoga nidra's content.
  - **Health claims stated as fact** on the publisher, distributor and shop pages (the Greenleaf text appears to reproduce the package copy), and in the English introduction track's preview: blood pressure "normalized", immune system "strengthened", rest "that sleep seldom provides". The school's "Relaxation in Yoga" page also quotes Satyananda: "Half an hour of Yoga Nidra is equivalent to four hours of sleep" (a stronger variant of the "1 hour = 4 hours" claim in `06` §6.3), and a testimony describes partly replacing sleep three nights a week. None of this is supported (`06` §6.3). Some testimonies (schizophrenia medication side effects "practically disappeared"; shingles and eyesight; a lactic-acid explanation of postnatal aches that is outdated physiology) are not material to repeat.
  - **The research is promotional here.** The school presents "up to 65%" more dopamine without its limits. Research check:
    - *Lou et al. 1999* (blood flow, Copenhagen): nine "highly experienced yoga teachers" (abstract), yoga nidra vs the resting state of normal consciousness. The school's article on this study speaks of "seven" teachers measured; I cannot resolve the difference (perhaps subjects excluded from analysis; check the methods). The morning Kriya Yoga belongs to this study, per the school.
    - *Kjaer et al. 2002* (dopamine, London/Copenhagen): the abstract gives no sample size (`06` §6.1 gives n = 8); the school says the dopamine rise occurred while listening to this CD, guided by Janakananda (`06` describes the subjects as experienced practitioners, reportedly meditation teachers). Control: attending to speech with eyes closed. Raclopride binding fell 7.9%, which the authors equate to a 65% increase in dopamine release; the fall correlated with more EEG theta. **Gratification and depth of relaxation did not differ** between the two conditions (abstract). I found no replication. Note for `06` §6.1: it should record this subjective null result.
    - *EEG claims:* the school reports theta up 11 percentage points and alpha essentially unchanged in the PET sessions, and a Cologne EEG comparison (early 1980s) in which yoga nidra produced more even alpha than suggestion-based relaxation; I found no publication of the Cologne work. Measure these against `06` §6.1 (alpha/theta, local sleep).
    - The school's claim that the study proves meditation is "the fourth major state" goes beyond the papers.
  - **The "not hypnosis" claim is debatable.** Being asked to experience heaviness and warmth is the core formula of autogenic training (Schultz 1932), and a single resolution repeated in a deep state is close to autosuggestion (`08` §10). Avoiding the word "relax" does not remove suggestion. It moves it.
  - **Guru-centred framing.** Secrecy, initiation, testing the aspirant, and the requirement that only long-experienced teachers guide. The essay also places yoga nidra next to tantric sexual ritual as examples of nyāsa. It gives Satyananda unqualified credit ("his genius"). The Satyananda lineage was the subject of Australian Royal Commission Case Study 21 (hearing December 2014, report 2016; `08` §6; Birch & Hargreaves also flag the hearing). I found no mention of it in the school pages I read, and janakananda2021 found none on the English site.
  - **No trauma-sensitive options are offered** (choice language, eyes-open option, opting out) in anything I read or in the Swedish transcript (`07` §7.1–7.2). The deep practice asks you to call up a pain and go into it, to imagine lying almost unclothed in winter cold, and later to leave the body in a subtle body and look down at yourself. For some listeners (trauma, dissociation, psychosis) these are exactly the passages to soften or make optional.

## For your own book
- **Bottom line:** Listen once to the free 44–45-minute Swedish or Danish stream of the deep practice (or the English album on Apple Music) to hear the pacing, the stillness instructions and the absence of relaxation instructions; the 20-minute track is worth hearing for its contact-plane opening. Buy the English MP3 (8.95 at the school shop) only if you want to quote the English wording. The booklet is not worth buying for text. Cite it in your research chapter (the Copenhagen studies, told accurately) and your rotation chapter (pace, nyāsa). Usefulness ★ to ★★.
- Take:
  - **The "no relax words" test** as an editing rule for your scripts. Janakananda's reason, that a fixed sequence lets relaxation arise rather than be ordered, is a clear way to explain why rotation works. Present it as a design principle, not as proof that the method avoids suggestion.
  - **The pace principle** (the thumb example: move on before the mind drifts). It is the best plain-language justification for rotation speed I have seen in this lineage. Use it to explain why rotation is brisk, not lingering.
  - **The right-side/left-side demonstration.** Micheline Flak (RYE, France) stopped after the right side and asked students to compare the two sides; a Håå student describes the "lopsided" effect when Janakananda accidentally did the right side twice. It is a vivid teaching moment for a chapter on rotation, or a reader experiment. Attribute it to Flak, via Janakananda.
  - **The two-tier structure:** a short core practice to learn and eventually self-guide, then a long practice for depth. It is a good template if your book comes with audio.
  - **One resolution only**, placed after breath awareness and repeated many times at the end, phrased positively as if already happening (Swedish stream), with Janakananda's balance between influencing and becoming conscious. It is a useful middle position for debate 8 (`08` §8).
  - **Contact planes** from the short practice (body and floor, lips, eyelids): a fresh, concrete alternative to opening with a full rotation, good for a short script. Describe it in your own words and credit the recording.
  - **The Copenhagen story, told accurately.** Name the actual script moments that were scanned (body and face, happiness, a summer landscape, "who am I"). Say what was *not* scanned (the chakras), who the subjects were, and what the control conditions were. Note that subjective relaxation did not differ in Kjaer 2002. This makes a strong, honest box for your research chapter (`06` §6.1; `10`). Cite Lou 1999 and Kjaer 2002 for the data, and the school only for which recording was used.
  - **The nyāsa explanation**, but go to the sources it cites (Agehananda Bharati's definition; the essay does not name the book, probably *The Tantric Tradition*, 1965; check) and pair it with the historians' caution that nyāsa is a claimed inspiration, not a proven line (`01` §1.1).
  - **The practical sleep tips** (uncovered hands and feet, forearms raised). They are concrete and kind. Attribute them to the school (Mira), not to "tradition".
- Skip or treat critically:
  - "4000-year-old" texts and "exact descriptions" in medieval sources: use Birch & Hargreaves instead.
  - Blood pressure normalised, immunity strengthened, "65% more dopamine", "fourth state proven", "half an hour equals four hours of sleep": leave them out, or quote them only as lineage claims and correct them (`06` §6.3).
  - The anti-hypnosis rhetoric: report it as the lineage's self-understanding, then give the autogenic and autosuggestion parallels (`08` §10).
  - Healing testimonies (psychosis, shingles, eyesight): don't reuse them. Psychosis calls for clinical support (`07` §7.1).
  - Head-to-the-north: mention it as a tradition only, if at all.
  - Secrecy and "only experienced teachers may guide": a fair point about quality, but if your book teaches readers to guide themselves or others, say what experience you think is actually needed instead of borrowing the guru frame.
  - Don't present this as "Satyananda's" yoga nidra unchanged: the short practice is Janakananda's own composition, and the deep one is his adaptation, which includes a chakra section set to Roop Verma's chakra music.
