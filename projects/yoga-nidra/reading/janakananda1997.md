## In one paragraph
This is not a book in the usual sense. It is a guided-relaxation recording with a short explanatory booklet, made by a direct Satyananda disciple who has taught yoga nidra in Scandinavia since 1970. It has been in print, in the same voice, since a cassette of 1983 (Janakananda says he prepared his first yoga nidra tape in the 1970s). It is for anyone, beginners included: "all you have to do is lie still on your back and follow the instructions." There are two practices. The first is a 20-minute "Little Yoga Nidra" to learn on. The second is a 45-minute "Deep Yoga Nidra" that adds the chakras, with their seed mantras, visual symbols and musical "sound symbols" recorded by Roop Verma. Its central promise is that a fixed, systematic technique triggers relaxation by itself, without anyone telling you to relax, and then opens onto meditation and self-knowledge. Its fame comes from outside the yoga world. Danish researchers used its deep practice in the 1999 Copenhagen PET study and the 2002 dopamine study, the most-cited neuroimaging of yoga nidra.

## Core ideas
(From Janakananda's essay "Tantra and Yoga Nidra", which twice sends the reader to the booklet for more, and from the school's companion articles.)
- **Yoga nidra is nyāsa.** The body rotation is the tantric practice of "touching" body parts, done mentally by naming them. Mantras can be "placed" on body parts in the same way. The deep practice is built on simple variations of nyāsa from beginning to end. One of its larger sections resembles *mātṛkā nyāsa*, in which the Sanskrit letters are distributed over the body. He gives dictionary definitions and Agehananda Bharati's definition of nyāsa, and a motor-homunculus figure linking each body part to its brain area.
- **Never say "relax."** The word relaxation does not occur in the guidance, and you are never asked to feel relaxed or to imagine a part relaxing. Keeping the mind busy with a fixed sequence "triggers" relaxation without effort. This is the school's main argument against suggestion-based and hypnotic relaxation, which it says produces an "artificial and limited state".
- **Pace keeps the mind occupied.** Attention moves on (thumb, index finger, middle finger…) before the mind has time to wander, so it "cannot hold any tension". Janakananda says the same principle runs through tantric ritual and Kriya Yoga.
- **One resolution only.** Making a resolution is "good and effective"; it would be foolish not to. But one resolution, not several, so as not to scatter energy. In the relaxed state the resolve works at full strength. He wants a balance between influencing yourself (the resolution) and becoming conscious, by which he means touching and awakening, not analysing.
- **Fixed instructions, an experienced guide.** The long practice should be the same each time, so the subconscious "feels secure" (Mira). Whoever guides it needs long experience of yoga (Janakananda, citing Satyananda).
- **Layers of being.** The practice "touches" body, breath, feelings, images, chakras and finally identity. Janakananda maps this onto the five *kośas* (quoting the *Paiṅgala Upaniṣad*) and onto the chakras. The goal is to realise that you are not bound to any one layer, and that your true identity is "the experiencing consciousness".
- **Chakras through sound and symbol.** In the deep practice the major chakras are named and given their seed mantras and visual symbols, "in accordance with the traditions of India and Europe". Roop Verma's music includes what the school calls the first recording of the chakras' traditional musical sound symbols (Nada Yoga).
- **Ancient roots.** Janakananda calls the practice tantric, says medieval Indian texts describe its content "exactly", and credits Satyananda's "genius" with making it usable for everyone. He opens with a *Vijñānabhairava* verse about the point between waking and sleep, which he calls "more than 4000 years old".

## The practice as this book teaches it
I could not hear the recordings, so this is a reconstruction from the author's essay, the school's articles and the PET-study account. Each line says where it comes from. Compare it with Satyananda's eight stages in `04` §4.1.

**Deep Yoga Nidra ("Discover Your Self", about 45 min)**

| Stage | What Experience Yoga Nidra does (as reported) | Source / certainty | Compared with Satyananda 1976 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preparation | Lie on your back, arms by your sides, legs a little apart, eyes closed, completely still. Just listen. | School stream pages; publisher; LIBRIS blurb (certain) | Same |
| Sankalpa | One personal resolution, central to the method; the booklet explains its use | Essay (certain that it is taught); **where and how often it occurs on the track: not confirmed** | Satyananda: three times at the start and the end |
| Rotation of awareness | Fixed order starting at the right thumb, then the other fingers; right side, then left. Done early and without a break. Includes detailed attention to the parts of the face | Essay (Flak anecdote); PET article (face) (reported, consistent) | Same skeleton; the face detail is noted |
| Breath | The breath is among the things you experience | One line on the school's science page (thin) | Satyananda counts breaths; counting not reported here |
| Opposites | Heaviness/lightness, warmth/cold, pain and pleasure ("pain and contentment") | Essay (certain) | Same |
| Feeling state | Experience of happiness and contentment (the second PET scan point; called "abstract" by the researchers) | PET article (reported) | Satyananda has no separate joy stage; compare iRest's joy step |
| Images | A summer day in the countryside (third PET scan point); "symbols, landscapes, pictures". Likely mountain, animal and egg symbols (see note) | PET article; essay; 2021 ToC (likely, not confirmed for the CD) | Satyananda uses rapid images and *chidākāśa* |
| Chakras | After body and mind are "thoroughly aware and relaxed": the major chakras (he says eight) with names, seed mantras and visual symbols, accompanied by Roop Verma's chakra sound symbols | Publisher text by Janakananda; essay (certain that it is there; exact list and wording not seen) | Beyond the basic eight stages; Janakananda says Satyananda taught that yoga nidra "begins with the experience of the chakras" |
| Self-inquiry | Near the end, the question "who am I" and the experience of identity, of being centred (fourth PET scan point) | PET article (reported) | Not part of Satyananda's basic sequence; closer to Miller's awareness stage |
| Ending | Not described in what I read. The 2021 book has a section called "Coming out again" | ToC only | Satyananda: slow externalisation |

Order: the PET account puts body (face), then happiness, then summer landscape, with "who am I" at the end. Janakananda's publisher text puts the chakras after the body and mind work. The chakra section's exact position relative to the landscape is unknown. The chakra section was **not** among the four moments scanned in the PET study (Janakananda says this himself).

Note on symbols: the 2021 successor has sections called "The mountain symbol in Yoga Nidra", "The four animals and…" and "The shape of the egg", and its blurb says common human symbols with shamanic links are "used deliberately in Yoga Nidra". The essay lists "animal symbolism (possibly a connection back to shamanism)" among the nyāsa methods used in yoga nidra. These are probably images in the deep practice, but I have not confirmed it.

**Little Yoga Nidra ("The Wholeness of Your Nature", about 20 min)**
- Set to "the sounds of Mother Earth". The publisher calls it "composed" by Janakananda, whereas the deep practice is "adapted", which suggests the short one is his own composition and the long one is his version of Satyananda's.
- It is the entry practice. The 2021 ebook tells readers to start with track 1 and move to track 3 once they know it and benefit from it. Mira adds that, once familiar with the short version, you can guide yourself through it mentally before taking on the deep one.
- Its stage content is not described in anything I read. Users report doing it daily, for example the long one in the morning and the short one in the early evening.

**Sleep (as the school teaches it; whether the booklet says exactly this is not confirmed)**
- You may fall asleep at first, and that is fine, but the aim is to stay awake and aware. Full sleep is usually a passing stage. If you lose the instructions for a moment, don't try to recall what you missed; rejoin where the voice is now (Mira, *Bindu*).
- Practical tips (same source): lie with your head to the north; wear loose clothes; use a firm rug or blanket, not a soft bed, with a light cover. If you tend to doze, uncover your hands and feet. If that is not enough, rest your elbows on the floor with the forearms upright, so they drop and wake you when you nod off.
- The 2021 successor has three sections on sleep: sleep and wakefulness in yoga nidra, yoga nidra and normal sleep, and staying awake in practice.

**Voice and language style:** directive and fixed: the same words every time, a sequence the listener follows. By the school's own account it never uses relaxation words. The school frames the listener's attitude as "a child listening to a fairy tale": actively taking part, but without effort. I have not heard the tone.

## What's distinctive
- **The research recording.** This is the only yoga nidra book or recording tied directly to the major neuroimaging studies. The school says its teachers listened to this CD's deep practice in the Copenhagen PET study (Lou et al. 1999: nine "highly experienced yoga teachers") and in the 2002 London/Denmark dopamine study (Kjaer et al.). The school's account names the script content at each scan point, which lets you link specific stages to specific brain findings. No other book can offer that.
- **One of the earliest Western Satyananda-line recordings, and still in print**, by a disciple who learned from Satyananda in 1968. There was a first tape in the 1970s (year not given) and an English cassette in 1983, ahead of most English-language yoga nidra audio. It has been the same voice in four languages for four decades.
- **Nyāsa explained more fully than by Satyananda.** It has dictionary and scholarly definitions, the link between *mātṛkā nyāsa* and the deep practice, and the homunculus image. The 2021 blurb's charge that "body-scanning" was taken from nyāsa and "regrettably" used in "shallow therapies" is a sharp lineage position you won't find stated so bluntly elsewhere.
- **Music as part of the method.** The chakra stage is built on recorded Nada Yoga sound symbols. No other book on the list integrates chakra tones into the guidance.
- **A "no-suggestion" doctrine** stated more strongly than in Satyananda: never ask anyone to relax.
- **A two-tier design:** a short self-composed practice to learn on, and a long adapted practice for depth.
- **A Scandinavian reception history**, outside the Bihar/US/UK narrative: a Danish women's-weekly health editor, the Swedish alternative-spirituality magazine *Sökaren*, Norwegian and Danish testimonies.

## Strengths and limitations
- Strengths:
  - Continuity and authority: one practice, one voice, from the 1970s tape through 1983, 1996/97 and 2012, guided by someone trained by Satyananda himself.
  - A clear, teachable theory of why the method works without relaxation instructions: fixed sequence, pace, occupying the mind.
  - Accessible: no prior knowledge needed. The short practice works as a beginner's on-ramp.
  - A rare, well-documented bridge between a lineage practice and laboratory research, with the school openly noting some limits (the chakra section was not scanned; subjects had done Kriya Yoga that morning).
  - The school's free streams in three languages let you hear the actual practice.
- Limitations / critiques:
  - **Thin as a "book".** The text is a short booklet. The ideas live in website essays and in the 2021 successor. I found **no independent serious review** of the CD or booklet: no journal, no Yoga Journal, no long-form blog. Reception material is publisher-curated blurbs (Lisa McColley Anderson of Spirit Links, USA; Nils-Olof Jacobson in *Sökaren*; Karen Pallisgaard, health editor of the Danish weekly *Femina*; a UK therapist-bookseller who called "this tape" a hit) and user testimonies. On Goodreads it has 46 ratings averaging 4.28, with one written review (October 2026).
  - **Overstated antiquity.** Scholars place the *Vijñānabhairava* in the early medieval period (roughly the second half of the first millennium CE), not "4000 years" ago (check against a scholarly edition before printing a date). The claim that medieval texts describe yoga nidra's content "exactly" conflicts with Birch & Hargreaves [@birch2015]. There, *yoganidrā* names a samādhi-like state, not this guided sequence (`01` §1.1, `08` §1).
  - **Health claims stated as fact** on the packaging and publisher pages: blood pressure "normalized", immune system "strengthened", rest "that sleep seldom provides". The school pages also repeat "half an hour of yoga nidra equals four hours of sleep" (attributed to Satyananda), and a testimony describes partly replacing sleep three nights a week. None of this is supported (`06` §6.3). Some testimonies (schizophrenia medication side effects "practically disappeared"; shingles and eyesight; a lactic-acid explanation of postnatal aches that is outdated physiology) are not material to repeat.
  - **The research is promotional here.** The "up to 65%" dopamine figure is presented without its limits: a small sample of the school's own teachers, guided by their own teacher's voice, after their morning Kriya Yoga, with no replication. The Kjaer 2002 abstract itself says that participants' **gratification and depth of relaxation did not differ** between the yoga nidra condition and the control (listening to speech with eyes closed). The school's claim that the study proves meditation is "the fourth major state" goes beyond the papers. The school's figures also differ: "seven" teachers in its article, nine in the 1999 abstract. A 1980s Cologne EEG comparison with suggestion-based relaxation is reported only by the school (I found no publication).
  - **The "not hypnosis" claim is debatable.** Being asked to experience heaviness and warmth is the core formula of autogenic training (Schultz 1932), and a single resolution repeated in a deep state is close to autosuggestion (`08` §10). Avoiding the word "relax" does not remove suggestion. It moves it.
  - **Guru-centred framing.** Secrecy, initiation, testing the aspirant, and the requirement that only long-experienced teachers guide. The essay also places yoga nidra next to tantric sexual ritual as examples of nyāsa. It gives Satyananda unqualified credit ("his genius") without any of the context discussed in `08` §6.
  - **No trauma-sensitive options are mentioned** (choice language, eyes-open option, opting out of the pain/pleasure pair) in anything I read (`07` §7.1–7.2).

## For your own book
- Take:
  - **The "no relax words" test** as an editing rule for your scripts. Janakananda's reason, that a fixed sequence lets relaxation arise rather than be ordered, is a clear way to explain why rotation works. Present it as a design principle, not as proof that the method avoids suggestion.
  - **The pace principle** (the thumb example: move on before the mind drifts). It is the best plain-language justification for rotation speed I have seen in this lineage. Use it to explain why rotation is brisk, not lingering.
  - **The right-side/left-side demonstration.** Micheline Flak (RYE, France) stopped after the right side and asked students to compare the two sides; a Håå student describes the "lopsided" effect when Janakananda accidentally did the right side twice. It is a vivid teaching moment for a chapter on rotation, or a reader experiment. Attribute it to Flak, via Janakananda.
  - **The two-tier structure:** a short core practice to learn and eventually self-guide, then a long practice for depth. It is a good template if your book comes with audio.
  - **One resolution, kept for a long time** (with Janakananda's balance between influencing and becoming conscious). It is a useful middle position for debate 8 (`08` §8).
  - **The Copenhagen story, told accurately.** Name the actual script moments that were scanned (body and face, happiness, a summer landscape, "who am I"). Say what was *not* scanned (the chakras), who the subjects were, and what the control condition was. Note that subjective relaxation did not differ. This makes a strong, honest box for your research chapter (`06` §6.1; `10`). Cite Lou 1999 and Kjaer 2002 for the data, and the school only for which recording was used.
  - **The nyāsa explanation**, but go to the sources it cites (Agehananda Bharati's definition; the essay does not name the book, probably *The Tantric Tradition*, 1965; check) and pair it with the historians' caution that nyāsa is a claimed inspiration, not a proven line (`01` §1.1).
  - **The practical sleep tips** (uncovered hands and feet, forearms raised). They are concrete and kind. Attribute them to the school (Mira), not to "tradition".
- Skip or treat critically:
  - "4000-year-old" texts and "exact descriptions" in medieval sources: use Birch & Hargreaves instead.
  - Blood pressure normalised, immunity strengthened, "65% more dopamine", "fourth state proven", "half an hour equals four hours of sleep": leave them out, or quote them only as lineage claims and correct them (`06` §6.3).
  - The anti-hypnosis rhetoric: report it as the lineage's self-understanding, then give the autogenic and autosuggestion parallels (`08` §10).
  - Healing testimonies (psychosis, shingles, eyesight): don't reuse them. Psychosis calls for clinical support (`07` §7.1).
  - Head-to-the-north: mention it as a tradition only, if at all.
  - Secrecy and "only experienced teachers may guide": a fair point about quality, but if your book teaches readers to guide themselves or others, say what experience you think is actually needed instead of borrowing the guru frame.
  - Don't present this as "Satyananda's" yoga nidra unchanged: the short practice is Janakananda's own composition, and the deep one is his adaptation with an added chakra-and-music section.
