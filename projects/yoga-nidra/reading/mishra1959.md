**Tradition / lineage:** Independent. Dr Rammurti S. Mishra (d. 1993), later Shri Brahmananda Sarasvati, was an Indian physician and surgeon (professor of medicine at Podar Medical College, Bombay, per Brunton's introduction, p. 15) who taught in the USA. He dedicates the book to his teacher "Bhagawan Bodhisatwa" (p. 4). His ashram's site says he founded the Yoga Society of New York (1958), Ananda Ashram (1964) and the Yoga Society of San Francisco (1972). It was the official text for his New York and Ohio classes (publisher's note, p. 9). I found no link to Sivananda or the Bihar School. ## In one paragraph
*Fundamentals of Yoga* is a general yoga course written by a medical doctor for American students in 1959. For yoga nidra history it matters for one reason. It is the earliest English yoga book this project has found that treats **"yoganidra" as a state you deliberately induce with a technique**, with four chapters on it (chs. 3, 9, 10, 15). That is 17 years before Satyananda's *Yoga Nidra* (1976), five years before his 1964 "I discovered yoga nidra" talk, and 14 years before Boyes (1973) (dates from the project's `satyananda1974`, `satyananda1976` and `boyes1973` summaries). Mishra's yoganidra is not the later guided body-rotation practice. It is a **sleep of the body with the mind awake, produced by "samyamah"** (fixation, suggestion and sensation; see Core ideas). He describes it in the vocabulary of hypnotism and "magnetism", and promises that it can be deepened to the point of surgical anaesthesia. **How much is about yoga nidra:** the four yoganidra chapters fill about 18 of 255 pages (about 7%). They sit inside a run on suggestion, relaxation, gazing and "magnetism" (chs. 2–15, about 30% of the book).

## Core ideas
- **Concentration is suggestion.** Mishra reads dhāraṇā, dhyāna and samādhi as fixation, suggestion and the "flowing of consciousness" (p. 41). Their combination is *samyamah*, which he renders as fixation, suggestion and sensation (pp. 60, 67, 190); on p. 44 the third term is consciousness instead.
- **Definition of yoganidra.** He splits the word: *yoga* means concentration and *nidrā* means sleep, so yoganidra is sleep produced by complete concentration (p. 44). The body sleeps soundly while the mind stays awake. You can call this sleep up at will and dismiss it at will (p. 45).
- **A state with degrees.** Yoganidra deepens from local to general and from superficial to deep. Its manifestations include lethargic, cataleptic and somnambulistic states (p. 79), the three phases Charcot distinguished in his 1882 paper on hypnotism (my comparison; Walusinski & Bogousslavsky 2020, p. 3). The degrees are: (1) numbness and loss of surface sensation, a first degree of "magnetism"; (2) all sensations, deep or superficial, lost, the body "is magnetized", body, senses and mind partially under control, partial anaesthesia, visions (p. 80); (3) the body fully "magnetized" and pulsing; (4) identity with supreme consciousness (pp. 79–80). Ch. 9 also lists 20 signs, from eyelid fluttering and yawning to analgesia and stronger intuition (pp. 81–82).
- **How it is induced.** Physiologically by repeated postural and reflex stimulation, psychologically by dhāraṇā, dhyāna and samādhi (p. 79). Ch. 10's **positive** method is active and command-based, for rapid induction; the **negative** method is passive identification with supreme consciousness, possible only after the first is mastered (pp. 83–85).
- **Pratyāhāra means relaxation.** Pratyāhāra is the withdrawal of "energy and consciousness" from the organs, either one part at a time ("local") or the whole body ("general"), graded by degrees of relaxation (pp. 41, 66–67). Satyananda later made the same link.
- **Yoga as dehypnotism.** Ordinary life is hypnosis by bad suggestion; yoga replaces it with deliberate good autosuggestion (pp. 40, 103), and hypnotism is a distorted yoga (p. 28).
- **Post-meditative suggestion** (ch. 14): suggestions planted for after the session, modelled on posthypnotic suggestion (pp. 101–104).
- **Anaesthesia and healing claims.** Ch. 15 claims yoganidra can make any body part, yours or another's, insensitive to needles, operations or flame, and that it improves character as drugs cannot (pp. 106–108). P. 45 claims it removes every disease, even karmic and inherited ones.

## The practice as this book teaches it
Pieced together from snippets. The order and wording of whole sessions are not confirmed. The closest thing to a complete guided session is the ch. 13 group session (pp. 96–99); ch. 10 (pp. 83–88) gives the methods.
- **Posture:** a comfortable reclining position, on a bed or easy chair (p. 63). In ch. 18, on the third eye rather than yoganidra, he adds that the seat should be stable enough that you will not fall if deep sleep comes (p. 123). No search hit for *śavāsana* or "corpse".
- **Body sequence:** legs, abdomen, chest, arms, head, each by fixation, suggestion, then flow of consciousness (p. 41): a slow ancestor of the rotation, not rapid naming.
- **Suggestion formulas (first person, repeated):** the worked example is the arm. You fix attention on the whole arm, announce that you are about to relax it, repeat a short first-person "I relax my arm" formula, then move into suggestions that the arms are relaxing and growing heavier and heavier. The snippet then breaks off at a clause about wishing to move. It looks like the classic hypnotic arm-heaviness test (pp. 45–46). The legs get a similar heaviness formula, said three times, with the promise that they will be under your command and cold (p. 72). The eyes and eyelids get the same kind of first-person relaxing formula (p. 63).
- **Gaze induction:** trāṭakam lying down, gazing unblinking at an overhead bulb, then eyelid-relaxation suggestions (p. 63); structurally an eye-fixation hypnotic induction (my comparison).
- **"Magnetizing":** once relaxed, feel heart pulsation and an "electromagnetic" current spreading through one part or the whole body; with practice one command does it (pp. 76, 92–94).
- **Anaesthesia (ch. 15):** relax and "magnetize" a chosen part, then give strong repeated suggestions of painlessness backed by will power, in yourself or another person (pp. 106–107).
- **Group form (ch. 13):** the teacher leads learners aloud ("listen to me"), adapting the Upaniṣadic "lead me from darkness to light" prayer, then a whole-body relaxing formula and attention to heart pulsation (p. 97). Visitors may watch this "Yoga performance" and decide whether to join (pp. 96–99).
- **Length:** no session length or practice schedule found in the snippets; check pp. 83–88 and 96–99 in a borrowed scan.
- **Language style:** strongly directive. It uses commands, will power and first-person autosuggestion ("I relax..."). No *saṅkalpa* by name, but the formulas and post-meditative suggestions do its job. No breath counting, opposites or image sequences seen.

## What's distinctive
- **The earliest dated English-language book in this project's reading set that uses "yoganidra" for an inducible practice**, with whole chapters on it (compared with `satyananda1974`, `satyananda1976` and `boyes1973`; the wider literature was not surveyed). Lamb's IAYT bibliography lists only ch. 15; chs. 3, 9 and 10 matter more.
- **Hypnosis in plain view.** Of the project's yoga nidra books compared here, none is this open about its debt to hypnotism: the Satyananda lineage uses autosuggestion but insists the method is not hypnosis (`satyananda1976`), and Boyes 1973 rejects hypnotic use and Schultz-style formulas (`boyes1973`). Mishra uses Charcot's states, "magnetism", eye fixation, heaviness formulas, posthypnotic-style suggestion and hypno-anaesthesia, and calls hypnotism degraded yoga. His heaviness formulas sit close to Schultz's autogenic training (1932), itself built on the heaviness reported by hypnotised patients (`schultz1932`), which makes the hypnosis/autosuggestion lineage concrete.
- **State and technique in one.** Mishra keeps the medieval sense of yoganidrā as samādhi (p. 84 pairs "supreme samādhi and supreme yoganidra"; compare Birch & Hargreaves). He also gives a step-by-step induction. Both sides of the "technique or state?" debate (`08` §2) are already in this one 1959 book.
- **The opposite pole to Boyes.** Mishra's command-based yoganidra (USA, 1959) and Boyes's anti-hypnotic, observational one (Paris, 1973) suggest that "yoga nidra" had no single origin.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:** primary, dated evidence for the 20th-century history of the term. A physician with a documented US teaching context; clear graded descriptions of what the word meant to one Indian teacher in the 1950s.
- **Limitations / critiques:** No published review or critique was found; reception is unchecked.
  - The anaesthesia and cure-all claims are unsupported and potentially dangerous. Hypnotic analgesia is a real research topic, but no study in `06` tests yoga nidra as surgical anaesthesia; see `06` §6.3 for claims to handle carefully. The nearest evidence, pain-outcome trials, shows no clear advantage over active controls (`06` §6.0, Ghai & Ghai 2025).
  - The "magnetism" physiology is 19th-century mesmerism in medical dress; anaesthesia "in others" and visitor demonstrations raise consent questions; the prose is uneven (the publisher's note admits as much, p. 9).

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **A precise history footnote**, correcting the registry's citation. Cite Mishra, *Fundamentals of Yoga* (New York: Julian Press, 1959), chs. 3, 9, 10 and 15 (pp. 44–46, 77–88, 106–108), as the earliest English manual you have found that treats yoganidra as a state induced by fixation and suggestion. This dates the modern usage before Satyananda (1964 talk, 1976 book) and outside India, without claiming Mishra invented it.
  - **Evidence for "Is it hypnosis?"** (`08` §10): in 1959 the word was already tied to hypnotic states, eye fixation, heaviness formulas and posthypnotic-style suggestion, supporting an honest "close cousin" position.
  - **"Body asleep, mind awake"** predates Satyananda's book; credit it to no single teacher.
  - **A precedent for the closing resolve:** ch. 14's post-meditative suggestions parallel the final saṅkalpa.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - Never repeat the anaesthesia claims as benefits; if mentioned, frame them as period claims with a safety note.
  - Leave out the "magnetism" and "electromagnetic" physiology, the karmic and chromosomal disease claims, the promise that it removes all diseases (see `08` §11, "What should you promise?"), and the "sinner becomes a saint" promise.
  - Don't model your voice on the command style or the visitor "performance"; both clash with invitational, trauma-sensitive teaching (`07` §7.4).
  - Don't cite "Lancer Books, 1959" (see Confidence), don't call Mishra the first to use the word (*yoganidrā* is medieval), and don't imply Satyananda drew on him (no link found).
