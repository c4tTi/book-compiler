**Tradition / lineage:** Independent. The author, Dr Rammurti S. Mishra (d. 1993), later Shri Brahmananda Sarasvati, was an Indian physician who taught in the USA. The book's introduction calls him a physician and surgeon, a professor of medicine at R. A. Podar Medical College in Bombay, and chief physician of the attached hospital (p. 15). He dedicates the book to his teacher, "Bhagawan Bodhisatwa" (p. 4). His ashram's site says he founded the Yoga Society of New York (1958), Ananda Ashram (1964) and the Yoga Society of San Francisco (1972). The publisher's note says the book was written as the official text for his classes in New York and Ohio (p. 9). I found no link to Sivananda or the Bihar School. **Length / format:**
- 1st ed.: New York, Julian Press, 1959. 255 pp., 22 cm, drawings by Oscar Weinland (LC 59-10065).
- **Lancer Books paperback reprint**: undated, made "by arrangement with the Julian Press" and carrying the 1959 copyright. Lancer only existed 1961–1973, so the reprint dates from then.
- London: Lyrebird Press, 1972, 255 pp.
- Anchor/Doubleday, 1974, xxviii + 210 pp.
- Julian Press/Crown, 1987 (also catalogued as Harmony 1986 and Three Rivers 1987), xxiii + 198 pp. It adds a foreword by John White, and the introduction is credited to Paul Brunton.

The book is a course in 31 numbered "lessons" and chapters, with exercises and first-person suggestion formulas. There are no scripts in the modern sense, apart from one teacher-led group session (ch. 13).

## In one paragraph
*Fundamentals of Yoga* is a general yoga course written by a medical doctor for American students in 1959. It covers mind, concentration, gazing, pratyāhāra, cakras, kuṇḍalinī, āsana, prāṇāyāma, "supernatural powers" and samādhi. For the history of yoga nidra, it matters for one reason. It is the earliest English yoga book this project has found that treats **"yoganidra" as a state you deliberately induce with a technique**, and it gives the subject four chapters: ch. 3 "Samyamah and Yoganidra" (p. 44), ch. 9 "Yoganidra" (p. 77), ch. 10 "Methods for Creating the State of Yoganidra" (p. 83) and ch. 15 "Anesthesia Produced by Yoganidra" (pp. 106–108). That is 17 years before Satyananda's *Yoga Nidra* (1976), five years before his 1964 "I discovered yoga nidra" talk, and 14 years before Boyes (1973). Mishra's yoganidra is not the later guided body-rotation practice. It is a **sleep of the body with the mind awake, produced by "samyamah"**, which he glosses as fixation, suggestion and sensation. He describes it in the vocabulary of hypnotism and "magnetism", and promises that it can be deepened to the point of surgical anaesthesia. **How much is about yoga nidra:** the four yoganidra chapters fill about 18 of 255 pages (about 7%). They sit inside a connected run of chapters on suggestion, relaxation, gazing and "magnetism" (chs. 2–15, pp. 32–108, roughly 30% of the book). The rest is general yoga.

## Core ideas
- **Concentration is suggestion.** Mishra reads dhāraṇā, dhyāna and samādhi as fixation, suggestion and the "flowing of consciousness" (p. 41). Their combination is *samyamah*, which he renders as "fixation, suggestion, and sensation" (pp. 44, 66). He calls suggestion the governing power of nature and the principle of concentration (ch. 2, pp. 32–38).
- **Definition of yoganidra.** He splits the word: *yoga* means concentration and *nidrā* means sleep, so yoganidra is sleep produced by complete concentration (p. 44). The body sleeps soundly while the mind stays awake. You can call this sleep up at will and dismiss it at will (p. 45).
- **A state with degrees.** Yoganidra deepens from local to general and from superficial to deep. Its manifestations include "lethargic, cataleptic, and somnambulistic states" (p. 79). These are the three hypnotic states Charcot described in 1882. The degrees are: (1) numbness and loss of surface sensation; (2) loss of all sensation, partial control, the body "magnetized in second degree", visions; (3) the body fully "magnetized" and pulsing; (4) identity with supreme consciousness (pp. 79–80). Ch. 9 also lists 20 signs ("evidences"), including eyes closing, eyelids fluttering, anaesthesia, analgesia, yawning, stretching, "sleepiness or sleeplessness" and stronger intuition (pp. 81–82).
- **How it is induced.** Physiologically, by repeated stimulation of postures and reflexes. Psychologically, by dhāraṇā, dhyāna and samādhi (p. 79). Ch. 10 gives two methods. The **positive** method is active, works by command, and is meant for "rapid induction". The **negative** method is passive: you forget the body and the universe and identify with supreme consciousness. It is only possible once the first method is mastered (pp. 83–85).
- **Pratyāhāra means relaxation.** Pratyāhāra is the withdrawal of "energy and consciousness" from the organs, either one part at a time ("local") or the whole body ("general"), graded by degrees of relaxation (pp. 41, 66–67). This links yoganidra to pratyāhāra, as Satyananda later did.
- **"Yoga is dehypnotism."** Ordinary people live under bad suggestions, a kind of hypnosis. Yoga replaces these with deliberate good autosuggestion (pp. 40, 103). Mishra calls hypnotism "a distorted form of Yoga" (p. 28).
- **Post-meditative suggestion.** Ch. 14 teaches suggestions planted in the subconscious for the period after meditation, explicitly on the model of posthypnotic suggestion (pp. 101–104).
- **Anaesthesia and healing claims.** Ch. 15 says yoganidra can make any part of the body, in yourself or in another person, insensitive enough for needles, operations or a candle flame. A Yoga anaesthesia also transforms character, which drugs do not (pp. 106–108). Elsewhere he says yoganidra removes "all mental and physical diseases", including diseases carried through past incarnations and inherited through the chromosomes (p. 45).

## The practice as this book teaches it
Pieced together from snippets. The order and wording of whole sessions are not confirmed.
- **Posture:** a comfortable reclining position, on a bed or easy chair (p. 63). It should be stable enough that if deep sleep comes you will not fall (p. 123). No search hit for *śavāsana* or "corpse".
- **Body sequence:** relax the legs, then the abdomen, chest, arms and head, applying fixation, then suggestion, then flow of consciousness (p. 41). This is a slow, part-by-part ancestor of the rotation of consciousness, not a rapid naming.
- **Suggestion formulas (first person, repeated):** the worked example is the arm. You fix attention on the whole arm, announce "Now I am going to relax my arm", repeat "I relax my arm", then shift to "my arms are relaxing... growing heavier... very heavy". Next comes a clause beginning "although I wish to move...", cut off in the snippet. It looks like the classic hypnotic arm-heaviness test (pp. 45–46). The legs get a similar heaviness formula, said three times, with the promise that they will be under your command and cold (p. 72). The eyes get "I relax my eyes; I relax my eyelids" (p. 63).
- **Gaze induction:** trāṭakam lying down, staring without blinking at a light bulb overhead, then eyelid-relaxation suggestions (p. 63). This is structurally Braid's eye-fixation hypnotic induction (my comparison, not Mishra's).
- **"Magnetizing":** once relaxed, feel heart pulsation and "electromagnetic" current spreading through one part ("local magnetism") or the whole body ("general magnetism"). With practice a single command magnetizes the whole body (pp. 76, 92–94).
- **Anaesthesia (ch. 15):** relax a chosen part (tongue, ear, hand, foot, abdomen...), "magnetize" it, then give strong, repeated suggestions that it will feel no pain, backed by will power. He calls producing it in yourself "subjective" and in another person "objective" (p. 106). Regular training is said to make this routine (p. 107).
- **Group form (ch. 13):** the teacher leads learners aloud ("listen to me"), adapting the Upaniṣadic "lead me from darkness to light" prayer, then "I relax my entire body", heart pulsation and breath retention (p. 97). Visitors may watch this "Yoga performance" and decide whether to join (pp. 96–99).
- **Language style:** strongly directive. It uses commands, will power and first-person autosuggestion ("I relax..."). There is no *saṅkalpa* by name, but the suggestion formulas and the post-meditative suggestions do the same job. I saw no breath counting, pairs of opposites or image sequences in these chapters, though I could not check every page.

## What's distinctive
- **The earliest dated English-language book we have that uses "yoganidra" for an inducible practice**, with whole chapters on it. Lamb's IAYT bibliography lists only the 3-page ch. 15. In fact chs. 3, 9 and 10 treat the term more fully.
- **Hypnosis in plain view.** No later yoga nidra book is this open about its debt to hypnotism. It uses Charcot's three states, mesmeric "magnetism", eye-fixation induction, heaviness formulas, posthypnotic-style suggestion and hypno-anaesthesia. It even claims hypnotism as degraded yoga.
- **State and technique in one.** Mishra keeps the medieval sense of yoganidrā as samādhi (p. 84 pairs "supreme samādhi and supreme yoganidra"; compare Birch & Hargreaves). He also gives a step-by-step induction. Both sides of the "technique or state?" debate (`08` §2) are already in this one 1959 book.
- **The opposite pole to Boyes.** Before Satyananda, Mishra's pro-suggestion, command-based yoganidra (USA, 1959) and Boyes's anti-suggestion, observational one (Paris, 1973) show that "yoga nidra" did not have a single origin.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:** primary, dated evidence for the 20th-century history of the term. The author was a physician who taught openly in the USA, with a documented teaching context (the Yoga Society of New York). His clear graded descriptions show what the word meant to at least one Indian teacher in the 1950s.
- **Limitations / critiques:** I found no published review. My web-search budget for this session was used up, so I could not search for reviews properly, and I cite none.
  - On the evidence, the anaesthesia and "all diseases" claims are unsupported and potentially dangerous. Suggesting that trained students need no anaesthetic for urgent operations is a safety problem.
  - Hypnotic analgesia is a real research topic. But the yoga nidra pain trials show no clear advantage over active controls (`06` §6.0, Ghai & Ghai 2025).
  - The "magnetism" and "electromagnetic current" physiology is 19th-century mesmerism in medical dress.
  - Producing anaesthesia "in others" and the visitor demonstrations raise questions of consent and authority.
  - The prose is uneven. The publisher's note says it was printed in "Dr. Mishra's personal expression".

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **A precise history footnote**, correcting the registry's citation. Suggested wording: "The earliest English yoga manual I have found that treats 'yoganidra' as a state to be induced is Rammurti S. Mishra's *Fundamentals of Yoga* (New York: Julian Press, 1959), chs. 3, 9, 10 and 15 (pp. 44–46, 77–88, 106–108). There, yoganidra is 'sleep resulting from complete concentration', produced by fixation and suggestion." This dates the modern usage before Satyananda (1964 talk, 1976 book) and outside India, without claiming Mishra invented it.
  - **Evidence for the "Is it hypnosis?" section** (`08` §10). In 1959 the word was already tied to Charcot's hypnotic states, eye-fixation induction, heaviness formulas and posthypnotic-style suggestion. This supports an honest "close cousin" position better than any modern source in the project.
  - **The "body asleep, mind awake" definition** is older than Satyananda's book. Credit it to no single teacher.
  - **A precedent for the closing resolve.** Ch. 14's post-meditative suggestions to the subconscious are a 1959 parallel to repeating the saṅkalpa at the end. They are useful when you explain where the closing resolve comes from.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - Never repeat the anaesthesia, surgery, needle or candle claims as benefits. If you mention them at all, present them as period claims and add a safety note.
  - Leave out the "magnetism" and "electromagnetic" physiology, the karmic and chromosomal disease claims, and the "sinner becomes a saint" promise.
  - Don't model your voice on the command style or the group "performance" for visitors. They clash with trauma-sensitive, invitational teaching (`07` §7.4).
  - Don't cite "Lancer Books, 1959" (see Confidence), and don't call Mishra the first to use the word. *Yoganidrā* is medieval, and the modern practice crystallised among several teachers.
  - Don't imply that Satyananda drew on Mishra. No link was found.
