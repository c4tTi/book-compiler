## In one paragraph
Call taught "nerve training" in Massachusetts (Nathoo 2016), including at Lasell Seminary in Auburndale (Andrick 2012, abstract). This popular handbook argues that modern people, especially women, are worn out less by work than by **useless tension**: they hold themselves on the bed in sleep, brace in chairs and carriages, and clench while they think or worry. She adopts "Americanitis", a name she credits to a German physician practising in America, for this condition (Ch. II, p. 13). Her remedy is a **trained, gradual return to "Nature's" way of resting**, learned like a gymnastic skill. You start by lying on the floor and giving your weight to gravity, then carry the same economy of effort into movement, study, emotion and the will. For a yoga nidra writer, it is the earliest text in the project that sets out a **supine, part-by-part heaviness practice followed by slow nasal breathing**. Call is one of the figures (with Jacobson) whom Nathoo (2016), citing Singleton (2005), names in modern yoga's Western relaxation ancestry.

## Core ideas
- **Repose is a trained skill.** Learning to let go needs as regular a training "from the first simple steps" as muscle-building (Ch. I). It comes slowly, takes "many hours" to learn (p. 70) and is lost again without practice (p. 99).
- **Lying down is not the same as resting.** Few people rest fully on the bed; many hold some of these tense: the spine, knees and legs, clenched hands, tongue, throat and face (pp. 16–17). Call adds that cases using all these contractions at once are "not in the majority", but very few have none (p. 17). Five minutes of complete rest is said to beat hours of tense reclining (p. 19). This is the same point Jacobson makes about rest and relaxation (see `jacobson1929.md`).
- **Body first, mind follows.** Racing thought at bedtime is to be "attacked" through the body: do not fight the thoughts, relax the muscles, and the thoughts lose their grip (p. 19). The order is muscles, then dropping thoughts "without resistance", then freedom from care (p. 20).
- **Do not try.** "To do nothing with an effort is impossible" (p. 102). Effortful relaxing does harm, so do not "care especially whether you do it right or not" (pp. 109–112).
- **Worry is tension to be released, not argued with.** Never resist a worry: relax first, philosophise afterwards (p. 70). Her one-line remedy is to think your legs heavy when you begin to worry (p. 70). Elsewhere, writing about students' lesson-worry, she says outright that "worry is brain tension" (Ch. XIV, p. 132).
- **The will is trained to yield.** Yielding is the hardest and strongest use of the will. A stammering boy improves by dropping resistance, not by forcing (Ch. XIX, enlarged edition only; not in the 1891 text).
- **Nature, the sleeping baby and the cat are the models.** The cat appears in Ch. X (p. 78 in the 1892 OCR; the reviewer placed it on p. 79) and again in Ch. XII (p. 97); the baby is the subject of Ch. XI. Fifteen minutes a day spent thinking about Nature's ways (p. 74) is called "the first form of prayer" (p. 75). The framing is liberal Protestant and naturalist, not "Oriental".
- **Gravity is the teacher.** Let the bed, chair or train carry you instead of holding yourself up (Ch. IV).

## The practice as this book teaches it
**Ch. XII "Training for Rest" (pp. 93–112), in order:**
1. **Posture:** flat on the back. The floor is better than the bed because it does not yield. Give the whole weight to it.
2. **Passive "proving" by a helper** (pp. 98–102). A friend lifts, bends, tosses and drops each arm, then each leg, then rolls the head and lifts the trunk by the hands. The pupil neither helps nor resists, until each limb is like sandbags loosely joined. This is hands-on work and has no counterpart in yoga nidra, which is guided by voice.
3. **Self-practice, eyes closed throughout** (pp. 103–104). **Think** one leg heavy, then the other, then each arm and both arms, keeping the legs heavy, then the trunk and head, and finally the whole body: "wonder how the floor can hold such a weight". It is a mental, imagery-based heaviness sweep, with no tensing. The order runs from the limbs to the centre, not from the right thumb.
4. **Breath** (p. 104). Inhale quietly through the nose, letting the lungs seem to fill by themselves, lower lungs first and then upper. Exhale "with a sense of relief" and let the body sink like a deflating rubber bag. Then breathe rhythmically at about **six breaths a minute**, starting with 10 breaths and building up day by day to 50. Two or three long breaths follow every later movement.
5. **Very slow movements** (pp. 104–107): dragging the heel up and letting the leg drop, raising the arm and letting it fall "by pieces", rolling the head, uncurling the spine "like beads on a string", and rolling over "like a bag of loose bones".
6. **Dose and cautions** (pp. 107–112). The first effects may be unpleasant, from nausea and giddiness to fainting. Very tense people should start with five minutes a day. Practise with a teacher. Without one, practise ten minutes a day for several weeks.

**Class routine (Ch. XIV, p. 139):** eyes closed and opened slowly; **fifty slightly lengthened breaths, counted** "to keep the mind from wandering"; relaxing motions; then directing the will into one arm while the rest stays free; then a minute's concentration on one chosen subject.

**Against the standard yoga nidra stages** (my own mapping, parallels only): settling/posture = yes (floor, supine); sankalpa = no; rotation of consciousness = partial (a limb-heaviness sweep, limbs → centre, not starting at the right thumb); breath awareness = yes (counted, building to 50, about 6 a minute); opposites = no (heaviness only); visualisation = no; ending = no formal stage (eyes opened slowly, then slow movements).
**Language:** directive and second-person ("think one leg heavy", "let go"), but always paired with "do not work at it".

## What's distinctive
- **The earliest datable primary text in the project** for a lying-down sequence of heaviness, part by part, with eyes closed, followed by slow breathing. It is about 38–41 years before Jacobson (1929) and Schultz (1932), and a year before Genevieve Stebbins's *Dynamic Breathing and Harmonic Gymnastics* (1892), whose lying-down "Yoga Breathing" exercise is energising rather than relaxing (per `singleton2010.md`).
- **Its heaviness is imagined, not tensed.** It is closer to autogenic heaviness and to yoga nidra's "feel the body heavy" stage than to Jacobson's method, which rejected "thinking" a muscle relaxed (`jacobson1929.md`).
- **The "relaxation first, then the mind quiets" sequence and the advice not to fight thoughts** are both stated plainly in 1891.
- **It checks shafer2024's claim:** neither "nidra" nor "yoga" occurs anywhere in either text (keyword search of the Gutenberg e-text and the 1892 OCR, zero hits), and nor do "Oriental", "Hindu" or "India". Shafer's statement that Call describes "Nidra" (p. 107) is unsupported. The resemblance is structural only.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:** free, short and concrete (posture, order, breath rate, dose). It openly warns about adverse reactions on first relaxing, which is rare for its time. The anti-effort framing is good, and so are the everyday examples (the train, the carriage, waiting).
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **Gendered moralising** about "nervous" women, hysteria and jealousy (Ch. IX, XIX), and "Americanitis" as a cultural diagnosis.
  - **No evidence beyond anecdote.** Its physiology is about the "fuel" of nervous force.
  - **Jacobson called Call a "cult" follower of Swedenborg whose interests were "not scientific"** (Jacobson 1934, pp. 44–45, as quoted by Nathoo 2016).
  - **The hypnosis controversy:** in 1890, two Lasell students reportedly collapsed after her nerve-training class, and the press blamed hypnotism. Call denied hypnotising them. Andrick (2012, abstract) describes her method as a blend of Delsartean relaxation, New Thought and self-hypnotic techniques ("Delsartean hypnosis").

  - **Debate 10 (`08` §10, "Is it hypnosis?"):** add Call/Andrick to "Close cousin" as the 1890 case where a relaxation class was publicly accused of hypnosis. At present that bullet names only Coué and Schultz.
  - **Her heaviness sweep (limbs → trunk → head → whole body), as a short settling stage** before a rotation, and "wonder how the floor can hold such a weight" as an image (paraphrase it).
  - **"Do not try to stop thinking; let the body go and the thoughts lose their hold" (p. 19)** for your chapter on the busy mind. It is a 19th-century precedent for the non-striving instruction.
  - **Her safety advice, updated:** first sessions may bring nausea, dizziness or fainting, so start with 5–10 minutes for very anxious people and practise with a teacher (pp. 107–112). The modern counterpart is in `07`: a review of meditation studies found adverse events in about 8% of participants [@farias2020], and yoga nidra trials rarely record adverse events at all [@dutta2026]. It fits trauma-sensitive pacing (`07`).
  - **A historical note on breath:** about six breaths a minute, counted to fifty, and the exhale as "letting go". Any claim about the effects of slow breathing **needs a source**: the compendium (`06`) has no slow- or resonance-breathing evidence yet. Call's own reasons are 19th-century physiology.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - The hands-on "proving" by a helper (consent and touch issues; not transferable to voice-guided teaching).
  - The gendered diagnoses and the moralising about invalids, and any suggestion that tension is a character flaw.
  - **Do not repeat the claim that Call taught "Nidra"** (shafer2024); she did not use the word.
  - Cite the 1891/1892 text, not the enlarged 1900 edition (Gutenberg, LibriVox), for anything about "1891" (Ch. XVII–XIX are later).
