## In one paragraph
A Harvard cardiologist's popular book, written with the writer Miriam Klipper and first published a year before Satyananda's *Yoga Nidra* (1976). It argues four things:
- Modern life sets off Cannon's fight-or-flight reaction over and over, and this feeds high blood pressure and heart disease.
- The body has an innate opposite, which Benson names the **relaxation response**: a wakeful state of lowered metabolism, measured first in TM meditators.
- Prayer and meditation in many religions, and secular methods such as autogenic training, progressive relaxation and hypnosis, all produce this **same** response.
- Anyone can produce it with a simple, non-religious, free technique: sit, relax the muscles, and repeat "one" silently on each out-breath for 10–20 minutes.

It became a long-running bestseller (the publisher claims over six million copies sold).

**How much concerns yoga nidra: none directly.** As far as I could check, the word *nidra* does not appear. Google's frequent-terms list for the 1975 edition includes "Yoga", "Yogi", "Transcendental Meditation", "prayer", "Taoism" and "sleep", but not "nidra"; I could not search the text itself. ## Structure
Table of contents not available. Reported contents, in roughly the book's own order:

- **The medical stakes:** hypertension, atherosclerosis, heart attack and stroke (Google term list).
- **The TM studies** (Wikipedia, citing pp. 61–68 of the 2001 edition):
  - TM meditators approached Harvard in 1968.
  - Benson met Maharishi Mahesh Yogi to agree in advance that any outcome could be published.
  - R. Keith Wallace was doing parallel work at UCLA.
  - Metabolism fell within minutes of starting meditation.
- **Blood-pressure studies** in hypertensive patients (pp. 111–118, 2001 edition).
- **The "age-old wisdom" survey** of religious and secular practices (pp. 125–154, 2001 edition). The term list shows "recollection" (probably the Christian contemplative term), Taoism, Yen Hui (probably the disciple in the Zhuangzi's "fasting of the mind" passage), prayer and yoga.
- **The technique.** The 2000 edition adds a long new introduction (54 roman-numbered pages), which I did not see.

## Core ideas
- **Fight-or-flight versus its counterpart.** Benson's papers call the relaxation response "an integrated hypothalamic response" whose changes mirror the "emergency reaction" (Benson, Greenwood & Klemchuk 1975, abstract). It is meant to work by lowering sympathetic nervous activity.
- **The physiological markers:**
  - **Falls** in oxygen consumption, carbon-dioxide output and breathing rate (Beary & Benson 1974); in heart rate, metabolism and "brain activity" (Benson's institute); and in blood lactate (PBS).
  - **Lower blood pressure in hypertensive patients**, but only after weeks of regular practice (book pp. 111–118 per Wikipedia; Benson's 1974 *Lancet* and *J Chronic Dis* papers).
  - It is explicitly a **wakeful** state, not sleep. The 1971 paper is titled "A wakeful hypometabolic physiologic state".
- **The four elements** (Kirkus; PBS):
  1. a quiet environment;
  2. a mental device, meaning a word, sound or phrase repeated to keep the mind from wandering;
  3. a passive attitude;
  4. a comfortable position.
  The lab version lists "decreased muscle tonus" in place of the comfortable position (Beary & Benson 1974).
- **One mechanism under all methods.** The same response is said to underlie meditation and prayer "throughout various religious traditions" (Wikipedia's summary; Kirkus: TM and "the religious practices studied by Dr. Benson") as well as Western relaxation and hypnosis. So you need no particular tradition, teacher or fee. Benson presents the book as a scientific check on old wisdom, not an innovation.
- **The passive attitude.** Don't worry about whether it is working; let distractions go and return to the word.
- **Later simplification** (not in the 1975 book): Benson's institute now names only **two essential steps**: repeat a sound, word, phrase, prayer or movement, and passively set aside intruding thoughts and return to it. It lists yoga, tai chi, progressive muscle relaxation, jogging and even knitting as ways to do this.
- **Later stress on belief:** in a 1998 interview Benson tied healing to belief and "remembered wellness" (his name for the placebo effect), and encouraged people to use their own faith or other belief.

## The practice as this book teaches it
The following steps are given as the book's in two independent sources (PBS 1998; *Psychology Today* 2013). They match Kirkus's summary of the 1975 text.
1. **Sit** quietly in a comfortable position and close your eyes.
2. **Relax the muscles** deeply, starting at the feet and moving up to the face, and keep them relaxed.
3. **Breathe through the nose** and notice the breath. On every out-breath, say "one" silently. Breathe easily and naturally.
4. **Practise for 10–20 minutes.** You may open your eyes to check the time, but don't use an alarm. Afterwards, sit for a few minutes with your eyes closed, then open; don't stand up straight away.
5. **Don't worry about success.** Let relaxation come at its own pace. When thoughts intrude, don't dwell on them; go back to "one".
6. **Practise once or twice a day**, but not within two hours of a meal, because digestion seems to interfere.

The *Psychology Today* version adds two things that are probably from Benson's later website and are unchecked against 1975: a bracketed line about relaxing the tongue, and a footnote advising a soothing word with no meaning.

**Compared with yoga nidra:**
- The posture is **sitting, not lying**, and the practice is self-guided rather than led by a voice.
- There is **no sankalpa, rotation of consciousness, pairs of opposites or visualisation**.
- The **attention stays on one object** instead of moving.
- The **body sweep is a brief preliminary**, not the main practice.
- The **wording is directive** about procedure ("do not use an alarm") but **invitational** about attitude.
- The **length** is the same as a short yoga nidra.

## What's distinctive
- **The only physiology-first book in the set.** It supplies the vocabulary that later yoga nidra books borrow: fight-or-flight versus the relaxation response, sympathetic activity, hypometabolic. Lusk 2021 frames the body stage as switching on "the relaxation response". Dinsmore-Tuli 2020 puts Benson in the Western relaxation line (pp. 41–42), and Singleton 2005 cites him (see those summaries).
- **The secularising move.** Benson renamed a Hindu-derived meditation as a medical "response" and replaced the Sanskrit mantra with "one". This is the 1975 template for what NSDR later did to yoga nidra (`08` debate 9). Kirkus noted the debt: the steps owe "much to Transcendental Meditation and the religious practices" Benson studied.
- **A direct challenge to any claim that yoga nidra is physiologically special.** If all methods converge on one response, yoga nidra's case has to rest on something other than its physiology.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Kirkus (1975) found Benson "careful in his claims", with lab work "documented and well explained".
  - The method is free, short, secular and easy to teach, and it rests on Benson's own peer-reviewed studies (1971–78).
  - It has been widely recommended by clinicians. Wikipedia cites a NYT report (1989) that it topped psychologists' self-help recommendations (I could not open the article).
  - Goodreads: 3.82 from 2,205 ratings, i.e. well liked but dated.
- **Limitations and critiques:**
  - **1970s data.** Samples were small, many subjects were already TM meditators, and most measures were taken during single sessions.
  - **The one-mechanism thesis is contested:**
    - *Meditation versus rest:* Holmes and colleagues (1983) found experienced TM meditators no less aroused while meditating than untrained people simply resting. Holmes's 1984 *American Psychologist* review is the standard reference for this view (I saw the record only).
    - *Specific effects:* Lehrer, Carr, Sargunaraj & Woolfolk (1994) reviewed the comparisons. Muscle-focused methods have mainly muscular effects, autonomic methods autonomic effects, and cognitive methods cognitive effects, and conditions respond best to the matching method.
  - **Benson's own data cut both ways:**
    - Sitting quietly did not produce the drop in oxygen use (Beary & Benson 1974).
    - In a workplace trial, relaxation-response breaks gave the largest gains and plain sitting breaks gave intermediate ones. The abstract reports significance only for relaxation response versus no instruction (Peters, Benson & Porter 1977).
    - For anxiety, meditation and self-hypnosis did equally well; how hypnotisable a patient was mattered more than which technique they used (Benson et al. 1978).
  - **The medical framing** is mostly about hypertension and broad stress-and-disease claims, which have dated.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **Benson's thesis as your honest positioning.** At the level of physiology, yoga nidra is one well-built way to produce a general relaxation response. The 2025–26 meta-analyses find it about as good as other active methods (`06` §6.0), which is what Benson's thesis predicts. Then make yoga nidra's case on other grounds:
    - lying down and being guided suits beginners and people who cannot sit;
    - the moving sequence trains attention and interoception;
    - it explores the hypnagogic edge;
    - the sankalpa gives it meaning;
    - it has contemplative aims (the witness, the state).
    These are claims about experience and use, not about a different physiology.
  - **The markers for your "what happens in the body" box.** Name the relaxation response and Benson 1975, then use the measures yoga nidra trials actually report: heart rate, blood pressure, heart-rate variability (Ghai & Ghai 2025; Markil et al. 2012, a parasympathetic shift after yoga nidra, n = 20) and cortisol.
  - **"Passive attitude"** as the plain, clinically tested name for the witness or "don't try" stance (`08` debates 3 and 5). "Let relaxation come at its own pace" is a model of invitational wording.
  - **The two-step test for each stage of a script:** is there something to come back to, and permission to let distractions go?
  - **Practical details:** don't practise straight after a meal (Benson's observation, not established fact), avoid an alarm, and come out slowly.
  - **History:** one sentence placing Benson (1975) between Boyes (1973) and Satyananda (1976), all three drawing on the same mid-century relaxation culture (`01` §1.2; Singleton 2005).
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **"All relaxation methods share one mechanism" stated as fact.** Call it Benson's hypothesis and cite Lehrer et al. 1994 for specific effects. Soften the compendium wording to match: the `02` §F row ("The mechanism all relaxation methods share") and `06` §6.4.
  - **"A hypometabolic state deeper than sleep".** Holmes's resting controls undercut it. PBS paraphrases the early finding as meditators' oxygen use falling to the level of four to five hours of sleep. That comparison may be one seed of the "1 hour of yoga nidra = 4 hours of sleep" lore (`06` §6.3). This is my hypothesis: check Wallace & Benson 1972 (*Scientific American*) before you say it.
  - **Broad disease claims** (most doctor visits are stress-related; preventing heart disease). NCCIH now rates the evidence for relaxation techniques mostly low to very low (`06` §6.5).
  - **Posture as a rule.** Sitting guards against sleep and lying invites it. Use the contrast for the "is falling asleep failure?" discussion (`08` debate 3), not to rank the methods.
  - **Appropriation.** If you criticise NSDR for stripping yoga nidra of its roots, be consistent: Benson did the same to TM in 1975, though he credited his sources.
