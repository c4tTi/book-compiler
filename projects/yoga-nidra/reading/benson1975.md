## In one paragraph
A Harvard cardiologist's popular book, written with Miriam Klipper and published a year before Satyananda's *Yoga Nidra* (1976). As reported by Kirkus, PBS and Wikipedia, it argues that:
- modern life repeatedly triggers the fight-or-flight reaction, which feeds high blood pressure and heart disease;
- the body has an innate opposite, the **relaxation response**: a wakeful state of lowered metabolism, first measured in TM meditators;
- meditation and prayer across religious traditions produce this **same** response (Wikipedia; Kirkus names TM and "the religious practices studied"). Benson's papers and institute later extend this to secular methods; whether the 1975 book discusses autogenic training (Schultz), progressive relaxation (Jacobson) or hypnosis is unchecked;
- anyone can produce it with a simple, free, non-religious technique: sit, relax, and repeat "one" silently on each out-breath for 10–20 minutes.

It became a long-running bestseller (the publisher claims over six million copies).

**How much concerns yoga nidra: none directly.** Per the Google Books "common terms" list for the 1975 edition, as seen at drafting (not re-checkable: Google Books later returned a CAPTCHA), the list included "Yoga", "Yogi", "Transcendental Meditation", "prayer", "Taoism" and "sleep", but not "nidra"; I could not search the text. The useful background is the technique, the physiology and the survey of traditions.

## Core ideas
- **Fight-or-flight versus its counterpart.** The book contrasts the fight-or-flight reaction (the term appeared in Google Books' term list as seen at drafting; the Cannon attribution is from the 2010 Harvard Health blog) with its opposite. Benson's papers call the relaxation response "an integrated hypothalamic response" mirroring the "emergency reaction" (Benson et al. 1975, abstract), working by lowered sympathetic activity.
- **The physiological markers:**
  - Falls in oxygen consumption, carbon-dioxide output and breathing rate (Beary & Benson 1974, abstract); blood lactate (PBS); heart rate, metabolism and "brain activity" (Benson-Henry Institute, secondary).
  - "Brain activity" is the institute's later wording; the 1971–75 papers measured oxygen use, CO2, respiration, lactate and blood pressure. For EEG claims about yoga nidra, see `06` §6.1 (EEG, flagged ⚠️). Do not borrow Benson to support brainwave claims.
  - Lower blood pressure in hypertensive patients after weeks of practice (book pp. 111–118 per Wikipedia; the underlying 1974 papers themselves were not checked).
  - A **wakeful** state, not sleep: the 1971 paper is titled "A wakeful hypometabolic physiologic state".
- **The four elements** (Kirkus; PBS): a quiet environment; a mental device (a repeated word or sound); a passive attitude; a comfortable position. The lab version has "decreased muscle tonus" instead of a comfortable position (Beary & Benson 1974).
- **One mechanism under the religious methods.** So no particular tradition, teacher or fee is needed.
- **The passive attitude.** Don't worry whether it works; let distractions go and return to the word.
- **Later developments** (not the 1975 book): the institute now names **two essential steps** (repeat a sound, word, phrase, prayer or movement; passively set aside intruding thoughts) and lists yoga, tai chi, progressive muscle relaxation, jogging and knitting as ways to do it. In a 1998 PBS interview Benson tied healing to belief and "remembered wellness".

## The practice as this book teaches it
These steps are given as the book's in two independent sources (PBS 1998; *Psychology Today* 2013). They agree with Kirkus's four elements and 10–20-minute duration; Kirkus says twice a day and does not give the individual steps.
1. **Sit** quietly in a comfortable position; close your eyes.
2. **Relax the muscles** deeply, from the feet up to the face, and keep them relaxed.
3. **Breathe through the nose** and notice the breath; on each out-breath say "one" silently.
4. **Practise 10–20 minutes.** You may open your eyes to check the time but don't use an alarm. Afterwards sit a few minutes, eyes closed then open; don't stand straight away.
5. **Don't worry about success.** Let relaxation come at its own pace; when thoughts intrude, return to "one".
6. **Practise once or twice a day** (PBS, *Psychology Today*), not within two hours of a meal.

The *Psychology Today* version adds a line about relaxing the tongue and a footnote recommending a meaningless soothing word; probably later material, unchecked against 1975.

**Compared with yoga nidra:** sitting, not lying; self-guided, not led; no sankalpa, rotation of consciousness, opposites or visualisation; attention stays on one object; the body sweep is only a brief preliminary; wording is directive about procedure but invitational about attitude; length is that of a short yoga nidra.

## What's distinctive
- **The only physiology-first book in the set.** It supplies vocabulary later yoga nidra books borrow (fight-or-flight versus relaxation response, sympathetic activity, hypometabolic). Lusk 2021 frames the body stage as switching on "the relaxation response"; Dinsmore-Tuli 2020 puts Benson in the Western relaxation line (pp. 41–42); Singleton 2005 cites him (reference list only; see `reading/singleton2010.md`).
- **The secularising move.** Benson renamed a Hindu-derived meditation as a medical "response" and replaced the mantra with "one": the 1975 template for what NSDR later did to yoga nidra (`08` debate 9). Kirkus noted the steps owe "much to Transcendental Meditation and the religious practices" Benson studied.
- **A challenge to any claim that yoga nidra is physiologically special.** If methods converge on one response, yoga nidra's case must rest on something else.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:** Kirkus found Benson "careful in his claims", the lab work "documented and well explained". The method is free, short, secular and easy to teach, and rests on Benson's peer-reviewed studies (1971–78).
- **Limitations and critiques:**
  - **1970s data:** many subjects already TM meditators; the early physiology studies (1971–74) were small and largely single-session (later trials ran longer: Peters et al. 1977, 12 weeks, n = 126; Benson et al. 1978, 8 weeks).
  - **The one-mechanism thesis is contested.** Holmes et al. (1983) found experienced TM meditators no less aroused while meditating than untrained people resting (Holmes 1984 reviews this literature; record only). Lehrer et al. (1994) found method-specific effects: muscular methods act mainly on muscles, autonomic on autonomic measures, cognitive on cognition.
  - **Benson's own data cut both ways:** sitting quietly did not lower oxygen use (Beary & Benson 1974); in a workplace trial plain sitting breaks gave intermediate gains (Peters et al. 1977); for anxiety, meditation and self-hypnosis did equally well, and hypnotic responsivity, not technique, predicted improvement on psychiatric assessment and systolic BP, with no consistent relation to the other measures (Benson et al. 1978).
  - **The medical framing** (hypertension, broad stress-and-disease claims) has dated.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **Benson's thesis as honest positioning.** Physiologically, yoga nidra is one good way to a general relaxation response (about as good as other active methods, `06` §6.0); make its case on other grounds: guidance lying down, attention training, the hypnagogic edge, sankalpa, contemplative aims.
  - **Markers for a "what happens in the body" box:** name Benson 1975, then use what yoga nidra trials report: heart rate, blood pressure, HRV (Markil et al. 2012, parasympathetic shift, n = 20) and cortisol.
  - **"Passive attitude"** as the clinically tested name for the witness or "don't try" stance (`08` debates 3 and 5).
  - **A two-step test for each script stage:** something to come back to, and permission to let distractions go.
  - **Practical details:** not straight after a meal (Benson's observation), no alarm, come out slowly.
  - **History:** one sentence placing Benson (1975) between Boyes (1973) and Satyananda (1976) (`01` §1.2; Singleton 2005, reference list only, see `reading/singleton2010.md`).
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **"All relaxation methods share one mechanism" as fact.** Call it Benson's hypothesis; cite Lehrer et al. 1994. Soften `02` §F ("The mechanism all relaxation methods share") and `06` §6.4 to match.
  - **"Deeper than sleep".** Holmes's resting controls undercut it. PBS paraphrases the early finding as oxygen use falling to the level of four to five hours of sleep. `06` §6.3 currently traces the "1 hour = 4 hours of sleep" claim to lineage lore (akhandjyoti1983); the Wallace & Benson oxygen-use comparison is an unverified candidate earlier source, so do not add it to `06` until the 1972 *Scientific American* text is checked (to-do for §6.3).
  - **Broad disease claims.** NCCIH rates relaxation-technique evidence mostly low to very low (`06` §6.5).
  - **Posture as a rule.** Sitting guards against sleep; lying invites it. Use the contrast in the "is falling asleep failure?" discussion (`08` debate 3).
