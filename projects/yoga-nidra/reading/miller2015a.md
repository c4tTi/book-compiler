## In one paragraph
This is a self-help workbook for people living with post-traumatic stress, written by the clinical psychologist who founded iRest. It turns his yoga nidra protocol into "ten tools", each taught on its own with scripted practices and then combined into a full session. Per Weintraub, it speaks directly to the reader in a non-judging, compassionate teacher's voice and assumes no background in meditation or yoga. Its central promise, in the publisher's words, is a "proven-effective" ten-step programme that eases PTSD symptoms (anxiety, insomnia, depression) by reconnecting the reader with an "underlying sense of calm that is always present". The publisher's description and blurbs frame it with military hospitals, the Army Surgeon General and the Defense Centers of Excellence, and Walter Reed appears in the foreword-writer's bio and in Robin Carnes's blurb; how far the book's own text tells the Walter Reed story is unverified. In my judgement from the sources (I have not read Miller's other books here), it is apparently the most clinically framed of his yoga nidra titles. Several Goodreads reviewers are yoga or iRest teachers who use or recommend it.

## Core ideas
- **Ten tools, usable alone or together.** Each step is a self-contained skill. You can use one step for a specific problem (Weintraub's example is insomnia) or chain all ten for the full effect of "experiencing your wholeness". The Institute's current guidance is to include the first three steps in every session; for beginners the first five are "usually sufficient". I have not confirmed that the book says this.
- **Wholeness is already present.** Healing is described as recognising a calm, whole, well background that symptoms do not damage. This is the non-dual premise of Miller's 2005 book, restated in secular language ("joy is always present", see Quotes).
- **Welcome rather than fix.** Sensations, emotions and thoughts are met, located and welcomed before anyone tries to change them. Miller's definition of bodysensing (see Quotes) is the template for every layer. Weintraub's reading: welcoming the negative first is what lets self-talk shift.
- **Opposites as a regulation tool.** For emotions *and* for thoughts and beliefs, you find the opposite, move between the two, and hold both. Track titles also mention "continuums" of opposites.
- **The Inner Resource is a safe base.** It is a felt sense of security and well-being, set up before going deeper and available at any time. Miller presents it as a way to titrate: ground in it, "take a time out", then come back to the difficult material (Yoga U Online, 2014). The interview article (in the writer's narration, not Miller's words) contrasts this with "psychologically draining" exposure therapy.
- **Resolve split into three.** The Institute describes iRest as breaking the classical sankalpa into **heartfelt desire** (in the book's audio, **"Heartfelt Mission"**), **intention** and **Inner Resource**.
- **Bottom-up and paced.** Healing comes "through the body". If you push a traumatised person into body sensation too quickly, they may be overwhelmed and drop out (Elephant Journal interview, 2012).
- **Messengers.** In Miller's wider iRest teaching, sensations, emotions and thoughts are "messengers" that point to actions you need to take (Yoga Journal column reposted by the Institute). A "Messenger Meditation" (track 02) is among the audio's introductory practices. The book's exact wording is unverified.
- **Ownership by the practitioner.** Per Weintraub, readers may record the scripts themselves and re-word them.
- **Little and often.** Even a few minutes a day is presented as worthwhile (Weintraub). The companion audio offers complete practices in 8-, 20- and 35-minute versions.

- **Length:** Practices for single components run from about 4 to 26 minutes. Complete sessions come in **8, 20 and 35 minutes**. This matches compendium 04's "clinical protocols 20–35 min". The 2007 Walter Reed classes lasted 75–90 minutes (poster). Participants also practised at home from CDs.
- **Posture, eyes open or closed:** **not documented for the book itself.** [companion] The related recordings say "Sit or lie down" with "eyes gently open or closed" (BK06370-09, whose 14:31, measured from the Sounds True MP3 per `miller2015b.md`, is close to, not identical with, the book set's 14:48 track) and "lie or sit" (BK06370-11); see also `miller2005.md` and `miller2015b.md`. Compendium 07 cites this book for "eyes open allowed; seated option; external anchors; skip opposites involving pain/grief". I found no source confirming that these adaptations appear *in this book*. The Institute's later bodysensing article says practice may start with eyes closed and "ultimately" be done with eyes open. Verify in the book.
- **Sankalpa:** split into Mission/Desire, Intention and Inner Resource, and set at the start. iRest Institute material says the heartfelt desire is invited back near the end. The book's audio uses "Heartfelt Mission"; the Institute offers Mission, Purpose and Core Value as variants for people who cannot relate to "desire" (blog 2021). That "mission" suits a military readership is my inference; the word also appears in Miller's general-audience recordings (`miller2015b.md`).
- **Language style:** per Weintraub, a first-person teacher's voice speaks directly to the reader, with no kośa vocabulary and no philosophical jargon. [companion] The related recordings are invitational and permission-giving ("when it feels right", "if it's helpful", "at your own pace"), with a few firm directives ("Give up thinking").
- **How a stage is guided ([companion] "Welcoming Feelings and Emotions", Sounds True transcript BK06370-10; 18:16 (duration measured from the Sounds True MP3, per `miller2015b.md`), against the book set's 18:17 track of the same title, so very probably the same recording, but not verified as the book's printed wording):**
  1. Open the senses to the room; release unneeded tension.
  2. Feel the Inner Resource; then welcome whatever feelings are present (warm/cool, heavy/light).
  3. Move between a physical opposite pair, then hold both; then alternate each pole with the Inner Resource, then hold all three.
  4. Welcome an emotion, or recall one; the script offers recalling "a memory that invites this emotion more fully". Locate it in the body, imagine it entering through a door and taking a shape, and ask what it wants, needs and asks you to do.
  5. Repeat the opposites-and-resource weaving with the opposite emotion.
  6. Open and close the eyes several times; affirm that ease goes with you; journal any actions as intentions.
  
  A refrain recurs: "you can always return to the safe haven of your inner resource whenever you feel the need to take a momentary timeout" (transcript p. 1). The Institute's own description of the Inner Resource step is an embodied felt sense of safety, optionally cued by a remembered place, person or experience, that you can return to whenever overwhelmed; that is the Institute's wording, not the book's.
- **Sleep:** The book seems to treat sleep as a *goal* for some practices: there is a separate "iRest for Sleep" practice and a 12-minute nap. Weintraub says single tools can be used for insomnia. I found no source that says what the book tells readers who fall asleep during an awake practice. In Miller 2005 the position is that awareness is present even in sleep (compendium 03 §3.11). Verify.
- **Compared with Miller 2005** (per `miller2005.md`; I have not read that book here): the order from gross to subtle is the same, mapping to the kośas: body → breath/energy → feelings → thoughts/beliefs → joy → awareness. But **the kośa names are gone** (Weintraub). New up front is the **three-part opening of Mission, Intention and Inner Resource**. The Inner Resource is absent from the 2005 CD, present by Miller's 2011 interview and central in the 2022 edition (`miller2005.md`); it was already in use at Walter Reed by about 2006–07, where a feasibility-study participant credits it with calming them. Step names drift across iRest publications ("Experiencing Your Wholeness" here, "Reflect on Your Practice" on today's Institute page, "Integration" in compendium 04), so cite the edition you use.

## What's distinctive
- **Written to the trauma survivor as a self-help reader.** It includes a PTSD self-test, a programme built step by step, and writing exercises (Weintraub). To my knowledge it is Miller's only trauma workbook; his other titles also address general self-help readers.
- **Teaches the parts before the whole.** Each stage is learned as a stand-alone practice and assembled into full sessions only later. Shared with miller2015b; unusual on the rest of the shelf. Satyananda's book, by contrast, gives complete scripts.
- **Openly folds Western relaxation into yoga nidra.** The audio includes Progressive Muscle Relaxation and a "Body Sensing with Autogenics" practice. Shared with miller2015b; most yoga nidra books leave these roots unnamed (see Singleton 2005 in compendium 02).
- **Applies the opposites to thoughts and beliefs**, not only to sensations. "Welcoming joy *with* stress" is a separate practice.
- **An institutional, military frame in the packaging.** The foreword is by a former Army Surgeon General, the audio uses mission language, and the publisher's materials invoke military hospitals, the Surgeon General and DCoE.
- **Full sessions in three lengths (8, 20, 35 min)**, plus separate sleep and nap practices and daily-life practices (gratitude, day's review).
- **Against Miller's other books and the shelf:**
  - *miller2015b* (*iRest Meditation*, Sounds True audio course, about 7 hours) shares about eight identical track titles with this book's audio, but it has no printed text, no workbook or self-test, and no sleep or nap tracks. *miller2005* keeps the kośa framing; its 2005 audio has no Inner Resource.
  - So this is, as far as I can tell, Miller's only print book reported to carry dozens of scripted practices, and his only trauma workbook.
  - *Lusk 2015* is the same publisher and year, also a secular workbook, but framed around stress, and none of its available descriptions mention an Inner Resource or safe-place step (`lusk2015.md`).
  - *Moore 2019*'s ten steps closely follow iRest (he trained in iRest first), and *Brody 2017*'s author trained to iRest Level 2 (per the 2017 Sounds True interview introduction; her iRest teacher is not named in the sources read) (`moore2019.md`, `brody2017.md`).
  - **Which to get:** this book if you want text and scripts; miller2015b if you want to hear Miller's guiding voice.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Accessible, jargon-free writing that "directly meets readers experiencing PTSD" (Weintraub, who also credits the developmental editor, Jami Macarty).
  - A clear, modular structure. Goodreads average 4.29 from 163 ratings (15 text reviews; checked 3 Oct 2026). Several Goodreads reviewers are teachers; one (a Miller student) calls it a daily reference for practice and teaching, another recommends it to students. One singles out its explanation of how to choose a sankalpa; others mention its handling of opposites.
  - The Inner Resource gives traumatised readers an exit and a base to return to.
  - Readers have explicit permission to adapt the scripts.
  - Usable by people without PTSD. One reader, who says it will shape their teaching, felt the "PTSD" title puts off people who would benefit.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **The evidence is oversold in the subtitle and blurb.** "Proven-effective" and "extensive research" rested, at publication, on small uncontrolled studies:
    - Walter Reed feasibility study, 2007: n = 7 active-duty personnel, 9 weeks, no control group. The authors state the sample was too small for inferential analysis and report only a trend in PCL scores, with 43% home-practice compliance and a 14% dropout rate. ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00269490 (Samueli Institute; Walter Reed; observational; 7 enrolled; 2005–2007) is very probably its registration.
    - Stankovic 2011: 16 veterans enrolled, 11 completed, qualitative.
    - Pence 2014: 15 enrolled, 10 completed, pre/post only.
    - Cramer et al.'s 2018 meta-analysis of yoga for PTSD included **no** iRest trial among its 7 RCTs. It gave yoga as a whole only a weak recommendation as an *adjunct*.
    - As of October 2026, ClinicalTrials.gov lists one randomized iRest trial on post-traumatic symptoms: NCT06888336 ("Yoga Nidra for Insomnia and Posttraumatic Stress Symptoms", 3-arm feasibility study, n = 60; started March 2025, completed March 2026; no results posted).
    - **Registry count:** compendium `06` §6.5's "27 on iRest" comes from a raw keyword search. On re-running it (3 Oct 2026), 27 hits came back, but most are unrelated eye, lens and vision-rehabilitation trials that use "iRest" as a device or product word. Only three of the 27 concern iRest yoga nidra (NCT06888336; NCT02180243, VA, Gulf War illness, iRest plus acupuncture; NCT04773743, back pain). The exact phrase "integrative restoration" returns two (NCT06888336, NCT04773743). With NCT00269490 (filed under "yoga nidra"), that makes about four registrations of Miller's method, not 27.
    - The Institute's trauma page also calls iRest "evidence-based" and says its steps were "tested in clinical trials", but it notes that the Defense Centers of Excellence recognise iRest as complementary medicine "warranting continuing research". How the book itself summarises the research is unknown (not read).
  - **Endorsements are close to the programme.** The *Elephant Journal* interview introduction (2012) reports that the 2010 Army Surgeon General endorsement was of yoga nidra *for chronic pain*. It was made by Eric Schoomaker, who writes this book's foreword. Robin Carnes, who endorses the book on the publisher page, is a co-author of the Walter Reed feasibility study. Weintraub's review is by a fellow yoga-therapy programme founder, and she asserts, in her own voice, that the practice has been in use for 4,500 years.
  - **Antiquity.** Miller's own iRest material calls yoga nidra a "4,500-year-old approach" (Sounds True intro transcript BK06370-07, p. 2; see `miller2015b.md`). Whether this book says so is unverified. Historians do not support it; see `08` §1 [@birch2015; @singleton2005].
  - **A universal promise.** Per Ridder's review, Miller promises benefit regardless of the reader's background or how long ago the trauma happened. That is a strong claim for a self-help book.
  - **Sleep promise.** The blurb promises relief from sleeplessness. For yoga nidra in general, Singh et al. 2026 found pooled RCT effects on insomnia that were not statistically significant, with very low certainty (`06` §6.0 [@singh2026]). Whether the book repeats the "1 hour = 4 hours of sleep" claim (`06` §6.3) is unknown; it was not found in searches of miller2005 (`miller2005.md`).
  - **Brain language.** The companion track "Experiencing Your Focusing and Defocusing Network" and the 2014 interview article's claim that the hippocampus "thickens and grows" with symptom management (writer's narration) are best read as metaphor, not a demonstrated mechanism of iRest. The best default-mode-network study in `06` §6.1 found decoupling only in experienced meditators, not novices [@fialoke2024].
  - **A self-regulation toolkit, not a recovery model.** One Goodreads reader, who has used similar techniques for years, found Judith Herman's *Trauma and Recovery* more useful as a map of long-term recovery.
  - Readers describe the writing as **repetitive**, and one mentions weak grammar. Another finds the 10 steps hard to follow without prior experience of yoga nidra scripts.
  - **Most of the audio is sold separately.** The practices are meant to be heard. Reviewers report the book links to some online recordings (Weintraub; Ridder: irest.us/practices; one Goodreads reader counts 8), but the full set of 42 is sold separately ($49.99 from the Institute).
  - **Safety in unsupervised use.** Later trauma-informed work reports flashbacks, emotional distress and prolonged dissociation after yoga nidra (Luu 2024). The related emotion recording invites the listener to recall a memory that intensifies the emotion [companion]. What screening and stop-rules the book gives a lone reader needs checking.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The three-part opening, with the Inner Resource *before* any depth.** Teach it first as a stand-alone skill, then name it as the exit point throughout ("you can return here at any time"). This is the single most useful trauma-sensitive device in the modern literature (compendium 03 §3.10). Credit it to Miller and iRest.
  - **Modular design.** Each stage can be practised on its own. Offer complete sessions at a few fixed lengths (for example 10, 20 and 35 min). This answers the choice in compendium 08 debate 7 between "one core practice or a collection".
  - **Little and often** as the practice instruction, rather than long weekly sessions.
  - **Opposites extended to thoughts and beliefs,** and practised with the Inner Resource as an anchor ("weaving"). It is a gentler, more regulated version of Satyananda's opposites. **Only after the Inner Resource is established;** give explicit permission to stop, open the eyes or move; never prompt trauma memories in self-study; start with physical opposites (warm/cool) before emotional ones (`07` §7.1–7.2; Luu 2024).
  - **Vocabulary chosen for your reader.** The book's audio uses "Heartfelt Mission"; the Institute offers Mission, Purpose and Core Value as variants for those who cannot relate to "desire" (iRest blog 2021). The military fit is my inference. Offer readers alternatives instead of imposing "sankalpa".
  - **Permission to rephrase and record scripts in their own voice.** Pair it with a clear note on whether teachers may read your scripts aloud.
  - **Separate sleep practices from awake practices.** This takes the guilt out of "I keep falling asleep" (debate 3 in compendium 08).
  - **Honesty about Western relaxation roots.** That PMR and autogenics appear in yoga nidra recordings is evidence for your history chapter.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Don't repeat "proven-effective" or "endorsed by the Surgeon General".** Say instead: studied in small pilot studies in military and VA settings, promising but not established, and used *alongside* treatment, not instead of it (compendium 06 §6.3). If you mention the endorsement, say it concerned chronic pain.
  - **"4,500 years old".** Say "ancient roots, modern method" (`08` §1).
  - **Don't present your book as PTSD treatment.** Include screening, stop-rules and "see a therapist" guidance (compendium 07).
  - **Don't reproduce iRest's ten-step sequence as your own method.** iRest is a registered trademark and the protocol is the Institute's teaching product. Describe it with attribution, and use generic names for your own stages.
  - **Watch the non-dual end-point hidden inside "secular" wording.** "Who am I?" and "I Am" practices are Advaita self-inquiry. Be open about your own framing rather than calling it neutral.
  - **Miller's sankalpa etymology.** In the 2012 interview he glosses *san* as "born from the heart". That is not the standard derivation (*sam* + *√kḷp*, "to form, to resolve"). Check whether the book repeats it, and don't copy it.
  - **Brain-change and brain-network claims** (hippocampus, focusing/defocusing networks) used to promote the method; treat them as metaphor (`06` §6.1).
- **If you borrow it (Libby/OverDrive):** check (1) the table of contents; (2) the Inner Resource chapter; (3) the emotions and thoughts chapters, for memory prompts and stop-rules; (4) the research chapter, for how the n = 7/16/15 studies are presented; (5) any passages on posture, eyes open, sitting and falling asleep; (6) whether the heartfelt desire/mission returns at the end of the scripts; (7) any "4,500 years" or sleep-equivalence claims.
