# The iRest Program for Healing PTSD: A Proven-Effective Approach to Using Yoga Nidra Meditation and Deep Relaxation Techniques to Overcome Trauma — Richard C. Miller (2015)

**Bottom line for your book:** *Take* the Inner Resource (in the book, tool #3, set up with the heartfelt mission and intention before any body work) and the modular, tool-by-tool design that ends in one complete practice. *Skip* "proven-effective", the "4,500+ years" claim (it is in the book), the "you can heal your PTSD" promise, and the trademarked ten-tool sequence as your own method. *Note* what the opening chapters do well: a PTSD self-checklist followed by an instruction to get a professional assessment, and a research section that calls iRest *complementary*. What stop-rules the practice chapters (3–12) give a reader practising alone is still unverified.

## In one paragraph
A self-help workbook for people living with post-traumatic stress, written by the clinical psychologist who founded iRest. Chapter 1 explains PTSD and gives a self-test. Chapter 2 introduces yoga nidra and iRest, summarises research and the military's adoption, and lists ten "tools for life". Chapters 3–12 teach the tools one at a time with scripted practices and then combine them into one complete practice. In the chapters I read, the voice speaks directly to "you", is warm and affirmative, and assumes no background in meditation or yoga (Weintraub says the same of the whole book). The central promise is strong. Chapter 2 opens by telling readers they can heal their PTSD whatever their background and however long ago the trauma, and asks them to repeat "I can heal my PTSD" as an intention. The framing is military and clinical throughout the opening: a foreword by a former Army Surgeon General, the Walter Reed origin story of the name iRest, a section headed "Recommended by the Military", and case vignettes of veterans and civilian survivors. The same pages also tell readers who suspect PTSD to get a professional assessment, and present iRest as a complementary intervention alongside other treatment. Several Goodreads reviewers are yoga or iRest teachers who use or recommend it.

## Core ideas
- **Ten tools, usable alone or together** (chapter 2, read). Miller calls the parts of the programme "tools for life", a tool belt to use one at a time or as the whole protocol. The introduction says each tool builds on the earlier ones. The Institute's current guidance is to include the first three in every session, and for beginners the first five are "usually sufficient". I have not seen that guidance in the book.
- **Something's wrong vs. something's wrong with me** (chapter 1, read). Miller's account of trauma: the nervous system's defences are natural, but the ego personalises them, turning a non-personal signal ("something's wrong") into a self-judgement ("something's wrong with me"). This framing sets up the later practices of welcoming sensations, emotions and thoughts as non-personal.
- **Wholeness is already present.** Chapter 3 is titled "Healing in Wholeness" and chapter 11 "Experiencing Your Wholeness" (TOC). Per Weintraub, joy is presented as always available, even in difficult moments. This is the non-dual premise of Miller's 2005 book in secular language.
- **Intentions stated as fact** (chapter 2, read). Miller calls "You can heal your PTSD" a statement of fact, asks the reader to repeat "I can heal my PTSD", and says the negative opposite belief will come up in response, to be met with the later tools.
- **Welcome rather than fix.** Sensations, emotions and thoughts are met, located and welcomed before anyone tries to change them. Miller's definition of bodysensing (see Quotes, via Ridder) is the template. Chapter 12 has a section on welcoming as the "perfect response" to each moment (TOC).
- **Opposites.** The 1970 class that started it all worked through pairs of opposites until he could hold them without aversion or attachment (introduction). Chapters 7 and 8 apply opposites to emotions and to thoughts (TOC).
- **The resolve split into three** (TOC, chapter 4): heartfelt **mission**, **intention**, and **Inner Resource**, taught together under "Affirming Your Resolutions" before the body tools. The book's term is "Heartfelt Mission"; the Institute today says "heartfelt desire".
- **The Inner Resource as a safe base.** It is tool #3 in the book. What the book says about it I have not read. From Miller's interviews: a felt sense of security and well-being to "take a time out" in, then come back to the difficult material (Yoga U Online, 2014).
- **Messengers.** Chapter 3 has "Your Messengers Within" and chapter 12 "Welcome Every Messenger" (TOC). In Miller's wider teaching, sensations, emotions and thoughts are messengers pointing to actions (Yoga Journal column reposted by the Institute). The book's exact treatment is unread.
- **Little and often** (introduction, read). Regular short practice, day by day, is the stated recipe; see Quotes.
- **Practise in every position** (introduction, read). Begin sitting or lying, then practise on the side, on the front, standing, walking, eventually in any position, so that iRest is available whatever life does to you.

- **How to use the scripts (introduction, read).** Miller calls iRest a guided practice at first and says it can later be done without a guide. Each of the 41 practices carries icons for four options: record it in your own voice, have a trusted friend read or record it, use the downloadable recordings, and write answers or reflections in a journal. An ellipsis (…) means pause for several seconds. If a friend is reading, agree that you will raise your hand when you want a pause. If you use a recording, keep a finger on the pause button.
- **Setting and posture (introduction, read).** Practise somewhere quiet and secure where you won't be interrupted. Sit in a comfortable chair, or lie on a bed, couch or floor with a pillow under the head and neck and one under the knees to let the lower back relax. Then practise in other positions too: on the back, side or front, sitting on floor or chair, standing, even walking, and in time any position at all. **Eyes open or closed: not stated in the pages I read.** [companion] The related recordings say "Sit or lie down" with "eyes gently open or closed" (BK06370-09). Compendium 07 cites this book for "eyes open allowed; seated option; external anchors; skip opposites involving pain/grief". The seated option is now confirmed; the others are not confirmed by anything I read.
- **Length:** Single-tool practices on the companion audio run from about 3 to 26 minutes; complete sessions come in **8, 20 and 35 minutes**. Whether the printed scripts state lengths is unknown. The 2007 Walter Reed classes lasted 75–90 minutes (poster).
- **Sankalpa:** split into Heartfelt Mission, Intention and Inner Resource (ch. 4 tool titles). The Institute offers Mission, Purpose and Core Value as alternatives for those who cannot relate to "desire" (blog 2021). That "mission" suits a military readership is my inference; the word also appears in Miller's general-audience recordings (`miller2015b.md`).
- **Language style (chapters 1–2, read):** direct second person, affirmative and encouraging, with short case vignettes (Ted, Mary, Jerry, Mark) and testimonials. Weintraub describes the practice scripts as having a compassionate teacher's voice. [companion] The related recordings are invitational and permission-giving ("when it feels right", "at your own pace"), with a few firm directives.
- **How a stage may be guided ([companion] "Welcoming Feelings and Emotions", Sounds True transcript BK06370-10, 18:16, against the book set's 18:17 track of the same title; very probably the same recording, but not the book's printed wording):**
  1. Open the senses to the room; release unneeded tension.
  2. Feel the Inner Resource; then welcome whatever feelings are present (warm/cool, heavy/light).
  3. Move between a physical opposite pair, then hold both; then alternate each pole with the Inner Resource, then hold all three.
  4. Welcome an emotion, or recall one; the script offers recalling "a memory that invites this emotion more fully". Locate it in the body, imagine it entering through a door and taking a shape, and ask what it wants, needs and asks you to do.
  5. Repeat the opposites-and-resource weaving with the opposite emotion.
  6. Open and close the eyes several times; affirm that ease goes with you; journal any actions as intentions.

  A refrain recurs: you can return to the safe haven of the Inner Resource whenever you need a momentary timeout (transcript p. 1).
- **Sleep:** The book treats sleep as a goal for some practices (ch. 9 sections "Joy Invites Restful Sleep" and "iRest Nap"; the companion audio's "iRest for Sleep"). A chapter 2 testimonial credits iRest with a first good night's sleep since returning from war. What the book tells readers who fall asleep during an awake practice is unknown.
- **Compared with Miller 2005** (per `miller2005.md`; I have not read that book here): the order from gross to subtle is the same and maps to the kośas (body → breath → feelings → thoughts → joy → awareness), but the kośa names are gone. New up front is the **three-part opening of mission, intention and Inner Resource**. The Inner Resource is absent from the 2005 CD, present by Miller's 2011 interview and central in the 2022 edition (`miller2005.md`). Tool names drift across iRest publications ("Experiencing Your Wholeness" here, "Reflect on Your Practice" on today's Institute page, "Integration" in compendium 04), so cite the edition you use.

## What's distinctive
- **Written to the trauma survivor as a self-help reader.** Chapter 1 gives a 27-item checkbox **Quick Self-Test for PTSD**, grouped as arousal (12 items, including the re-experiencing items), numbing (8), avoidance (5) and vigilance (2). Its scoring rule: at least one symptom you have daily, five that distress you, for at least a month, means you are probably experiencing PTSD (citing Weathers et al. 1994). It then tells readers who suspect PTSD to see a qualified doctor or mental health practitioner for an assessment and treatment plan, which can include the iRest programme. To my knowledge this is Miller's only trauma workbook.
- **Teaches the parts before the whole.** Each tool has its own chapter and practices; the complete practice comes only in chapter 12. Shared with miller2015b; unusual on the rest of the shelf. Satyananda's book, by contrast, gives complete scripts.
- **Openly folds Western relaxation into yoga nidra.** Chapter 1 lists autogenics, progressive muscle relaxation and the relaxation response among conventional PTSD treatments, and the companion audio includes PMR and "Body Sensing with Autogenics". Most yoga nidra books leave these roots unnamed (see Singleton 2005 in compendium 02).
- **Applies the opposites to thoughts and beliefs**, not only to sensations (ch. 8).
- **An institutional, military frame.** Foreword by a former Army Surgeon General; the Walter Reed naming story in the introduction; a "Recommended by the Military" section listing VA and military sites; "mission" language; veteran testimonials.
- **Multiple ways to receive a script**: self-recording, a friend reading with an agreed hand signal to pause, or the recordings; plus journal prompts.
- **Practice in any posture**, including standing and walking, as an explicit training goal.
- **Against Miller's other books and the shelf:**
  - *miller2015b* (*iRest Meditation*, Sounds True audio course, about 7 hours) shares about eight identical track titles with this book's audio, but it has no printed text, no workbook or self-test, and no sleep or nap tracks. *miller2005* keeps the kośa framing; its 2005 audio has no Inner Resource.
  - *Lusk 2015* is the same publisher and year, also a secular workbook, but framed around stress, and none of its available descriptions mention an Inner Resource or safe-place step (`lusk2015.md`).
  - *Moore 2019*'s ten steps closely follow iRest (he trained in iRest first), and *Brody 2017*'s author trained to iRest Level 2 (`moore2019.md`, `brody2017.md`).
  - **Which to get:** this book if you want text and scripts; miller2015b if you want to hear Miller's guiding voice.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Accessible, jargon-free writing (Weintraub, who also credits the developmental editor, Jami Macarty, as do Miller's acknowledgments). Chapters 1–2 bear this out.
  - Responsible signposting in the opening chapters: the self-test ends with advice to get a professional assessment, and the research section calls iRest a complementary intervention that supports other methods (citing Moore et al. 2011).
  - A clear, modular structure, visible in the table of contents. Goodreads average 4.29 from 163 ratings (15 text reviews; checked 3 Oct 2026). Several Goodreads reviewers are teachers; one calls it a daily reference for practice and teaching, another recommends it to students. One singles out its explanation of how to choose a sankalpa.
  - Practical delivery advice (pauses at ellipses, a hand signal with a friend reader, a finger on the pause button) that gives the listener control of pacing.
  - Readers are told to record the scripts in their own voice; per Weintraub, they may also re-word them.
  - Usable by people without PTSD. One reader felt the "PTSD" title puts off people who would benefit.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **The evidence is oversold.** The subtitle says "proven-effective"; the introduction says the Walter Reed study was "so successful" and that more than twenty successful studies have been done. At publication the published evidence was small and uncontrolled:
    - Walter Reed feasibility study (the book cites it as Engel et al. 2006; the poster I read is dated 2007): n = 7 active-duty personnel, 9 weeks, no control group. The authors state the sample was too small for inferential analysis and report only a trend in PCL scores, with 43% home-practice compliance and a 14% dropout rate. ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00269490 is very probably its registration.
    - Stankovic 2011: 16 veterans enrolled, 11 completed, qualitative. Pence 2014: 15 enrolled, 10 completed, pre/post only.
    - Cramer et al.'s 2018 meta-analysis of yoga for PTSD included **no** iRest trial among its 7 RCTs and gave yoga only a weak recommendation as an *adjunct*.
    - As of October 2026, ClinicalTrials.gov lists one randomized iRest trial on post-traumatic symptoms: NCT06888336 (3-arm feasibility study, n = 60; completed March 2026; no results posted).
    - **Registry count:** compendium `06` §6.5's "27 on iRest" comes from a raw keyword search; on re-running it (3 Oct 2026), most hits were unrelated eye and vision trials using "iRest" as a product word. About four registrations concern Miller's method (NCT06888336, NCT02180243, NCT04773743, NCT00269490).
    - To be fair, the book's own research section (ch. 2, read) is more measured than its subtitle: it calls iRest complementary and says the Defense Centers of Excellence recommended iRest *for ongoing research* as a PTSD treatment. It reports participant improvements in general terms without sample sizes.
  - **Endorsements are close to the programme.** The foreword says Miller's work and others' led the Army Surgeon General's Pain Management Task Force in 2010 to recommend these practices for *chronic pain*, and that the book "expands that work to PTSD". So the endorsement concerned pain, not PTSD. Chapter 2 likewise says the task force listed yoga nidra for pain management. The foreword's author, Eric Schoomaker, was that Surgeon General. Robin Carnes, who endorses the book on the publisher page, co-authored the Walter Reed study (the acknowledgments thank a "Robin" among the iRest teachers working with veterans; first name only).
  - **Antiquity: in the book.** The introduction calls iRest a name for a "4,500+-year-old approach", and chapter 2 says the ten tools have been developed over more than 4,500 years, adding that they have lasted "because they work". Historians do not support the date; see `08` §1 [@birch2015; @singleton2005].
  - **Etymology.** Chapter 2 glosses *nidrā* as changing states of consciousness (waking, sleeping, dreaming) rather than its ordinary meaning, "sleep". Treat this as Miller's interpretation.
  - **A universal promise.** Chapter 2 tells the reader that iRest can heal their PTSD whatever their background and however long ago the trauma, and calls "You can heal your PTSD" a statement of fact. That is a strong claim for a self-help book, and it sits uneasily with the same chapter's "complementary" framing.
  - **Loose self-diagnosis.** Chapter 1 says you *are* experiencing PTSD if something distressing happened and you have any of a list of common symptoms (trouble sleeping, feeling on guard, avoidance and so on). Its trauma list includes divorce and unemployment. The self-test is a yes/no checklist with a simple rule. As far as I know, the PCL it cites (Weathers et al.) rates 17 items on a severity scale. Its four groups also differ from DSM clusters: re-experiencing items are filed under "arousal". The referral advice that follows softens this, but a teacher should not reuse the checklist as a screening tool.
  - **Brain language.** Chapter 1 says that as you heal PTSD symptoms your brain grows new and healthy cells, and that meditation programmes such as iRest produce healthy changes in brain structure and stimulate tissue repair (citing several studies and Miller 2006). The companion track "Experiencing Your Focusing and Defocusing Network" uses similar language. Read this as encouragement, not a demonstrated mechanism of iRest; the best default-mode-network study in `06` §6.1 found decoupling only in experienced meditators [@fialoke2024].
  - **Sleep promise.** The blurb promises relief from sleeplessness, and chapter 9 links joy and sleep. For yoga nidra in general, Singh et al. 2026 found pooled RCT effects on insomnia that were not statistically significant, with very low certainty (`06` §6.0 [@singh2026]). Whether the book repeats the "1 hour = 4 hours of sleep" claim (`06` §6.3) is unknown; it is not in the chapters I read.
  - **A self-regulation toolkit, not a recovery model.** One Goodreads reader found Judith Herman's *Trauma and Recovery* more useful as a map of long-term recovery.
  - Readers describe the writing as **repetitive**; another finds the ten steps hard to follow without prior experience of yoga nidra scripts.
  - **Most of the audio is sold separately.** The introduction promised eight free streams and the rest for a fee; the free page is now access-protected (Oct 2026), and the set of 42 costs $49.99 from the Institute.
  - **Safety in unsupervised use: partly unknown.** Later trauma-informed work reports flashbacks, emotional distress and prolonged dissociation after yoga nidra (Luu 2024). The related emotion recording invites the listener to recall a memory that intensifies the emotion [companion]. The pacing controls in the introduction (pauses, hand signal, pause button) help, but what stop-rules the emotion and thought chapters give a lone reader is unread.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The three-part opening, with the Inner Resource *before* any depth.** The book teaches mission, intention and Inner Resource together in one chapter before the body tools. Teach the Inner Resource first as a stand-alone skill, then name it as the exit point throughout. This is the single most useful trauma-sensitive device in the modern literature (compendium 03 §3.10). Credit it to Miller and iRest.
  - **Modular design.** One chapter per stage, then one complete practice. Offer complete sessions at a few fixed lengths (for example 10, 20 and 35 min). This answers the choice in compendium 08 debate 7 between "one core practice or a collection".
  - **Little and often** as the practice instruction.
  - **Script-delivery conventions:** mark pauses explicitly (Miller uses an ellipsis for a pause of several seconds), tell readers they can record the script themselves or have a friend read it, and give the listener a way to stop the reader (an agreed hand signal). Pair it with a clear note on whether teachers may read your scripts aloud.
  - **Posture as a progression:** start seated or lying with props (a pillow under head and knees), then practise in other positions, even standing or walking, so the skill travels into daily life.
  - **Opposites extended to thoughts and beliefs,** practised with the Inner Resource as an anchor. **Only after the Inner Resource is established;** give explicit permission to stop, open the eyes or move; never prompt trauma memories in self-study; start with physical opposites (warm/cool) before emotional ones (`07` §7.1–7.2; Luu 2024).
  - **Refer, then offer.** Miller's sequence (self-check, "see a professional", then the practice as part of a treatment plan) is a sound model; use a validated screener or none at all, not his checklist.
  - **Vocabulary chosen for your reader.** The book uses "Heartfelt Mission"; the Institute offers Mission, Purpose and Core Value as alternatives (iRest blog 2021). Offer readers alternatives instead of imposing "sankalpa".
  - **Separate sleep practices from awake practices** (the book puts sleep and the nap in their own sections). This takes the guilt out of "I keep falling asleep" (debate 3 in compendium 08).
  - **Honesty about Western relaxation roots.** Miller himself lists autogenics and PMR as PTSD treatments, and uses both in the audio. That is evidence for your history chapter.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Don't repeat "proven-effective", "more than twenty successful studies" or "endorsed by the Surgeon General".** Say instead: studied in small pilot studies in military and VA settings, promising but not established, and used *alongside* treatment (compendium 06 §6.3). If you mention the 2010 task force, say it concerned chronic pain.
  - **"4,500+ years".** It is in this book; don't copy it. Say "ancient roots, modern method" (`08` §1).
  - **"You can heal your PTSD" as a "statement of fact".** Don't promise healing. Don't present your book as PTSD treatment; include screening, stop-rules and "see a therapist" guidance (compendium 07).
  - **Don't reproduce iRest's ten-tool sequence or tool names as your own method.** iRest is a registered trademark and the protocol is the Institute's teaching product. Describe it with attribution, and use generic names for your own stages.
  - **Watch the non-dual end-point inside "secular" wording.** Chapter 10's "Who, What, Why, When, and Where Am I?" and the "I Am" audio are Advaita-style self-inquiry. Be open about your own framing rather than calling it neutral.
  - **Etymologies.** The book's *nidrā* gloss is interpretive; in the 2012 interview Miller glosses *san* (of *saṅkalpa*) as "born from the heart", which is not the standard derivation (*sam* + *√kḷp*). Whether the book repeats the second is unread.
  - **Brain-growth claims** used to promote the method; treat them as metaphor (`06` §6.1).
- **If you borrow it (Libby/OverDrive):** check (1) the Inner Resource section of chapter 4; (2) chapters 7 and 8, for memory prompts and stop-rules; (3) any eyes-open guidance and what it says about falling asleep; (4) whether the heartfelt mission returns at the end of the scripts, and how "The Complete Practice of iRest" in chapter 12 is sequenced and timed; (5) the writing exercises; (6) any sleep-equivalence claim; (7) whether the book repeats the "born from the heart" sankalpa gloss.
