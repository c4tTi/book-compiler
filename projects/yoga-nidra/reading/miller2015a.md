## In one paragraph
This is a self-help workbook for people living with post-traumatic stress, written by the clinical psychologist who founded iRest. It turns his yoga nidra protocol into "ten tools", each taught on its own with scripted practices and then combined into a full session. It is addressed directly to the reader ("you") in a warm, non-judging teacher's voice, and assumes no background in meditation or yoga (Weintraub). Its central promise, in the publisher's words, is a "proven-effective" ten-step programme that eases PTSD symptoms (anxiety, insomnia, depression) by reconnecting the reader with an "underlying sense of calm that is always present". The book is framed by iRest's institutional story: Walter Reed Army Medical Center, the VA, a foreword by a former Army Surgeon General, and an endorsement from the Defense Centers of Excellence. It is the most clinical and most secular of Miller's yoga nidra books. It is also the one teachers and VA programmes cite for trauma work.

## Core ideas
- **Ten tools, usable alone or together.** Each step is a self-contained skill. You can use one step for a specific problem (Weintraub's example is insomnia) or chain all ten for the full effect of "experiencing your wholeness". The Institute's current guidance is to include the first three steps in every session; for beginners the first five are "usually sufficient". I have not confirmed that the book says this.
- **Wholeness is already present.** Healing is described as recognising a calm, whole, well background that symptoms do not damage. This is the non-dual premise of Miller's 2005 book, restated in secular language ("joy is always present", see Quotes).
- **Welcome rather than fix.** Sensations, emotions and thoughts are met, located and welcomed before anyone tries to change them. Miller's definition of bodysensing (see Quotes) is the template for every layer. Weintraub sums up the claim this way: acknowledging and welcoming the negative first is what lets self-talk shift.
- **Opposites as a regulation tool.** For emotions *and* for thoughts and beliefs, you find the opposite, move between the two, and hold both. Track titles also mention "continuums" of opposites.
- **The Inner Resource is a safe base.** It is a felt sense of security and well-being, set up before going deeper and available at any time. Miller presents it as a way to titrate: ground in it, "take a time out", then come back to the difficult material (Yoga U Online, 2014). He contrasts this with exposure therapy, which he calls draining.
- **Resolve split into three.** The Institute describes iRest as breaking the classical sankalpa into **heartfelt desire** (here apparently called **"Heartfelt Mission"**), **intention** and **Inner Resource**.
- **Bottom-up and paced.** Healing comes "through the body". If you push a traumatised person into body sensation too quickly, they may be overwhelmed and drop out (Elephant Journal interview, 2012).
- **Messengers.** In Miller's wider iRest teaching, sensations, emotions and thoughts are "messengers" that point to actions you need to take (Yoga Journal column reposted by the Institute). A "Messenger Meditation" opens the book's audio. The book's exact wording is unverified.
- **Ownership by the practitioner.** Readers are encouraged to record the scripts in their own voice, with full permission to change the words so they sound natural (Weintraub).
- **Little and often.** Even a few minutes a day is presented as worthwhile. Complete practices come in 8-, 20- and 35-minute versions.

- **Length:** Practices for single components run from about 4 to 26 minutes. Complete sessions come in **8, 20 and 35 minutes**. This matches compendium 04's "clinical protocols 20–35 min". The 2007 Walter Reed classes lasted 75–90 minutes including teaching. Participants also practised at home from CDs.
- **Posture, eyes open or closed, sitting up, permission to move:** **not documented in anything I could read.** Compendium 07 cites this book for "eyes open allowed; seated option; external anchors; skip opposites involving pain/grief". I found no source confirming that these adaptations appear *in this book*. The Institute's later bodysensing article does say practice may start with eyes closed and "ultimately" be done with eyes open. Verify in the book.
- **Sankalpa:** split into Mission/Desire, Intention and Inner Resource, and set at the start. iRest Institute material says the heartfelt desire is invited back near the end. The book's "Mission" wording fits the military readership. Elsewhere the Institute calls it a variant for people who cannot relate to "desire".
- **Language style:** second person, invitational and permission-giving ("welcome …"). A first-person teacher's voice speaks directly to the reader (Weintraub). There is no kośa vocabulary and no philosophical jargon. The book does not use the classic opening "I am going to practise yoga nidra".
- **How a stage is guided (paraphrased from the Institute's description of the Inner Resource step):** Recall a place, a person or a moment in which you felt secure and at ease. Notice where and how that feeling lives in your body. Let it grow. You can come back to it at any point in the practice, or in daily life, whenever an emotion, a memory or a situation becomes too much. Then, in the emotions step, you welcome a difficult feeling, look for its opposite, and move between the two, with the Inner Resource as a home base you can always return to.
- **Sleep:** The book seems to treat sleep as a *goal* for some practices: there is a separate "iRest for Sleep" practice and a 12-minute nap. Weintraub says single tools can be used for insomnia. I found no source that says what the book tells readers who fall asleep during an awake practice. In Miller 2005 the position is that awareness is present even in sleep (compendium 03 §3.11). Verify.
- **Compared with Miller 2005** (structure as the compendium describes it; I have not read that book here): The order from gross to subtle is the same, mapping to the kośas: body → breath/energy → feelings → thoughts/beliefs → joy → awareness. But **the kośa names are gone** (Weintraub). What is new up front is the **three-part opening of Mission, Intention and Inner Resource**. The Inner Resource was already in use at Walter Reed by about 2006–07: in the feasibility study, a participant credits it with calming them. Check whether the 2005 book already has it. The steps are also renamed and reordered to suit a PTSD workbook: "Experiencing Your Wholeness" here, "Reflect on Your Practice" on today's Institute page, "Integration" in compendium 04. Step names drift across iRest publications, so cite the edition you use.

## What's distinctive
- **The only Miller book written to the trauma survivor as a self-help reader** rather than to yoga practitioners or teachers. It includes a PTSD self-test, a programme built step by step, and writing exercises. It is the most secular statement of iRest.
- **Teaches the parts before the whole.** Each stage is learned as a stand-alone practice and assembled into full sessions only later. Satyananda's book, by contrast, gives complete scripts.
- **Openly folds Western relaxation into yoga nidra.** The audio includes Progressive Muscle Relaxation and a "Body Sensing with Autogenics" practice. Most yoga nidra books leave these roots unnamed (see Singleton 2005 in compendium 02).
- **Applies the opposites to thoughts and beliefs**, not only to sensations. "Welcoming joy *with* stress" is a separate practice.
- **An institutional, military frame.** The foreword is by a former Army Surgeon General, the vocabulary is mission language, and the Walter Reed and VA story is told. No other yoga nidra book carries this kind of clinical-institutional legitimacy.
- **Full sessions in three lengths (8, 20, 35 min)**, plus separate sleep and nap practices and daily-life practices (gratitude, day's review).

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Accessible, jargon-free writing that "directly meets readers experiencing PTSD" (Weintraub, who also credits the developmental editor, Jami Macarty).
  - A clear, modular structure that readers and teachers find easy to follow and implement. Several Goodreads reviewers are teachers who use it as a daily reference. One singles out its explanation of how to choose a sankalpa; others mention its handling of opposites.
  - The Inner Resource gives traumatised readers an exit and a base to return to.
  - Readers have explicit permission to adapt the scripts.
  - Usable by people without PTSD. Some readers felt the title puts off people who would benefit.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **The evidence is oversold in the subtitle and blurb.** "Proven-effective" and "extensive research" rested, at publication, on small uncontrolled studies:
    - Walter Reed feasibility study, 2007: n = 7 active-duty personnel, 9 weeks, no control group. The authors state the sample was too small for inferential analysis and report only a trend in PCL scores, with 43% home-practice compliance and a 14% dropout rate.
    - Stankovic 2011: 16 veterans enrolled, 11 completed, qualitative.
    - Pence 2014: 15 enrolled, 10 completed, pre/post only.
    - Cramer et al.'s 2018 meta-analysis of yoga for PTSD included **no** iRest trial among its 7 RCTs. It gave yoga as a whole only a weak recommendation as an *adjunct*.
    - As of October 2026, ClinicalTrials.gov lists one randomized iRest-type trial on post-traumatic symptoms (NCT06888336, n = 60, a feasibility study, completed 2025, no results posted).
    - The Institute's own trauma page is more careful than the blurb: it says the Defense Centers of Excellence recognise iRest as a complementary medicine "warranting continuing research". How the book itself summarises this research is unknown (not read).
  - **Endorsements are close to the programme.** The *Elephant Journal* interview introduction (2012) reports that the 2010 Army Surgeon General endorsement was of yoga nidra *for chronic pain*. It was made by Eric Schoomaker, who writes this book's foreword. Robin Carnes, who endorses the book on the publisher page, is a co-author of the Walter Reed feasibility study. Weintraub's review is by a fellow yoga-therapy programme founder, and it repeats a "4,500 years" antiquity claim without comment.
  - **A universal promise.** Miller writes, roughly, that whatever your background and however long ago the trauma, iRest can help heal your symptoms (quoted by Ridder). That is a strong claim for a self-help book.
  - **A self-regulation toolkit, not a recovery model.** One Goodreads reader with PTSD found Judith Herman's *Trauma and Recovery* more useful as a map of long-term recovery.
  - Readers describe the writing as **repetitive**, and one mentions weak grammar. Another finds the 10 steps hard to follow without prior experience of yoga nidra scripts.
  - **The audio is behind a paywall.** The practices are meant to be heard, but most recordings are sold separately (Goodreads reviewer; Institute shop page).
  - **Safety in unsupervised use.** Later trauma-informed work reports flashbacks, emotional distress and prolonged dissociation after yoga nidra (Luu 2024). What screening and stop-rules the book gives a lone reader needs checking.
  - **Neuroscience in the promotion.** The 2014 interview article links practice to the hippocampus "thickening". That is not a finding about iRest.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The three-part opening, with the Inner Resource *before* any depth.** Teach it first as a stand-alone skill, then name it as the exit point throughout ("you can return here at any time"). This is the single most useful trauma-sensitive device in the modern literature (compendium 03 §3.10). Credit it to Miller and iRest.
  - **Modular design.** Each stage can be practised on its own. Offer complete sessions at a few fixed lengths (for example 10, 20 and 35 min). This answers compendium 04's choice between "one core practice or a collection".
  - **Little and often** as the practice instruction, rather than long weekly sessions.
  - **Opposites extended to thoughts and beliefs,** and practised with the Inner Resource as an anchor ("weaving"). It is a gentler, more regulated version of Satyananda's opposites.
  - **Vocabulary chosen for your reader.** Miller says "Mission" for soldiers. Offer readers alternatives (desire, purpose, core value) instead of imposing "sankalpa".
  - **Permission to rephrase and record scripts in their own voice.** Pair it with a clear note on whether teachers may read your scripts aloud.
  - **Separate sleep practices from awake practices.** This takes the guilt out of "I keep falling asleep" (debate 3 in compendium 08).
  - **Honesty about Western relaxation roots.** That PMR and autogenics appear in a yoga nidra book is evidence for your history chapter.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Don't repeat "proven-effective" or "endorsed by the Surgeon General".** Say instead: studied in small pilot studies in military and VA settings, promising but not established, and used *alongside* treatment, not instead of it (compendium 06 §6.3). If you mention the endorsement, say it concerned chronic pain.
  - **Don't present your book as PTSD treatment.** Include screening, stop-rules and "see a therapist" guidance (compendium 07).
  - **Don't reproduce iRest's ten-step sequence as your own method.** iRest is a registered trademark and the protocol is the Institute's teaching product. Describe it with attribution, and use generic names for your own stages.
  - **Watch the non-dual end-point hidden inside "secular" wording.** "Who am I?" and "I Am" practices are Advaita self-inquiry. Be open about your own framing rather than calling it neutral.
  - **Miller's sankalpa etymology.** In the 2012 interview he glosses *san* as "born from the heart". That is not the standard derivation (*sam* + *√kḷp*, "to form, to resolve"). Check whether the book repeats it, and don't copy it.
  - **Brain-change claims** (the hippocampus) used to promote the method.
