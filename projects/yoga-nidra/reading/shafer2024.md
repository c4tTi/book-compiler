## In one paragraph
This is a practitioner's manual for psychotherapists, addiction counsellors and yoga therapists. Shafer describes herself as having about four decades of experience as a psychotherapist, addiction professional and trauma specialist (pp. 24, 51, 164). She says she is certified in EMDR (p. 79) and a certified yoga therapist (pp. 39, 51). The foreword calls her "Dr. Shafer" (p. 14), and she mentions having left Florida (p. 80). The book sets four "brainbody" approaches side by side: her own FUN® Program, EMDR, Brainspotting and yoga nidra. It explains each in neuroscience terms, with case stories, and shows how to combine them. Yoga nidra needs no mental-health licence and no talking (pp. 136–137), though she calls yoga teacher training and yoga nidra training "highly recommended" (p. 136). Brainspotting also needs no licence but does need formal BSP training, while EMDR is limited to licensed master's-level clinicians (pp. 135, 142). In her framing, yoga nidra is the restful, integrating partner to the two processing therapies. "Falling awake" means staying aware in deep relaxation. The tone is chatty. Endorsers include David Grand (founder of Brainspotting) and Amy Weintraub.

## Core ideas
- **The quiet partner to the processing therapies.** EMDR and Brainspotting are done sitting up, with talking or close tracking. Yoga nidra is done lying down, and no one has to say what came up. She counts this as one of its main benefits, while noting that lying down can feel exposed (pp. 104, 135).
- **Session phases** (p. 103): a secure position and space, an intention, awareness of physical sensation, guided relaxation around the body, breath, the five koshas (energy layers), awareness of cognitions and imagery, and a return to the wakeful present moment.
- **A brainwave map borrowed from Desai** (citing Desai 2017). Alpha is awareness of thinking without reacting. Theta is dreamlike imagery. Delta is pure consciousness, identified with turiya (pp. 103–104). Theta is also said to reach the deep memories where healing takes place (p. 104, citing Miller 2023).
- **Sankalpa as belief work.** An intention can move a client toward a preferred belief despite a "blocking belief", using an "I accept myself even though…" formula (pp. 121–122). Clients may instead name a stress or craving to let go of (p. 106). Appendix C gives sample intentions.
- **Trauma-informed framing.** She uses SAMHSA's six principles. Her yoga-specific rules: stay within your scope, use calm and inclusive language, and avoid Sanskrit and deity references unless the client raises faith first. Yoga sits inside a clinical team, not alone (p. 116).
- **Who may teach what.** Yoga nidra training is best done by a certified yoga instructor with trauma-informed training; you need not be a mental health professional (p. 136). For the licensing comparison see the paragraph above.
- **Choice language.** The script and the short exercises keep telling people that there is no right or wrong way to practise (pp. 170, 192, 209, 211; see quote 2).

## The practice as this book teaches it
- **Setup and length.** Lying on the back or sitting in a chair (p. 106). In Ch. 5 eyes may be open or closed (p. 107), but the script asks for eyes closed throughout and suggests an eye covering (p. 193). Sessions usually run 30–45 minutes, and shorter ones are possible. Gentle stretching or tense-and-release can come first (p. 106).
- **Appendix D, the YogaFun® script (pp. 191–207).** It is written for a live group, including on Zoom, as a morning session titled "Falling Awake with Intention". Stages as far as the snippets show:
  1. joint rotations, leg raises, knees-to-chest and side-to-side "windshield wiper" knees, with a bolster under the knees offered for the lower back (p. 192);
  2. lying down, held by the earth, with a theme of the **sixth chakra (third eye)** (pp. 192–194);
  3. final adjustments (blanket, eye pillow) so that no one needs to move during the practice, then a resolve to stay aware and alert throughout (p. 193);
  4. own sankalpa or a third-eye intention; she offers sample wordings about intuition, trusting one's guides and being at peace (p. 194);
  5. **tense-and-release** of shoulders, arms and fists, then letting go with a sigh (p. 195);
  6. responding to the guide's words without thinking or analysing, and a "complete yogic breath" filling the lungs bottom to top like a water bottle, with cues such as "Take 4 breaths" (p. 196);
  7. a **naming-body-parts scan** (all ten toes, ankles, knees, pelvis, abdomen, chest, arms, hands, throat, face, back of the head, shoulder blades, spine), noticing each part without comment or judgment (p. 197);
  8. face and eye relaxation, and energy drawn up through the chakras (p. 199);
  9. **heaviness** (body sinking into the earth like stone into sand), followed straight away by **an invitation to recall a painful memory and re-experience it**, then breath and relaxation (p. 202);
  10. three chosen affirmations and sending light to an area that needs healing (p. 203);
  11. a **61-point rotation** starting at the eyebrow centre, resting a "blue starlight" point at each point (throat, right shoulder, elbow, wrist, thumb, each finger…) (p. 206);
  12. resting as the witness, then deepening the breath and returning (p. 206).

  So the script contains two rotations: the short toes-to-spine naming scan (p. 197) and the 61-point rotation (p. 206). This is directive in style. **How firm the sequence is:** the numbered order follows the book's own page order (pp. 192–206), so stages on different pages are in the printed order. What is inferred: the order of items that share a page (e.g. heaviness and the painful memory on p. 202; the 61 points and the witness on p. 206), and whether stages exist in the pages I got no snippets from. Treat it as an outline with possible gaps, not a complete script.
- **Example of how a stage is guided (paraphrase, Appendix E "Four Elements", Earth step, p. 209).** The guide tells the client the exercise will take about two minutes, so anxious or fidgety people know it will be short. They are asked to notice how the ground holds them, starting at the feet if seated in a chair or at the tailbone if on the floor, and then to feel the whole body supported. The instructions are short and present-tense, give a choice of posture, and do not interpret anything.
- **Sleep in the script.** Participants are told they will not fall asleep. With permission, partners may nudge snorers, and Zoom participants are asked to mute (pp. 191–192). Yet the same page suggests that chair users sit with their back to a wall so the head has support if they doze off (p. 191; also p. 136).
- **Props and room (pp. 111, 114–116).** A thick mat with a folded blanket on top, a wall behind a chair, eye covers (a washcloth will do), and a blanket because the body cools; blankets can also support knees, ankles or neck. Her trauma-informed setup list adds that participants should not have their backs to doors or exits, that instructions go slowly with options to modify, and that teachers stay within their scope (p. 116). For self-practice she suggests recording the script in advance and finding a private place for about 45 minutes (p. 111).
- **Other practices.** An adapted Himalayan Institute Vishoka meditation done seated, with one breath at each point of a body journey (p. 112). Appendix E adds a container exercise (p. 208), the Four Elements (p. 209), and a healing-light stream that can close an incomplete session, in which light leaves through an opening in the soles of the feet (p. 211).
- **Combining with EMDR:** one client account of an EMDR session on a Tuesday and yoga nidra on a later evening, both on Zoom (p. 116); the snippet does not show how many days apart they were. It is an anecdote, not a protocol.
- **What I could not see:** how the script introduces the sankalpa beyond the sample wordings on p. 194; whether lightness follows heaviness (lightness is not confirmed); the full 61-point sequence; how the painful-memory passage is resolved. For the I AM structure this script likely follows, see `desai2017.md` (Ch. 7 / Ch. 20). That is unverified for Shafer's version.

## What's distinctive
- It is the only book in the set that **places yoga nidra next to EMDR and Brainspotting** from inside a psychotherapy practice. It compares them on posture, talking, licensing and when to use each (pp. 133–136).
- It brings the **I AM / Amrit line into clinical trauma work**, which is otherwise dominated by iRest. Compared with `desai2017.md`: the **tense-and-release** and **heaviness** stages match Desai's printed script (Desai pp. 353–354, 357–358), and so do the brainwave map and the 45-minute/three-hour sleep claim (Desai p. xii, p. 95). Where they differ: besides a short naming scan from toes to spine (p. 197), Shafer prints a **61-point blue-star rotation** (p. 206), which is Himalayan in origin, whereas search-inside found no rotation in Desai's printed script.
- It links **sankalpa to EMDR-style blocking beliefs**.
- Its **"when triggered" appendix** of short exercises (the Four Elements is announced as about two minutes, p. 209) is a usable model of an exit or micro-practice.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:** clear on scope of practice; practical trauma options (chair, eyes open in Ch. 5, no disclosure, choice language); yoga nidra framed as an adjunct, which matches the evidence (`06`); a group script and short exercises in one place.
- **Limitations (all from snippets; there are no independent reviews to cite):**
  - **Unsupported sleep claim:** 45 minutes equals three hours of sleep (p. 110, repeated p. 191). This is a variant of the `06` myth-table row (1 h = 4 h), and no study supports either ratio.
  - **Speculative neuroscience.** Delta is presented as pure consciousness, and yoga nidra as a possible aid after brain injury (pp. 103–104, citing Desai 2017). The EEG literature mostly shows alpha and theta (`06`).
  - **Thin safety guidance, and a risky script stage.** "Contraindicated" returns no hits. The only exclusions I found are clients under the influence of alcohol (the snippet is cut off before this) or with a "psychotic mood disorder" (p. 134). Dissociation is discussed mainly in the EMDR and Brainspotting chapters (pp. 61–71, 94), with passing mentions in Ch. 1 (p. 33) and a Ch. 6 case (p. 127). I found no dissociation guidance specific to yoga nidra. Yet the group script asks participants to bring up a painful memory and relive it (p. 202). That is memory activation in an unscreened group, including on Zoom, which sits awkwardly beside her own scope-of-practice rule (`07`).
  - **Her rules and her script pull in different directions.** She says to avoid Sanskrit and deity references (p. 116), but the script is chakra-themed. Partner nudging brings touch into a trauma-oriented session. Sleep is fine in one exercise (p. 43) but ruled out in the script (p. 191); see `08` §3 (is falling asleep failure?).
  - **History is unreliable.** The Upanishads are given as the first written source, c. 700 BCE (p. 107). Satyananda's sleep-learning story is retold (p. 108). Call is said to describe "Nidra" (p. 107), but her 1891 text contains neither "nidra" nor "yoga". On Swami Rama, p. 108 gives a single date: in 1970 he came to the US and formed the Himalayan Institute in Honesdale, Pennsylvania (confirmed in search-inside). Standard accounts: arrived in the US 1969, Institute founded 1971 in Illinois, moved to Honesdale 1977 (hihtindia.org founder page; Wikipedia). The Menninger lab work was 1970 (`green1977.md`; `01` §Swami Rama & the Menninger experiments). Shafer conflates these.
  - **"Evidence-based research"** (publisher): PubMed finds no studies by her on these methods.
  - **Reception (seen once in the first draft, Oct 2026, not re-checkable):** Goodreads 0 ratings; Amazon US 3 ratings, average 5.0, sales rank around #800,000. The only published praise is endorsements collected by the publisher.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - The **posture and talking comparison**; offer chair and eyes-open options as standard (`07` §7.1). Her point that lying down can feel exposed, while not having to talk is a relief (p. 104), is a good two-sided framing for a trauma-aware chapter.
  - Her **room checklist** (p. 116): no backs to doors or exits, slow guidance with options, teacher within scope. Combine it with the props list (pp. 114–115: mat plus folded blanket, wall behind chair, washcloth as eye cover, blanket for cooling and support) as a practical setup box.
  - The **cue to make all adjustments before the practice so no one needs to move** (p. 193) and the **"Take 4 breaths" pacing cue** placed after stages (seen on pp. 196, 203): simple devices for timing a spoken script.
  - **Recording your own script** for self-practice (p. 111), useful advice for readers who have no teacher or app.
  - **Choice language** ("no right or wrong way", pp. 170, 192, 209, 211) and **offering posture choices inside the exercise itself** (the Earth step begins at the feet for chair users and at the tailbone for floor users, p. 209).
  - The **scope boundary**: trained yoga teachers may teach yoga nidra, and trauma *processing* belongs to clinicians. Cite SAMHSA's principles directly.
  - A **short (about two-minute) exit practice**, announced as short in advance (Appendix E, p. 209).
  - **Intention despite a blocking belief**, for readers whose positive sankalpa feels false. Credit the EMDR origin.
  - A **real example of an I AM-derived script (co-written with Kamini Desai), with Himalayan 61 points, used in a clinic**, as an alternative to assuming that clinical yoga nidra means iRest (`01`). Her p. 108 lineage sketch of Amrit and Kamini Desai is a handy pointer, but check dates against `desai2017.md`.
  - A **body-scan exit from an incomplete session** (the healing-light stream, p. 211) as a model for closing a practice that was cut short.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - The **45-minutes = 3 hours** line and the **delta / brain-injury** claims (`06` myth table; `08` §11).
  - Its **history** (Upanishad dating, Call, Swami Rama dates). Use Birch & Hargreaves and Singleton 2005 instead.
  - It is **not a safety source**. Above all, do not copy the **painful-memory re-experiencing stage** into a script for general readers. `07` covers dissociation and trauma more fully.
  - The **chakra-themed, directive script** shows one teacher's style. It is not a trauma-informed template. Its instruction to respond without analysing leans toward suggestion (`08` §10). If you write a clinical script, keep it secular and keep sleep permitted (`08` §3).
  - Kabat-Zinn's *Falling Awake* (2018) is a different book with the same title.
