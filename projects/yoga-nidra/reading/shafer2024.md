## In one paragraph
This is a practitioner's manual for psychotherapists, addiction counsellors and yoga therapists. It is written by a Florida trauma and addiction therapist (PhD, LCSW, certified EMDR and Brainspotting therapist, certified yoga therapist) with more than 40 years in private practice. It presents four "brainbody" approaches side by side: her FUN® Program, EMDR, Brainspotting and yoga nidra. It explains each in neuroscience language, tells case stories (including her own trauma and cancer), and shows how to choose and combine them in a session. Yoga nidra is the one approach of the four that needs no licence, no talking and no processing of memories. In effect it serves as the rest-and-integration partner to the two processing therapies: "falling awake" means staying aware in deep relaxation. The phrase is also the title of Jon Kabat-Zinn's 2018 book, which she cites (pp. 107, 218). The tone is chatty and enthusiastic, with pop-culture asides. Endorsers include David Grand (founder of Brainspotting), Amy Weintraub and Susan Tebb (IAYT).

## Core ideas
- **Yoga nidra as the processing therapies' quiet partner.** EMDR and Brainspotting are done sitting up and talking (or at least with the therapist tracking). Yoga nidra is done lying down, with no need to say what comes up. Shafer calls this relief from talking one of the practice's "gems", while noting that lying down can feel vulnerable for some (pp. 104, 135).
- **The phases of a session** (p. 103), paraphrased: settle into a secure position and space; set an intention; feel physical sensation; relax the body part by part; use the breath; move through the five koshas; watch thoughts and images; return to waking.
- **A brainwave map borrowed from Desai.** Alpha is "noticing thinking without reacting", theta is dream-like imagery, and delta is "pure consciousness" (pp. 103, 137, citing Desai 2017). Theta is also said to reach the deep-seated memories "where healing occurs" (p. 104, citing Miller 2023).
- **Sankalpa as belief work.** A sankalpa can point toward a "preferred belief" despite a "blocking belief". She uses an acceptance formula of the "I accept myself even though…" type as the intention (pp. 121–122). Appendix C gives sample intentions. Clients may instead name a stress, worry or craving to let go of (p. 106).
- **Trauma-informed framing.** She uses SAMHSA's six principles (p. 116) and yoga-specific rules: stay in your scope of practice; use calm, inclusive language; avoid Sanskrit and references to deities unless the client has raised faith matters first (p. 116). Yoga is presented as part of a clinical team, not a stand-alone treatment (p. 116).
- **Who may teach it.** Yoga nidra is best learned from a certified yoga instructor with trauma-informed training, and you do not need to be a mental-health professional to offer it (p. 136). EMDR, by contrast, is limited to licensed master's-level clinicians (p. 142).
- **Choice language throughout:** "no right way or wrong way… only your way" recurs in the script and the short exercises (pp. 170, 192, 209, 211).

## The practice as this book teaches it
- **Setup and length.** Lying on the back or sitting in a chair (p. 106), eyes open or closed. Some lineages fix this, she notes, and eye coverings can help (p. 107). A full session is usually 30–45 minutes, and shorter sessions are possible (p. 106). Gentle stretching or tense-and-release can come first (p. 106).
- **Appendix D, the YogaFun® script (pp. 191–207, about 17 pages).** It is written for a live group, including over Zoom, as a morning session titled "Falling Awake with Intention". It opens with joint rotations, leg raises and knees-to-chest before lying down (p. 192). The theme is the **sixth chakra (third eye, ajna)**, explained through the chakra's associated body parts and the idea of intuition (pp. 192–194). Students use their own sankalpa or work with "the power of the third eye" (p. 194). Body relaxation includes the face and the muscles around the eyes (p. 199). Energy is drawn up through the chakras to the crown (p. 199). The student picks three affirmations (p. 203), rests as the witness, and returns slowly (p. 206). One instruction asks the body to respond to the guide's words "without thinking or analyzing" (p. 196). That is the directive, absorptive style of Amrit/I AM yoga nidra.
- **Sleep in the script.** The introduction tells participants this is a session in which they will not fall asleep. With permission, partners may nudge someone who starts snoring, and Zoom participants are asked to mute (pp. 191–192).
- **Chapter 5, adapted Vishoka meditation (p. 112).** A seated practice. She says it comes from the Himalayan Institute (Tigunait 2019) and that she adapted it for settings outside a yoga centre.
- **Appendix E, micro-practices for overwhelm.** A container exercise (p. 208); a Four Elements exercise starting with Earth, which she tells clients lasts "about three minutes" so the thinking brain knows it won't take an hour (p. 209); and a healing-light visualisation in which light flows out through a "trap door" in the soles of the feet (p. 211).
- **Combining with EMDR.** A client reports doing EMDR on a Tuesday and yoga nidra the next evening, followed by a burst of energy the day after (p. 116). This is a case anecdote, not a protocol.
- **What I could not see:** the full body-rotation order, whether there is breath counting or a pairs-of-opposites stage in the script, and the exact wording of the sankalpa step.

## What's distinctive
- It is the only book in the project's set that **places yoga nidra next to EMDR and Brainspotting** from inside a psychotherapy practice. It compares them on posture, talking, licensing and when to use each ("How to decide which therapy to use", p. 133).
- It brings the **I AM / Amrit yoga nidra line into clinical trauma work**, which is otherwise dominated by iRest. Desai co-wrote the script and wrote the foreword.
- It links **sankalpa to EMDR-style "blocking beliefs"**, which no other book in the set does.
- Its **"when triggered" appendix** of three-minute grounding exercises is a useful model of an exit or micro-practice.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:** clear on scope of practice (who may teach what). Gives practical trauma options: chair, eyes open, no disclosure, choice language. Frames yoga nidra as an adjunct within a care team, which matches the evidence (`06`: studied as a complement, not a replacement). Puts a ready group script and short grounding exercises in one place. Draws on many case stories (Weintraub's endorsement singles these out).
- **Limitations (all from snippets; there are no independent reviews to cite):**
  - **The sleep claim is unsupported.** The book says research shows a 45-minute practice equals three hours of sleep (p. 110, repeated p. 191). The compendium's myth check (`06`) finds no evidence for any claim of this kind.
  - **Speculative neuroscience.** Delta is presented as "pure consciousness", and yoga nidra as a possible aid to recovery from traumatic brain injury because of the slow-wave delta it supposedly induces (p. 104, citing Desai 2017). The EEG literature mostly shows alpha and theta (`06`).
  - **Thin safety section.** "Contraindicated" and "contraindication" return no hits. The only exclusions I found are being under the influence of drugs or alcohol and "psychotic mood disorder" (p. 134). I found no yoga-nidra-specific guidance on dissociation, flashbacks or abreaction *during* the practice. Dissociation is discussed under EMDR and Brainspotting (pp. 61–69, 89, 94).
  - **Her own rules and her script pull in different directions.** She advises avoiding Sanskrit and deity references unless the client raises them (p. 116), yet the main script is built on chakra and third-eye material. Partner nudging of snorers brings touch into a trauma-oriented session. And falling asleep is "okay! No judgment!" in one exercise (p. 43) but ruled out in the script (p. 191).
  - **History needs checking.** The Upanishads are given as where the practice was "first written about", dated to about 700 BCE (p. 107). Satyananda's account of learning mantras while asleep is retold (p. 108). Annie Payson Call's *Power Through Repose* is said to describe "Nidra" as pure awareness (p. 107), but the public-domain text of Call's 1891 book contains neither "nidra" nor "yoga". Swami Rama's arrival and the founding of the Himalayan Institute are dated together to 1970 (p. 108); check this against standard accounts. Amrit Desai is described as having "left the Kripalu Ashram" (p. 108), and I could not see whether the 1994 circumstances are mentioned (`08` §6).
  - **The "research" claim can't be checked.** The publisher speaks of her "internationally recognised and evidence-based research", but a PubMed author lookup found no studies by her on these methods. The FUN® trademark material is self-promotional.
  - **Reception:** Goodreads shows 0 ratings and 0 reviews; Amazon US shows 3 ratings, average 5.0, sales rank around #800,000 (Oct 2026). The only published praise is publisher-collected endorsements.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - The **posture and talking comparison**. Lying down is restful but can feel exposed. No one has to say what came up. Offer a chair and eyes-open option as standard. This gives you a crisp paragraph for a trauma-sensitive section (cross-check `07` §7.1).
  - The **scope-of-practice boundary**: yoga nidra can be taught by trained yoga teachers, while trauma *processing* belongs to licensed clinicians. Stating this protects you and your readers. Cite SAMHSA's six principles directly, not through Shafer.
  - The idea of a **three-minute "exit" practice** for when a reader becomes overwhelmed (Appendix E), and of telling people in advance how short it will be. A good model for an emergency page in your book.
  - The **"intention despite a blocking belief"** move, for readers whose positive sankalpa feels false. Credit the EMDR and belief-work origin, and test it on yourself before offering it.
  - A **real-world example of the I AM / Amrit line in a clinic**, useful if you map lineages (`01`) and want an alternative to the assumption that clinical yoga nidra means iRest.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - Do not repeat the **"45 minutes = 3 hours of sleep"** line or the **delta / brain-injury** claims. If you mention them, mark them as unsupported claims (`06` myth table; `08` §11).
  - Do not cite this book for **history**: the Upanishad dating, Satyananda's sleep-learning story, and the Call "Nidra" attribution. Use Birch & Hargreaves and Singleton 2005 instead, and see Call herself for what she actually wrote.
  - Do not use it as a **safety source**. Its contraindications are thinner than `07`, which already covers dissociation, psychosis and trauma in more detail (from Miller 2015a and Luu 2024).
  - Treat the **chakra-themed script** as an example of one teacher's style, not a trauma-informed template. Its directive "respond to my words without analyzing" phrasing leans toward hypnotic suggestion (`08` §10), and partner touch to prevent sleep conflicts with choice-based language. If you write a clinical-setting script, keep it secular and keep sleep permitted.
  - Don't confuse it with Jon Kabat-Zinn's *Falling Awake* (2018), which shares the title.
