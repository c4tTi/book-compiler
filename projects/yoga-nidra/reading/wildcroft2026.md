## In one paragraph
This is a craft manual for yoga teachers (and others who guide rest) that teaches you to **compose and lead your own yoga nidras** instead of reading other people's scripts. The authors' starting point (interview) is that teachers who can lead asana or meditation without a script lose confidence with nidra, partly because the big 20th-century schools kept their methods proprietary and mystified the practice. The book's answer is to explain the *why* of each stage: background first (sleep science, the history of sleep, a critical history of yoga nidra), then the "process" a practice moves through, then a five-stage content "structure" (settling, body rotation, opposites, visualization, return), then delivery skills, troubleshooting and creativity. Its central promise is in the title: **simplicity as a principle, not a beginner's stage**. Most people first need a good experience of deep rest, and "It has taken us more than a decade to make our yoga nidras this simple" (p. 20).

## Core ideas
- **Simplicity is the goal, not the first step.** A safe and simple nidra is presented as one of the highest forms of the practice, not a watered-down version (p. 20; interview).
- **Composing beats reciting.** Teachers lack an understanding of what each stage is for, not skills. Learn the five-stage structure and add stages in order: start with settling, body rotation and return, then add opposites, then visualization (pp. 97, 161).
- **Yoga nidra prolongs the natural process of falling asleep.** It holds people near the hypnagogic edge instead of letting them drop into deep (delta) sleep, though that can happen if someone is very tired (p. 30; interview).
- **Safety, because the state is suggestible.** The practice puts people into vulnerable, suggestible states close to hypnosis. The book's image: yoga nidra can feel like paddling in a very deep ocean, but the shore is always close at hand (p. 19). In the interview Wildcroft introduces the same image as something people "sometimes talk about". Care, consent and boundaries come first; the aim is good rest, and insight is a bonus (interview).
- **Rest as justice.** Lack of deep rest is described as a social crisis that falls hardest on marginalised people (interview), and ch. 3 traces how industrial capitalism reshaped sleep (index: "capitalism (and the history of sleep) 51–55").
- **Safe and simple as a response to harm.** The authors state that at least one senior teacher of most of the schools they discuss has been credibly accused or found guilty of serious abuse (p. 78), and present safe and simple nidras as deliberately created in response to harm done under more traditional styles (p. 78).
- **Critical history.** The word *yoganidrā* once named a state and a divine power of sleep. The modern method starts with Satyananda's experiments combining Indian meditation ideas with Western relaxationism (p. 95); the book gives Dennis Boyes's 1973 book a role in that story (pp. 73–75) and notes that guided imagery came from psychotherapists rather than gurus (p. 72). It points readers to Mallinson & Singleton's *Roots of Yoga* (p. 62).
- **Sankalpa as a modern, hypnosis-like device.** The book suggests sankalpa may be a modern compromise between the pre-modern state and 19th-century therapeutic practice, and notes it is timed like a hypnotherapist's pre- or post-hypnotic suggestion (p. 74).
- **Against "one right way".** The interview frames the book as freeing teachers from the idea of a single correct method; NSDR, Liminal Dreaming and Somatic Dreaming are named as related practices (p. 12), and NSDR is described as repackaging aspects of yoga nidra with a claim to scientific proof (p. 77; the snippet breaks off before the authors' verdict).

## The practice as this book teaches it

Notes on the table:
- **Settling.** Can range from simply inviting a resting position to more active preparation (p. 98); uses what teachers may know as grounding, centring and orienting practices, turning attention through the senses in turn (p. 101). A recording called "Settling" models invitational language and choices (p. 135).
- **Sankalpa / intention.** Schools that use a sankalpa place it at the end of settling (p. 102). The book offers examples of general resolves ("I am calm and at peace") but warns that, unless you know your participants well, any intention may be inaccessible or inappropriate for someone (p. 92), and says intentions should always be discussed with practitioners beforehand (p. 102). This puts the book in the **"optional"** camp of `08` §8. It also links repeating an intention to sustaining concentration (pp. 92–93). The word *sankalpa* is indexed at pp. 73, 74, 92, 93, 102.
- **Body rotation.** A definitive, universal map of the body is impossible; people may have dissociated from full bodily awareness or have a different map (p. 104). Routes: motor (figure 4, p. 105), sensory (starting from the mouth and tongue, p. 107), esoteric/marma (figure 5, pp. 107–110), cross-body and imaginary (pp. 103, 111). The interview adds memorisation tips and the classic thumb-and-fingers route as an example. Recordings 1–5 (p. 183) demonstrate the routes, split between Theo and Beverley.
- **Opposites.** Can be combined with rotation (p. 116); iRest's kośa-by-kośa opposites are discussed (p. 117), and p. 118 tabulates pairs for breath (inhale/exhale), energy (lively/lethargic, grounded/spacey) and mind activity (busy/calm).
- **Visualization.** Some see it as the heart of the practice, yet it is optional (pp. 97, 118). A common frame is a short journey, e.g. a sunrise over a landscape you love (pp. 120–121); seasons and the local environment are recommended as themes (p. 121); name the theme in advance so no one is surprised (p. 148, the hot-air-balloon example).
- **Return.** Grounding and orienting techniques used in reverse (p. 102); keep eye contact to spot anyone still disoriented (p. 124).
- **Before the practice.** The facilitator's eyes stay open and they look after the space (pp. 81, 133); participants know that falling asleep, or leaving, is acceptable (p. 133). Room practicalities (lighting, windows, noise) are in ch. 7 (p. 129); the interview adds a drum kit starting up next door as an example.

**Length:** 20–30 minutes "feels about right"; 10 minutes is just possible with editing, and 40 can feel almost too long (p. 97). A shortened form can keep only settling, body rotation and return (p. 97).
**Posture:** supine is the default, but side-lying, supported leaning or sitting in a chair are all offered; props should cradle the body so muscular effort can be released (p. 99). Eye pillows, sheepskins and sleeping bags suit some people and not others (p. 99). Eyes may be open, closed or partly closed (p. 133). People who snore can try side-lying or sitting (p. 146).
**Language:** invitational. Every part is optional, and options are given with reasons where helpful (pp. 103, 135).
**Falling asleep:** expected and acceptable. "Sleeping and snoring" is a section of ch. 8 (p. 145), with advice such as suggesting side-lying or a supported seated position (pp. 146, 153). In the interview Nolan admits to falling asleep in Wildcroft's nidras (snoring is mentioned). This puts the book in the "sleep is fine" camp of debate 3 (`08`).

**Example of how a stage is guided (paraphrase, with one quote).** Moving from settling into rotation, the facilitator says the first invitation is to follow them around the body and reminds people that nothing is compulsory (p. 103). For posture, the guide suggests lying on the back, then offers lying on the side, then any other position, sometimes adding *why* someone might choose an option (p. 135). In the sensory route, attention starts in the mouth, with the feel of the tongue (p. 107). **Compare Satyananda** (`04` §4.1): the practitioner is told to say mentally "I am practising yoga nidra", and body parts are named rapidly in a fixed order. Wildcroft & Nolan sit clearly on the **invitational** side of `08` §5 (Directive or invitational?; see `07` §7.4).

## What's distinctive
- **Teacher craft, short and stage-by-stage.** Among the books compiled, this and Dinsmore-Tuli 2020 both reject a standard script and teach you to compose your own practice. The difference: Dinsmore-Tuli teaches composition inside the Total Yoga Nidra framework (nine ingredients in a fixed order, justified by its "adaptogenic" rationale; see `reading/dinsmoretuli2020.md`). Wildcroft & Nolan give a shorter, school-neutral five-stage structure, a separate "process" chapter, and a lot of room and voice craft (holding space, content delivery, managing difficulties).
- **A scholar of modern yoga writing a practitioner manual.** The history chapter follows the critical scholarship (Boyes, Western relaxationism, the Satyananda school's origin claims, the documented abuse cases), so it lines up with compendium `01`/`08` rather than with lineage myths. Unusual among the manuals compiled here.
- **A whole chapter on the history of sleep (ch. 3).** The snippets show biphasic sleep ("first" and "second" sleep, citing Ekirch 2001; the authors note it is hard to know how widespread it was, p. 46), medieval Books of Hours and how parish church bells marked the canonical hours, with a table of bell patterns and sleep phases (pp. 47–48), and the shift to clock time and monophasic sleep (pp. 52–53). Chronotypes (with Roenneberg et al. 2007) are in ch. 2 (p. 23). Placing yoga nidra in the wider history of sleep is unusual among the books compiled here.
- **Separate chapters on "process" and "structure"** (chs. 5 and 6): what happens to the participant, kept apart from the content sequence. Unusual among the manuals compiled here (check against `04` §4.4 before claiming more).
- **Hands-on facilitation craft:** memorising rotation routes, route diagrams, room management, voice, co-leading with musicians (p. 168), a two-voice nidra (interview), and QR-linked audio placed where each technique is explained.
- **Troubleshooting chapter** with named difficulties (emotional release, sleeping and snoring, restlessness, uncomfortable imagery, dissociation, disorientation) and a summary table (p. 152), including a 5-4-3-2-1 senses grounding technique (p. 152).
- Pronunciation guide and glossary for Sanskrit terms, which teacher-facing books rarely include.

## Strengths and limitations
- Strengths:
  - Intellectually honest history from someone working in the field. In the interview Wildcroft calls it a deliberately edited history, with examples chosen because they help practice.
  - It helps teachers become **independent of scripts**, which matches the stated purpose of your own project.
  - Concrete accessibility guidance: posture options and props (p. 99), optional visualization with themes named in advance (p. 148), intentions discussed beforehand (p. 102), grounding at the start and end (p. 102), a facilitator who stays alert with eyes open (p. 133).
  - Compact (208 pp.) and modern (2026), with audio examples so you can hear the voice.
  - Long practical experience (about 15 and 40 years of teaching). Six monthly "Live Lab" sessions with subscribers were scheduled for Feb–Jul 2025 to test the material, and the authors asked readers to send in their own recordings.
- Limitations / critiques (no independent reviews exist yet):
  - **No independent reception yet.** As of 2 Oct 2026: 0 ratings on Goodreads and no journal or magazine reviews found. The only published praise is the publisher's blurb from Jivana Heyman (Accessible Yoga), whom Wildcroft introduced as a dear friend in a 2024 Substack post. The NBN host was warm and gave no critique.
  - **Brainwave material needs checking against `06`.** Ch. 2 teaches the five brainwave bands (pp. 26–31). Binaural beats are covered (p. 36) with a sceptical-sounding note on evidence for "brain hacks" (snippet breaks off). Ch. 4 has a table of brainwave activity in experienced practitioners, introduced as cautious speculation (pp. 66–67), and p. 84 maps each kośa to brainwave bands (e.g. ānandamayakośa: gamma/delta). That mapping is not an established finding (`06` §6.1 EEG: the evidence mostly shows alpha/theta).
  - **The research may be dated on arrival.** The manuscript was due for submission at the end of September 2025, so the 2025–26 meta-analyses (Ghai 2026, Singh 2026, Dutta 2026) are probably missing.
  - Nolan's earlier public writing on sleep (2024) gives figures for larks and owls (about 40% / 30%) without sources, and calls NSDR simply a secular label for yoga nidra, a view the compendium (`08` §9) treats as contested. Check whether either survives into the book.
  - **Thin end reference list (about 2 pp.)**, though chapters carry footnotes.
  - **A position, not a survey.** In the interview the authors voice concern that some derived practices (NSDR, Somatic Dreaming) are proprietorial; how fully the book argues this is only partly visible (p. 77). Readers who want depth in a lineage (sankalpa as seed, turīya, the koshas as a path) will find less here than in Satyananda, Miller or Stanley.
  - **UK, yoga-teacher audience.** The authors themselves say some familiarity with yoga is needed.
  - The "safe" in the title can't be guaranteed by any method. Wildcroft's course manual is careful about this: it aims to start "from a place that is as safe as possible" and models telling a class the teacher will keep watch, instead of saying "you are totally safe here".

## For your own book
- Take:
  - **The process / structure split** as an architecture. Explain what each phase *does* before giving any script, and teach stages in a learnable order (settling, rotation and return first; opposites; then visualization). This fits the gross-to-subtle "common skeleton" in `04` §4.7.
  - **Their concrete accessibility rules.** Use them as a checklist next to Luu 2024 [@luu2024]: state length and contents up front; posture options and props; intentions only by agreement; imagery optional, short and named in advance; consistent naming of body sides (from Wildcroft's manual); ground at the start and the end; watch over the room instead of promising safety.
  - **The history line** (yoganidrā as state and goddess → 19th-century relaxationism → Satyananda's synthesis, with Boyes). It confirms the "ancient roots, modern form" position (`08` §1).
  - **The stance on sleep** ("sleep is fine, manage the snoring") and rest as access and justice.
  - **Craft features worth copying:** route diagrams and memorisation aids; QR-linked audio extracts at the point of explanation; short example wording alongside each technique; a co-led (two-voice) recording.
  - **Checks against the compendium's debates:**
    - Hypnosis (`08` §10): the book sits in the **"close cousin"** position. It says hypnosis, like yoga nidra, is actively induced and modulated by suggestion, unlike natural hypnagogia (p. 90), and compares sankalpa timing to post-hypnotic suggestion (p. 74).
    - Misconduct (`08` §6): the book **acknowledges it** and builds its approach partly as a response (p. 78). Its notes cite the Royal Commission and Pankhania & Hargreaves 2017 on Satyananda Yoga (p. 75), matching [@rc2016; @pankhania2017].
    - Promise (`08` §11): **modest-claims camp** as far as visible. The book discusses what to promise participants (p. 132; detail not visible) and praises a practice that does not promise profound transformation (p. 171). No "1 hour = 4 hours of sleep" claim turned up in searches (`06` §6.3).
    - Brainwaves (`06` §6.1, §6.3): it reports the Swami Rama "delta while aware" story and says concrete evidence is hard to find (p. 75), which matches `06` §6.3. Treat the kośa–brainwave table (p. 84) as speculation.
  - **Its framing for your proposal:** this book is now your most important *comparative title* (with Dinsmore-Tuli 2020). Wildcroft's own posts on writing a book say a proposal must list comparative titles and say what is unique about yours.
- Skip or treat critically:
  - **Don't borrow its science for claims about benefits.** Use the 2025–26 meta-analyses in `06` §6.0 instead.
  - **The "proprietary schools" critique.** If you make it, be specific and documented (rc2016, pankhania2017 [@rc2016; @pankhania2017]) rather than tonal, and be fair to iRest and Total Yoga Nidra, which publish their structures.
  - **"Trauma-informed" vs. "safe and simple".** On her website Wildcroft distrusts the "trauma-informed" label; the book uses the phrase at least once (p. 78). Your readers may include clinicians who expect that vocabulary.
  - **Sankalpa and the depth layer.** The book treats sankalpa as optional and hypnosis-like. If your book wants sankalpa as a seed or heartfelt desire, rely on Satyananda, Miller and Stanley (`08` §8).
  - **Simplicity as the only ideal.** It fits mixed-needs group classes well. Your book may also want an advanced track, which this book deliberately leaves out.
