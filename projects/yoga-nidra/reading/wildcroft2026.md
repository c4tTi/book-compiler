## In one paragraph
This is a craft manual for yoga teachers (and yoga therapists, hypnotherapists and other people who guide rest). It teaches you to **write and lead your own yoga nidras** instead of reading other people's scripts. The authors' starting point is that teachers who can lead asana or meditation without a script lose confidence when it comes to nidra. They lose it because the big 20th-century schools kept their methods proprietary and mystified the practice. The book's answer is to explain the *why* of each stage. It gives the background first (sleep science, the history of sleep, an honest history of yoga nidra), then the "process" a practice moves through and a content "structure you can rely on". After that come techniques, voice and room skills, troubleshooting, and finally creativity. Its central promise is in the title: **simplicity as a principle, not a beginner's stage**. The authors argue that a safe, simple, inclusive nidra is one of the highest forms of the practice, because what most people need first is a good experience of deep rest.

## Core ideas
- **Simplicity is the goal, not the first step.** A safe and simple nidra is presented as one of the highest forms of the practice, not a watered-down version. The authors say it took them more than a decade to make their nidras this simple.
- **Composing beats reciting.** Teachers already have the skills they need. What they lack is an understanding of what each stage is for. Once they understand the process and the structure, they don't need scripts, just as they don't need one for asana.
- **Yoga nidra prolongs the natural process of falling asleep.** It holds people at the hypnagogic edge instead of letting them drop into deep sleep. The hypnagogic state (going into sleep) and the hypnopompic state (coming out of it) are presented as threshold states that many traditions have used. The teacher only sets the conditions, and what happens there belongs to the practitioner.
- **Safety, because the state is suggestible.** The practice puts people into vulnerable, suggestible states close to hypnosis (the "hypnagogic/hypnotic" link). The authors' image is paddling in the shallow end of a deep ocean. So care, consent and boundaries come first. The aim is good rest, and wisdom or insight is a bonus.
- **Rest as justice.** Lack of deep rest is described as a social crisis that falls hardest on marginalised people (disabled people, people of colour, the underpaid). Accessibility is therefore an ethical requirement, not an add-on.
- **"Safe and simple" in place of "trauma-informed".** On her website Wildcroft says she distrusts the "trauma-informed" label because what is offered under it often isn't. She uses "safe and simple" instead: heavy on choices and invitations, light on visualization.
- **Honest history.** The word *yoganidrā* once named a state close to samādhi, and a divine power of sleep. The modern method dates from Satyananda in the 1960s–70s and draws heavily on 19th-century "relaxationism", early psychotherapy and hypnotherapy, as well as on tantric nyāsa and medieval pratyāhāra. The authors count Birch & Hargreaves (2015), Singleton (2005) and Parker et al. (2013) among their main sources, and they say they would take issue with some of Parker's generalisations.
- **Against proprietary "one right way" teaching.** This criticism covers the 20th-century schools and also newer derived brands (NSDR and "Somatic Dreaming" are named as practices that come from yoga nidra). The book is meant to free teachers from the idea that there is only one correct method.
- **Learn the scales, then improvise.** The last chapter gives readers a base for departing from the rules: themed, creative nidras built on a stable structure.

**Length:** short and light by default. Wildcroft's manual uses about 15 minutes as its example, and her five-track "Safe and Simple" collection runs 78 minutes in total. She calls a 35-minute recording "one of my longer". **Language:** invitational and plain. Give choice without loading people with mental work (offer one quality to notice, not a list to choose from). Balance clear instructions with acceptance that people will experience different things. Gentle, calm voice. **Falling asleep:** expected and acceptable. Nolan admits to dozing and even snoring in nidras. Sleep and snoring in a group are treated as practical difficulties to manage (ch. 8), not as failure. This puts the book in the "sleep is fine" camp of debate 3 (`08`). **Example of a stage, paraphrased (breath, from the manual):** the guide invites people to count their breaths in a given way, then immediately names an alternative for anyone who doesn't want to work with the breath today. Both options are framed as equally fine.

## What's distinctive
- **The only book among those compiled whose whole subject is how to compose and lead your own nidras.** Satyananda, Verma and Lusk give scripts. Miller gives a protocol. Dinsmore-Tuli gives an encyclopedic menu. This book teaches the reasoning behind each stage so that a teacher can write their own.
- **A scholar of modern yoga writing a practitioner manual.** The history chapter follows Birch & Hargreaves and Singleton (pre-modern state and goddess → Western relaxationism → Satyananda), so it lines up with compendium `01`/`08` rather than with lineage myths. No other teacher's manual does this.
- **A whole chapter on the history of sleep.** It covers chronotypes (Roenneberg et al. 2007), doubts about the popular "biphasic sleep" story, medieval Books of Hours and how church bells structured the night. Nobody else places yoga nidra in the wider human history of sleep.
- **Separate chapters on "process" and "structure"** (chs. 5 and 6): what happens in body, mind and spirit, kept apart from the content sequence. Other books merge the two.
- **Hands-on facilitation craft:** tricks for memorising body-rotation routes, room management, voice, co-leading, a **two-voice nidra**, and QR-linked audio extracts placed where each technique is explained.
- **Safety framed as care for suggestible states and as access to rest,** not as a clinical trauma protocol (compare iRest) and without the "trauma-informed" label.
- Pronunciation guide and glossary for Sanskrit terms, which teacher-facing books rarely include.

## Strengths and limitations
- Strengths:
  - Intellectually honest history from someone working in the field. In the interview Wildcroft describes it as a deliberately edited history, with examples chosen because they help practice.
  - It is the only manual that helps teachers become **independent of scripts**, which matches the stated purpose of your own project.
  - Its accessibility guidance is concrete and usable. Examples: don't promise safety, state the boundaries; the "whose right hand" consistency rule; grounding before and after to counter dissociation.
  - Compact (208 pp.) and modern (2026), and it includes the audio examples a reader needs to hear the voice.
  - The authors bring long practical experience (about 15 and 40 years of teaching). The manuscript was tested in six live "nidra lab" sessions with subscribers in 2025, and the authors asked readers to send in their own recordings.
- Limitations / critiques (no independent reviews exist yet):
  - **No independent reception yet.** As of 2 Oct 2026: 0 ratings on Goodreads and no journal or magazine reviews found. The only published praise is a blurb from Jivana Heyman (Accessible Yoga). Wildcroft introduced him as a dear friend in a 2024 "Shout Out" post on her Substack, so this is a friend's endorsement, not an independent review. The NBN host, Raj Balkaran, was warm and gave no critique.
  - **The evidence is about sleep, not about outcomes.** The science chapter covers sleep physiology (circadian rhythm, melatonin, NREM/REM, brainwaves, hypnagogia). Google's term index also lists **"binaural beat"** and **"beta waves"**. I could not check how these are handled. Compendium `06` warns that brainwave explanations are often simplified and that the EEG evidence mostly shows alpha/theta.
  - **The research may be dated on arrival.** The manuscript was submitted at the end of September 2025, so the 2025–26 meta-analyses (Ghai 2026, Singh 2026, Dutta 2026) are probably missing.
  - Nolan's earlier public writing on sleep (2024) gives figures for larks and owls (about 40% / 30%) without sources. It also calls NSDR simply a secular label for yoga nidra, a view the compendium (`08` §9) treats as contested. Check whether either survives into the book.
  - **Thin reference list (about 2 pp.).** Weaker for citation-chasing than Dinsmore-Tuli or the scholarly literature.
  - **A position, not a survey.** It openly argues against "one right way" schools (and against NSDR and Somatic Dreaming as proprietary). Readers who want depth in a lineage (sankalpa as seed, turīya, the koshas as a path) will find less here than in Satyananda, Miller or Stanley. How it treats sankalpa is unknown.
  - **UK, yoga-teacher audience.** The authors themselves say some familiarity with yoga is needed.
  - The "safe" in the title can't be guaranteed by any method. The authors' own manual is more careful: it aims to start from as safe a place as possible, and it warns teachers never to tell a class that they are totally safe.

## For your own book
- Take:
  - **The process / structure split** as an architecture. Explain what each phase *does* (settle → body → breath → feeling → image → rest → return) before giving any script. This is the key move that lets readers compose their own practices, and it fits the gross-to-subtle "common skeleton" in `04` §4.7.
  - **Their concrete accessibility rules** (from the manual extract). Use them as a checklist next to Luu 2024 [@luu2024]: state the length and contents up front; invite one thing at a time; always name an alternative to breath work; keep imagery optional and short; be consistent about whose hand you name; ground at the start and the end to counter dissociation; watch over the room instead of promising safety.
  - **The history line** (yoganidrā as state and goddess → 19th-century relaxationism → Satyananda's synthesis). It confirms the "ancient roots, modern form" position (`08` §1), and a practitioner audience has now accepted it in print.
  - **The stance on sleep** ("sleep is fine, manage the snoring") and the idea of rest as access and justice. Both suit an inclusive, non-guilt-inducing book.
  - **Craft features worth copying:** memorisation aids for rotation routes; QR-linked audio extracts placed in the text at the point of explanation; a short scripted example alongside each technique; a co-led (two-voice) recording.
  - **Its framing for your proposal:** this book is now your most important *comparative title*. Wildcroft's own posts on writing a book say a proposal must list comparative titles and say what is unique about yours. So decide clearly how yours differs: more depth on sankalpa and the spiritual layer, more research, or a ready-made practice program.
- Skip or treat critically:
  - **Don't borrow its science for claims about benefits.** Use the 2025–26 meta-analyses in `06` §6.0 instead, and check any brainwave or binaural-beat statements against `06` §6.1 before echoing them.
  - **The "proprietary schools" critique.** If you make it, be specific and documented (rc2016, pankhania2017 [@rc2016; @pankhania2017]) rather than tonal, and be fair to iRest and Total Yoga Nidra, which publish their structures.
  - **"Trauma-informed" vs. "safe and simple".** Their reason for dropping the label is sound, but your readers may include clinicians who expect trauma-informed vocabulary. You can keep both: state the principles and avoid overclaiming.
  - **Sankalpa and the depth layer.** Until you have checked chapter 6, don't assume the book handles sankalpa well. For that stage, rely on Satyananda, Miller and Stanley (`08` §8).
  - **Simplicity as the only ideal.** It fits group classes with mixed needs well. Your book may also want an advanced track (e.g. Himalayan conscious-sleep practice), which this book deliberately leaves out.
