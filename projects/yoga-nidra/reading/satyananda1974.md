## In one paragraph
This is a beginner-to-intermediate meditation manual from the Bihar School of Yoga, put together from Swami Satyananda's teaching and edited by a disciple. It gives the theory of meditation (a layered model of mind, raja yoga, other yogas), then preparation (postures, mudras and bandhas, pranayama), then about 13 practice chapters: japa, ajapa japa, **yoga nidra**, antar mouna, inner visualization, chidakasha dharana, trataka, nada yoga, prana vidya and kundalini kriyas. Many come with transcripts of live classes. Its central promise, set out in the Foreword, is that these are tantric techniques forgotten for millennia and recovered by Satyananda in a form modern people can practise. For your project, what matters is that it is one of the earliest Bihar publications, two years before the dedicated *Yoga Nidra* (1976) [@satyananda1976], in which yoga nidra appears in print with class transcripts. It sits as one tool in a tantric toolkit of inward-turning practices, not yet as a stand-alone method with its own book.

## Core ideas
- **Tantra is the source of yoga.** The Foreword presents tantra as older than the world religions and as the hidden basis of yoga. It says the book's practices are tantric methods, many forgotten for thousands of years, that Satyananda alone rediscovered and put into usable form. In the same period Satyananda told a Copenhagen audience that yoga nidra, antar mouna, ajapa japa, trataka and nada yoga are all "offshoots" of tantra (*YOGA*, Feb 1975).
- **Meditation means exploring the layers of the mind.** Chapter 1 maps a "lower" mind (instincts, complexes, phobias), a "middle" rational mind and a "higher" superconscious mind, and adds Jung's collective unconscious. Behind them all is the Self. Meditation takes awareness through these layers, and finally beyond the mind.
- **Disturbing material is expected.** Beginners may meet grotesque images, fears and complexes they did not know they had. The book treats this as the lower mind becoming visible, material to be cleared before deeper states are possible (pp. 10–11).
- **Change your mind, and use autosuggestion to do it.** The Preface and chapter 5 ("Reprogram Your Mind") openly use the Western language of **autosuggestion** and "psychic desensitization" for removing complexes. This bears on the sankalpa and on the "is it hypnosis?" debate (`08` §10).
- **Passive and active meditation.** Seated practice ("passive") is training for meditative awareness in daily action ("active"). Passive meditation develops in stages: fixing the mind, then a free flow of unconscious material, then exploring the "superconscious".
- **Practice over reading.** The Introduction tells readers to try a few methods rather than read about others' experiences, and the Preface warns that real meditation takes time.
- **A graded system.** Preparation (asana, mudra, pranayama) comes before the practices, and the practices themselves move from mantra and breath (japa, ajapa) through pratyahara (yoga nidra, antar mouna) to concentration (visualization, chidakasha, trataka) and kriya. The publisher's blurb calls antar mouna the "fundamental pratyahara" practice and lists yoga nidra among "other meditation techniques". Later Bihar writing labels yoga nidra itself a pratyahara technique (*YOGA*, 1977 and 1998).
- **Taught in a community.** The class transcripts assume a residential course: Om chanted as a group, references to the others practising in the same hall, and to the trataka session held before dawn that day (pp. 263–265).

## The practice as this book teaches it
**What I could not see.** The yoga nidra chapter itself (pp. 181–210) was not in any legitimate preview. So I cannot report its stage sequence, its rotation order, its sankalpa wording, whether it says "do not sleep", or how long its transcripts run. Everything below is either (a) what the sample pages show about how the book teaches practices in general, or (b) evidence from the same years. Treat it as a reading guide for the chapter, not a summary of it.

**(a) How the book presents a practice, using antar trataka (pp. 262–265) as the model.**
- **Two versions of each practice.** First a written, numbered-stage instruction ("Stage 1: Preparation … Stage 2 … Stage 3: Inner visualization"), ending with "Hari Om Tat Sat". Then a **"CLASS PRACTICE"** transcript of Satyananda's spoken guidance for the same practice. The 1976 *Yoga Nidra* also "includes several class transcriptions" (publisher's description), so the format carries straight over.
- **Language.** Short imperative lines, one instruction per line (become aware of the body; stop all movement). The voice is directive, but it builds in permission and alternatives: if you cannot see an image, imagine it; if it appears and vanishes, that does not matter, keep watching. The transcripts also give practical aids, such as touching the eyebrow centre with a moistened finger to locate the point.
- **Opening.** Settle in a sitting position, no more movement, Om chanted together several times and felt as vibration through the whole body, then awareness of the whole body for steadiness.
- **Imagery.** Rapid shifts between images: a single star in a cloudy sky, a candle flame on the desk, a shivalingam, a human eye, the "third eye" as a closed fleshy gland behind the eyebrows, a crystal ball, one's *ishta devata*. Some images come from the group's shared day: students are asked to recall the early-morning trataka session they all took part in. The same rapid-image method is the visualization stage of the 1976 yoga nidra.

**(b) Evidence from the same years about yoga nidra in Bihar teaching.**
- **Satyananda, Copenhagen speech, printed Feb 1975, soon after the book.** He presents yoga nidra ("psychic sleep") as a system from the tantra shastras that he first revealed in **1962**, while revising tantric texts. He says he discovered it rather than invented it. He speaks of **many** yoga nidras ("thousands" in the tantras), including one built on about 670 sounds. He lists three purposes: astral projection, long meditation, and rest and self-healing. The body survey **begins with the thumb, then the second, third, fourth and fifth fingers, then the palm**, and later goes on to sensing colours and emotions. He adds that yoga nidra can be done standing, sitting, eyes open or closed, as well as lying down. The single form he offers the public, he says, is one "taken from the body of Tantra" for everyday well-being.
- **Swami Janakananda (Satyananda disciple, founder of the Scandinavian Yoga and Meditation School).** He says Satyananda began teaching yoga nidra in **1963** and that in **1968** he learned "different Yoga Nidra variations" from him in person. Note the dates: 1962 (Copenhagen), 1963 (Janakananda), and the 1964 convention talk ("I discovered yoga nidra for myself"; second-hand, `01`, [@satyananda1965]).
- **Swami Shankardevananda, "Insomnia", *YOGA* Sept 1977.** Yoga nidra is done in shavasana. It goes through the body part by part, which the article explains with the body map in the motor and sensory cortex: the homunculus explanation is already there in 1977. It calls the practice "a meditative practice designed to induce pratyahara", in which the mind stays awake while the body sleeps. The sankalpa is "a short dynamic statement", planted when the subconscious opens (example: "I will sleep better"). For insomnia it advises practising on a full stomach. It also claims that **half an hour of yoga nidra equals two hours of sleep**.
- **Outside the Bihar school but in its magazine (Mar 1976).** Yogacharya K. B. Sahastrabudhe's "scientific shavasana" already combined a strong resolve with the instruction not to sleep but to keep consciousness awake. These elements were in general circulation in Indian yoga writing, not unique to Satyananda.

**Classification.** The book places yoga nidra among the "Practices for Meditation", derived from tantra. "Pratyahara" is explicitly attached to antar mouna in the blurb, and to yoga nidra in Bihar writing of 1977 and 1998. Check whether the chapter itself uses the word.

## What's distinctive
- **The earliest Bihar book-length setting for yoga nidra that I could confirm (1974), before the 1976 monograph.** It shows yoga nidra *inside a whole meditation curriculum*, next to antar mouna, ajapa japa, trataka and chidakasha dharana. No later yoga nidra book (Miller, Dinsmore-Tuli, Stanley, Desai) gives you that view of where the practice first sat. Satyananda's *Tantra-yoga panorama* (also 1974) reportedly mentions yoga nidra too (Wikipedia citation, not checked), so "earliest" needs care.
- **Class transcripts in a general meditation book.** You can compare how Satyananda talks people through yoga nidra with how he talks them through trataka or antar mouna, and see which verbal habits belong to his teaching voice and which to yoga nidra.
- **Autosuggestion named openly.** The 1970s editorial framing uses autosuggestion and psychic desensitization in plain terms, while the lineage later insists that yoga nidra is not hypnosis (`08` §10).
- **The "tantric rediscovery" origin story in its early form**, before the 1976 book's nyāsa explanation became standard.
- **A record of re-editing.** The YPT text points readers to autosuggestion "in chapter 4" (Preface) and to complex-removal in "chapter 5" (p. 11), while the current contents put "Reprogram Your Mind" at chapter 5. That suggests the chapters were renumbered between editions. The page count also changed (313 → 299 → 367). Don't assume the YPT text is the 1974 text.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - It shows yoga nidra's original setting: a tool among pratyahara and dharana practices, aimed at meditation, not only at relaxation.
  - The verbatim transcripts preserve the teacher's actual spoken style and its built-in alternatives (imagine what you cannot see), which you can study as scriptwriting.
  - It is broad and practical: about 13 practice chapters with staged instructions; readers describe it as a lucid, practical toolbox of techniques (Goodreads, 4.32 average from 118 ratings and 3 short reviews).
  - It is in print and cheap, and translated into French (1983) and German (2021).
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **No serious published review found.** Reception evidence is limited to Goodreads and retailer blurbs; no journal or long-form review turned up.
  - **Historical claims are not credible by today's scholarship.** The Foreword says tantra predates all religions, that the Aryans wedded it to Vedanta, and that the practices were forgotten for millennia and recovered by Satyananda alone. Birch & Hargreaves show that the modern yoga nidra structure is largely an innovation, and that the sankalpa stage in particular looks inspired by Western relaxation therapy [@birch2015]. The Yoga Nidrā Network notes that Boyes (1973) predates Satyananda's 1976 book and that Western relaxation methods clearly shaped the method.
  - **Dated psychology and neuro-claims.** The Introduction repeats the claim that 95–99% of mental capacity lies unused (while conceding the figures are merely illustrative). Chapter 1 uses a lower/middle/higher-mind model with Jung. Same-period Bihar writing adds the cortex-map explanation and a sleep-equivalence claim (see `06` §6.3).
  - **Safety.** The book treats fears, complexes and grotesque images surfacing in meditation as material to be removed by further practice and autosuggestion. There is no trauma-informed caution in the pages I saw (compare `07`).
  - **Directive, male-generic, guru-centred voice** (he, man; the mantra should ideally come from your guru).
  - **Lineage context.** The publisher marks "Yoga Nidra™" and "Antar Mouna™" on its site, and the German publisher uses "Satyananda Yoga Nidra®". Citing the lineage also brings in the misconduct history covered in `01` and `08` §6.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The dating fact for your history chapter.** Bihar School of Yoga, Monghyr, **1st ed. 1974** (Library of Congress record; 2nd ed. 1975, 3rd 1977, 5th 1983). The "1975" in some catalogues is the second edition. Use it to show that yoga nidra was being taught and printed as one tantric meditation tool before it became "the" Satyananda method in 1976.
  - **The 1975 Copenhagen speech** (free, official archive) as a primary source alongside the 1964 talk. In it Satyananda himself says there were *many* yoga nidras, including seated, standing and eyes-open forms, and that the public version is one selected form. That supports a "family of practices, not one fixed script" position (`08` §2 and §7). It also adds a third date for the "discovery" (1962 vs. 1963 vs. 1964). That inconsistency is worth one honest sentence.
  - **The 1977 sleep-equivalence claim** (30 minutes = 2 hours of sleep) in the Bihar School's own magazine. It pushes the lineage origin of that myth back from 1983 [@akhandjyoti1983] to at least 1977. Add it to the myths box in `06` §6.3.
  - **The two-layer format:** explain each stage briefly, then give the spoken script. This is a good model for your own book's practice chapters.
  - **The transcripts' built-in permissions** (if you cannot see the image, imagine it; if it vanishes, that is fine). These show that even the "directive" lineage voice already eased the pressure on the student. Useful evidence if you write about directive vs. invitational language (`07` §7.4).
  - **Placement next to antar mouna.** A natural "what next" chapter for readers: yoga nidra (body, sensation, image) leading into antar mouna (witnessing thought).
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **The Foreword's history** (tantra older than all religions; Aryans; sole rediscovery). Quote it, if at all, as the lineage's self-description, then give the scholarly view [@birch2015; @singleton2005].
  - **"Reprogramming" and autosuggestion as therapy for complexes.** Don't promise this. If you use the book, use it as evidence that the sankalpa has roots in autosuggestion (`08` §10), not as a method claim.
  - **Neuro- and capacity myths** (95–99% unused mind; the cortex-map idea that rotation puts disturbed brain circuits back in order; half an hour = two hours of sleep). Leave them out, or quote them as historical claims and say so.
  - **The YPT text as a stand-in for 1974.** If you write that "the 1974 transcripts already had X", check it in a 1970s printing. Until then, say only what the 1975–77 magazine articles show.
  - **Astral projection and reawakening degenerated brain centres** (1975 speech). Historically interesting, but not something to pass on.
