## In one paragraph
This is a beginner-to-intermediate meditation manual from the Bihar School of Yoga, put together from Swami Satyananda's teaching and edited by a disciple. In the current YPT text it gives the theory of meditation (a layered model of mind, raja yoga, other yogas), then preparation (postures, mudras and bandhas, pranayama), then about 13 practice chapters: japa, ajapa japa, **yoga nidra**, antar mouna, inner visualization, chidakasha dharana, trataka, nada yoga, prana vidya and kundalini kriyas. Its central promise, set out in the Foreword, is that these are tantric techniques forgotten for millennia and recovered by Satyananda in a form modern people can practise. For your project, it is one of the earliest Bihar publications on yoga nidra, two years before the dedicated *Yoga Nidra* (1976) [@satyananda1976]. Its ~30-page yoga nidra chapter (YPT edition) probably includes class transcripts, as the antar trataka chapter does, but I could not see it. Yoga nidra sits here as one tool in a tantric toolkit of inward-turning practices, not yet as a stand-alone method with its own book.

## Core ideas
- **Tantra as the root.** The Foreword presents tantra as older than the world religions and the esoteric basis of many of them, and says yoga arose when the Aryans wedded tantra to Vedanta. It says the book's practices are tantric methods, many forgotten for thousands of years, that Satyananda alone rediscovered and put into usable form. In the same period Satyananda told a Copenhagen audience that yoga nidra, antar mouna, ajapa japa, trataka and nada yoga are all "offshoots" of tantra (*YOGA*, Feb 1975).
- **Meditation means exploring the layers of the mind.** Chapter 1 maps a "lower" mind (instincts, complexes, phobias), a "middle" rational mind and a "higher" superconscious mind, and adds Jung's collective unconscious. Behind them all is the Self. Meditation takes awareness through these layers, and finally beyond the mind.
- **Disturbing material is expected.** Beginners may meet grotesque images, fears and complexes they did not know they had. The book treats this as the lower mind becoming visible, material to be cleared before deeper states are possible (pp. 10–11).
- **Change your mind, and use autosuggestion to do it.** The Introduction openly uses the Western language of **autosuggestion** and "psychic desensitization" for removing complexes, and refers readers to a later chapter for the method (it says chapter 4; p. 11 says chapter 5, the current "Reprogram Your Mind", which I have not seen). The Preface ends on the same note: change your mind, and the method is meditation. This bears on the sankalpa and on the "is it hypnosis?" debate (`08` §10).
- **Passive and active meditation.** Seated practice ("passive") is training for meditative awareness in daily action ("active"). Chapter 1 says passive meditation has four stages, of which the sample (p. 12) shows the first three: fixing the mind on one object, then a free flow of unconscious material that can be examined and cleared, then exploring the "superconscious".
- **Practice over reading.** The Introduction tells readers to try a few methods rather than read about others' experiences, and warns that true meditation takes practice and will not come at the first attempt.
- **An implied progression (my reading, not the book's words).** Preparation (asana, mudra, pranayama) comes before the practices. Read from the contents order, the practices seem to move from mantra and breath (japa, ajapa) through inward-turning practices (yoga nidra, antar mouna) to concentration (visualization, chidakasha, trataka) and kriya. No sampled page sets this out. The publisher's blurb calls antar mouna the "fundamental pratyahara" practice and lists yoga nidra among "other meditation techniques". Later Bihar writing labels yoga nidra itself a pratyahara technique (*YOGA*, 1977 and 1998).
- **Taught in a community.** The antar trataka class transcript suggests an intensive (probably residential, though no page says so) course: Om chanted as a group in a hall, and a reference to the 4 a.m. trataka session the group had practised (pp. 263–265).

## The practice as this book teaches it
**What I could not see.** The yoga nidra chapter itself (pp. 181–210 in the YPT edition) was not in any legitimate preview, and the 1974 text was not readable at all. So I cannot report its stage sequence, its rotation order, its sankalpa wording, whether it says "do not sleep", or whether it has transcripts. The only direct evidence about the 1974 text is the Google Books record of the 1974 Univ. of Michigan copy: its "Common terms and phrases" include "yoga nidra", "sleep", "toes", "relax", "pratyahara", "whole body" and "stage". That supports yoga nidra appearing in the 1974 text and suggests body-part language somewhere in the book; it does not show class transcripts or any particular stage. Everything below is either (a) what the YPT sample pages show about how the book teaches some practices, or (b) evidence from the same years. Treat it as a reading guide for the chapter, not a summary of it.

**(a) How the book presents a practice: antar trataka (pp. 262–265) as one example.**
- **Two versions, for antar trataka.** For antar trataka, the sample pages show two versions. First a written instruction, of which the sample shows "Stage 3: Inner visualization", ending with "Hari Om Tat Sat" (p. 262). Then a **"CLASS PRACTICE"** transcript of spoken guidance for the same practice, with its own headings: Stage 1: Preparation / Stage 2: Awareness of eyebrow centre / Stage 3: Inner visualization (p. 263). The japa sample pages (154–157) give prose explanation plus a bulleted list of rules, and the kundalini kriya pages (320–323) give numbered written instructions only, with no transcript. So the two-version format cannot be assumed for every practice or for the yoga nidra chapter. The 1976 *Yoga Nidra* also includes class transcriptions (publisher's description).
- **Language.** Short imperative lines, one instruction per line (become aware of the body; stop all movement). The voice is directive but offers alternatives. The written instruction (p. 262) lets the student imagine an image they cannot see and tells them not to worry if it does not last. The class transcript (p. 263) offers a fallback: if the star cannot be visualized or imagined, use a simpler point of light instead. Both versions give a practical aid for locating the eyebrow centre: touching it with a moistened finger.
- **Opening (class transcript).** Settle in a sitting position, no more movement, Om chanted together several times and felt as vibration through the whole body, then awareness of the whole body for steadiness.
- **Imagery.** Rapid shifts between images: a single star in a cloudy sky, a candle flame on the desk, a shivalingam, a human eye, the "third eye" as a closed fleshy gland behind the eyebrows, a crystal ball, one's *ishta devata*. Some images come from the group's shared experience: students are asked to recall the trataka practised at 4 o'clock in the morning. The same rapid-image method is the visualization stage of the 1976 yoga nidra.

**(b) Evidence from the same years about yoga nidra in Bihar teaching.**
- **Satyananda, Copenhagen speech, printed Feb 1975, soon after the book.** He presents yoga nidra ("psychic sleep") as a system from the tantra shastras that he first revealed in **1962**, while revising tantric texts. He says he discovered it rather than invented it. He speaks of **many** yoga nidras ("thousands" in the tantras), including one built on about 670 sounds. He lists three purposes: astral projection, long meditation, and rest and self-healing. The body survey **begins with the thumb, then the second, third, fourth and fifth fingers, then the palm**, and later goes on to sensing colours and emotions. He adds that yoga nidra can be done standing, sitting, eyes open or closed, as well as lying down. The single form he offers the public, he says, is one "taken from the body of Tantra" for everyday well-being.
- **Swami Janakananda (Satyananda's disciple; he calls Satyananda "my teacher").** He says Satyananda began teaching yoga nidra as early as **1963** and that in **1968** he learned "different Yoga Nidra variations" from him in person. Note the dates: 1962 (Copenhagen), 1963 (Janakananda), and the 1964 convention talk ("I discovered yoga nidra for myself"; second-hand, `01`, [@satyananda1965]).
- **Swami Shankardevananda, "Insomnia", *YOGA* Sept 1977.** Yoga nidra is done in shavasana. It goes through the body part by part, which the article explains with the body map in the motor and sensory cortex, claiming this relaxes the brain and puts disturbed circuits "back into order": the homunculus explanation is already there in 1977. It calls the practice "a meditative practice designed to induce pratyahara", in which the mind stays awake while the body sleeps, and says alpha waves increase. It cites the cardiologist K. K. Datey's report that relaxation in shavasana helped hypertensive patients (headache, giddiness, irritability and insomnia disappearing in almost all). The sankalpa is "a short dynamic statement", planted when the subconscious opens (example, in the future tense: "I will sleep better"). For insomnia it advises practising on a full stomach. It also claims that **half an hour of yoga nidra equals two hours of sleep**.
- **A Satyananda follower in the same magazine (Mar 1976).** Yogacharya K. B. Sahastrabudhe's "scientific shavasana" combined a strong resolve (worded partly "I am improving…", partly "I will…") with the instruction not to sleep but to keep consciousness awake, and claimed that 20–40 minutes of shavasana gives rest "almost twice as deep" as a whole night's deep sleep. He calls Satyananda "our Gurudev" and credits him with inventing this shavasana in Bihar "14 years ago" (so c. 1962, the same year Satyananda gives in the Copenhagen speech). So the article is evidence from within the Satyananda/Bihar orbit of resolve, staying awake and a sleep-equivalence claim by 1976, not evidence that these ideas were in general circulation.

**Classification.** The YPT edition places yoga nidra among the "Practices for Meditation", derived from tantra. "Pratyahara" is explicitly attached to antar mouna in the blurb, and to yoga nidra in Bihar writing of 1977 and 1998; "pratyahara" is also among the common terms of the 1974 copy. ## What's distinctive
- **Yoga nidra inside a full meditation curriculum, at length.** Like Janakananda's manual (1975), which this project calls the clearest example of yoga nidra embedded in a graded curriculum (`reading/janakananda1975.md`), it places yoga nidra as an inward-turning (pratyahara-type) tool just before antar mouna. Unlike Janakananda, it gives the practice about 30 pages (YPT edition) rather than one or two, possibly with a class transcript (not confirmed). Unlike Boyes (1973) [@boyes1973], a stand-alone relaxation-method book, it frames yoga nidra as meditation rooted in tantra. It is the earliest Bihar book setting for yoga nidra I could confirm (1974; yoga nidra is among the 1974 copy's common terms). Satyananda's *Tantra-yoga panorama* (also 1974) reportedly mentions yoga nidra too (Wikipedia citation, not checked), and his 1964 talk predates both, so "earliest" needs care.
- **Class transcripts in a general meditation book.** Where the book has transcripts, you can compare how Satyananda talks people through different practices and see which verbal habits belong to his teaching voice. Whether the yoga nidra chapter has one is not confirmed.
- **Autosuggestion named openly.** In the current YPT text, the Introduction uses autosuggestion and psychic desensitization in plain terms, while the lineage later insists that yoga nidra is not hypnosis (`08` §10). Whether the 1974 text already used these words is not checked.
- **The "tantric rediscovery" origin story.** The YPT Foreword states it; the 1975 Copenhagen speech shows the story was current in Bihar teaching by then. A 1998 Bihar article (*YOGA*, July 1998) says the tantric nyāsa concentration and meditation techniques were first presented "in their basic form" in this book, and credits Satyananda with bringing nyāsa into yoga in the form of yoga nidra. That is a later lineage claim; check in the text whether the 1974 book (and its yoga nidra chapter) actually names nyāsa, or whether the nyāsa explanation first appears in the 1976 *Yoga Nidra*. Whether the 1974 Foreword had the same wording is not checked.
- **A record of re-editing.** The YPT Introduction points readers to autosuggestion "in chapter 4" and p. 11 points to complex-removal in "chapter 5", while the current contents put "Reprogram Your Mind" at chapter 5. That suggests chapters were renumbered between editions. The page count also changed (313 → 299 → 367). Don't assume the YPT text is the 1974 text.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - It shows yoga nidra's setting in Bihar teaching of the mid-1970s: a tool among inward-turning and concentration practices, aimed at meditation, not only at relaxation.
  - Where transcripts exist (antar trataka), they preserve the teacher's spoken style, including a fallback for students who cannot visualize ("visualize or imagine … if this is not possible", p. 263), which you can study as scriptwriting.
  - It is broad and practical: about 13 practice chapters with staged instructions; readers describe it as a lucid, practical toolbox of techniques (Goodreads, 4.32 average from 118 ratings and 3 short reviews).
  - It is in print (about US$19–51 depending on retailer), and translated into French (1983) and German (2021).
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **No serious published review found.** Reception evidence is limited to Goodreads and retailer blurbs; no journal or long-form review turned up.
  - **Historical claims are not credible by today's scholarship.** The Foreword says tantra predates all religions, that the Aryans wedded it to Vedanta, and that the practices were forgotten for millennia and recovered by Satyananda alone. Birch & Hargreaves describe Satyananda's yoga nidra as a new systemisation of techniques drawn from medieval and modern sources. They find medieval precedents for several stages (śavāsana, nyāsa-like rotation, marmasthāna pratyāhāra, visualisation), but say the sankalpa stage "appears to be an innovation" inspired by Western relaxation therapies [@birch2015]. The Yoga Nidrā Network notes that Boyes (1973) predates Satyananda's 1976 book and that Western relaxation methods clearly shaped the method.
  - **Dated psychology and neuro-claims.** The Introduction repeats the claim that 95–99% of mental capacity lies unused (while conceding the figures are merely illustrative). Chapter 1 uses a lower/middle/higher-mind model with Jung. Same-period Bihar writing adds the cortex-map explanation and a sleep-equivalence claim (see the table below). Chapters 2 ("Science, Spirit and Meditation") and 3 ("Meditation and Health") are likely places for further health or EEG claims; I have not seen them.
  - **Safety.** The book treats fears, complexes and grotesque images surfacing in meditation as material to be removed by further practice and autosuggestion. There is no trauma-informed caution in the pages I saw (compare `07`).
  - **Directive, male-generic, guru-centred voice** (he, man; the mantra should ideally come from your guru).
  - **Lineage context.** The publisher marks "Yoga Nidra™" and "Antar Mouna™" on its site, and the German publisher uses "Satyananda Yoga Nidra®". Citing the lineage also brings in the misconduct history covered in `01` and `08` §6.

**Claims vs. evidence** (claims from the book's front matter and the same-period *YOGA* articles, checked against the compendium):

| Claim (source) | Evidence today |
|---|---|
| ½ hour of yoga nidra = 2 hours of sleep (1977); 20–40 min of Satyananda's "scientific shavasana" "almost twice as deep" as a night's sleep (1976, Sahastrabudhe, a follower crediting Satyananda) | No evidence; no study measures it (`06` §6.3) |
| Alpha waves increase (1977) | Roughly matches the alpha/theta findings, which come from small studies; only more theta in experienced practitioners is consistent (`06` §6.1) |

## For your own book
**Verdict:** Not needed for the method (use Satyananda 1976 [@satyananda1976]). Borrow it, check Satyam Yoga Prasad, or buy the YPT edition (about US$19 at Vedicbooks) only if your history chapter needs the yoga nidra chapter or its transcript; for 1974 specifically, a library or the Google Books snippet search is the route. The free 1975 and 1977 *YOGA* articles give most of what is usable now.

- **Take:**
  - **The dating fact for your history chapter.** Bihar School of Yoga, Monghyr, **1st ed. 1974** (Library of Congress record; 2nd ed. 1975, 3rd 1977, 5th 1983). The "1975" in some catalogues is the second edition. Use it, with the Google Books word list, to show that yoga nidra was printed as one tantric meditation tool among many before it became "the" Satyananda method in 1976.
  - **The 1975 Copenhagen speech** (free, official archive) as a primary source alongside the 1964 talk. In it Satyananda himself says there were *many* yoga nidras, including seated, standing and eyes-open forms, and that the public version is one selected form. That supports a "family of practices, not one fixed script" position (`08` §2 and §7). It also adds a third date for the "discovery" (1962 vs. 1963 vs. 1964). That inconsistency is worth one honest sentence.
  - **The sleep-equivalence claims** in the Bihar School's own magazine. The earliest Bihar-lineage instance found is **1976**: Sahastrabudhe, crediting the method to "our Gurudev" Satyananda, says 20–40 minutes of this shavasana gives rest "almost twice as deep" as a night's sleep. In 1977 Shankardevananda states it for yoga nidra by name (30 minutes = 2 hours of sleep). (The 1983 item cited in `06` §6.3 is *Akhand Jyoti* [@akhandjyoti1983], a different organisation.) Add both to the myths box in `06` §6.3.
  - **Sankalpa wording history.** The 1977 example ("I will sleep better") is future tense, matching early Bihar usage; present-tense "I am…" phrasing belongs to later teachers (satyananda1976). Useful for a short history of sankalpa wording; check the tense of resolve examples in the 1974 chapter.
  - **The two-layer format** seen in antar trataka: explain each stage briefly, then give the spoken script. A good model for your own book's practice chapters, whether or not the yoga nidra chapter uses it.
  - **Reassurance in the voice, with a caveat.** The antar trataka instructions let the student imagine what they cannot see and not worry if it fades (written, p. 262), and offer a simpler fallback (transcript, p. 263). But by 1978 the lineage's yoga nidra guidance told teachers never to say "don't worry if you don't see this" (satyananda1976). So this is evidence that the voice varied between practices and years, not that the lineage voice was invitational. Useful if you write about directive vs. invitational language (`07` §7.4).
  - **Placement next to antar mouna.** A natural "what next" chapter for readers: yoga nidra (body, sensation, image) leading into antar mouna (witnessing thought).
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **The Foreword's history** (tantra older than all religions; Aryans; sole rediscovery). Quote it, if at all, as the lineage's self-description, then give the scholarly view [@birch2015; @singleton2005].
  - **"Reprogramming" and autosuggestion as therapy for complexes.** Don't promise this. If you use the book, use it as evidence that the sankalpa has roots in autosuggestion (`08` §10), not as a method claim.
  - **Neuro- and capacity myths** (95–99% unused mind; rotation restoring disturbed brain circuits; half an hour = two hours of sleep). Leave them out, or quote them as historical claims and say so. The cortex-map idea itself can be offered as a metaphor (`06` §6.3).
  - **The YPT text as a stand-in for 1974.** If you write that "the 1974 text already had X", check it in a 1970s printing or the 1974 snippet search. Until then, say only what the 1975–77 magazine articles show.
  - **Astral projection and reawakening degenerated brain centres** (1975 speech). Historically interesting, but not something to pass on.
  - **Trademark styling.** Don't use "Satyananda Yoga Nidra®" or "Yoga Nidra™" styling for your own practice; describe your method in your own words and credit the lineage in prose.
