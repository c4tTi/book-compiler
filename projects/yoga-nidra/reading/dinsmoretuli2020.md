## In one paragraph
This is a practical beginner's manual. Its aim is that you can do yoga nidra **without a teacher, a trademarked method or, in the end, a recording**. The authors compress their Total Yoga Nidra training into a "nine-ingredient" cyclical process. They draw it as a labyrinth and teach it as a recipe that is "cooked" the same way each time but tastes different. Part I gives one chapter to each ingredient, preceded by a short history. Part II applies the ingredients to sleep, stress and pain, and vitality and creativity, then offers a staged plan for practising solo. The voice is warm, invitational and anti-guru. Practices are written as first-person, rhyming verse and as questions ("ask, don't tell"). The audience is the general reader who is tired, stressed or curious, but the authors also address teachers who want a less prescriptive way to guide. The promise is that yoga nidra is a natural state you have already been in, and that the book only reminds you how to return to it.

## Core ideas
- **Yoga nidra is a state, not a technique.** You cannot "do" it; methods only set up the conditions for a state everyone has already met while falling asleep and waking (pp. 8–9). This is the "state" side of debate 2 in `08`.
- **A nine-part cyclical process in a fixed order.** Each ingredient always comes in the same place, but every practice "tastes" different (pp. 11–13). The labyrinth diagram puts **paradox, the opposites, at the centre** (p. 14). Note: this contradicts compendium `04` §4.4, which says Total Yoga Nidra treats the elements as "a menu, not a sequence". In this book the sequence is fixed and only the content of each ingredient varies.
- **No standard script.** The book teaches you to compose your own practice. A standard script is said to flatten the "adaptogenic" quality of the practice: the same practice gives different people what they need (pp. 19–20).
- **Ask, don't tell.** Facilitators should not tell people they are relaxed or blissful. They should ask and offer choices. Agency is the key difference from "linear" methods (pp. 22–23).
- **Get off the recording.** Memory aids include rhyme and repetition, a labyrinth finger-trace and a staged self-guiding plan. With them the reader should eventually practise "in the wordless voice of your own knowing" (pp. 22–25, 157–171).
- **Anti-trademark, anti-guru.** Yoga nidra cannot be owned. Brand histories that put one guru at the origin are, in the authors' view, untruthful. Every modern method is a **hybrid of Indian traditions and Western relaxation science** (pp. 10, 37, 43, 46).
- **The goddess comes first.** The book roots the practice in Yoganidrā Shakti Devī, the goddess of sleep in the *Devī Māhātmya* and *Rāmāyaṇa* stories (pp. 37–40). It sets this beside the Western relaxation lineage: Annie Payson Call 1891, Vittoz 1911, Coué 1924, Jacobson 1934, Benson 1975 (pp. 41–42).
- **Rest as a radical, social act.** The book frames rest as resistance to constant doing. It cites Tricia Hersey's Nap Ministry (p. 49) and speaks of inherited, ancestral fatigue (p. 48).
- **Unusual candour about hypnosis.** Settling, body rotation and opposites are named as classic trance-induction devices. The returning intention (ingredient 7) is likened to a **posthypnotic suggestion**. The claimed difference is that in yoga nidra you guide yourself into trance (pp. 133–135).

## The practice as this book teaches it
**Overall shape.** Nine ingredients, always in this order:

1. preparing
2. settling
3. inner listening / intention
4. body journey
5. opposites
6. imaginative capacity (optional)
7. inner listening / intention again
8. externalizing
9. completion

**Length.** About 15–20 minutes is typical for everyday use. The shortest practice is about 7 minutes; long ones run 45 or 90 minutes. The authors explain the effects through ultradian cycles: short practices leave you alert, while longer ones can leave you groggy unless you complete a full 90 minutes (p. 24). Practices over half an hour can leave you foggy for up to 10 minutes afterwards (p. 148). The opening read-aloud practice takes nine minutes; companion tracks run about 9–28 minutes.

**Posture and comfort (the "nidrā nest", Ch 2).**
- **Space:** clean, dry and warm, with room to stretch the arms.
- **Lying down:** you do not have to lie flat. Knees bent, side-lying, belly-lying or a curled position are all fine (pp. 55, 63).
- **Props:** a pillow or folded blanket under the head, tucked under the tops of the shoulders, and support under the thighs or knees (pp. 61–62).
- **Warmth:** a cover within reach, because body temperature drops, and especially **warm feet** (p. 56).
- **Eyes:** lights dimmed or an eye pillow if you like. The eyes may stay open with a soft gaze (pp. 57, 64).
- **Special groups:** propped side-lying is recommended in pregnancy (p. 52). Side-lying or a curled position often suits teenagers (p. 58).
- **Bed:** you can practise in bed if you make a small change that signals that this is not the same as going to bed to sleep, such as lying on the floor beside it (p. 54).
- **Time of day:** any time you have. The mid-afternoon dip is a good slot (pp. 56, 71).
- **Equipment:** "this book and a timer" (p. 6). With a recording, set the timer **5–10 minutes after the recording ends**, as a buffer for slow returns (pp. 144, 149, 170).

**Language style.** Invitational and questioning. Typical forms are "I wonder…", "how about…" and "Am I cozy enough (especially my feet)?" The core practice is in the **first person** ("I take a pause to lay this body down…") and refers to "**this** body" rather than "your body". It is metred, rhymed verse built to be memorised.

Here is how the body journey is guided, paraphrased: rest is said to "flow" from the crown across brows, eyes, ears, nostrils, lips, tongue and throat. It then travels down the arms to each finger and the webbing between them, through belly, chest, heart, navel and pelvis, and down the legs to the spaces between the toes. Each line ends on a refrain that the place "can rest now". The settling recipe is a string of gentle self-questions about warmth, contact and whether anything needs adjusting.

**Sankalpa handling** is summarised in row 3. The return in row 7 is meant to be curious, not a test of whether the resolve "took".

**Sleep.**
- **Yogic sleep is distinguished from sleep.** Awareness stays present (pp. 174, 180).
- **Reassurance about drifting off.** If you "come back" when the closing instruction comes, you were practising yoga nidra, not asleep (p. 152).
- **There is no blanket permission to sleep.** The book offers stay-awake strategies: come well rested, do two practices back to back, or rest the forearms upright so that one drops if you nod off (p. 204).
- **Napping is honoured when needed.** If you are simply tired, the wise and kind choice is to recognise it and nap (p. 214).
- **Recordings at night.** Use one designed for sleep; one that ends by bringing you back up is jarring (p. 188).
- **Night practices** in Ch 11 include settling deeper into rest (p. 185) and a review of the day before sleep (pp. 186–188).

So the brief's "explicit permission to fall asleep" needs qualifying: the permission is real, but it comes with a clear line between yoga nidra and sleep.

**Self-practice (Ch 10).** Memory games: trace the labyrinth with a fingertip, one breath per ingredient (pp. 158–161). A nine-stage plan then gradually replaces the recording with your own guidance, with no rush. Stage 1 is "owning the start and the end"; Stage 3 is "inviting the inner guide to speak" (pp. 163–164). The authors advise **recording your own voice** (pp. 52, 95, 126).

**Safety notes found.**
- **Trauma:** not all yoga nidra triggers trauma. It depends on the person and the practice. Invitational practices that let you negotiate your own safety are recommended (pp. 60–61), and imposed imagery is a known trigger (p. 115).
- **Depression and anxiety:** some practices help and some aggravate these states. Watch your own responses (p. 174).
- **Breath:** equal-length breaths for anxious people (p. 174).
- **Other situations covered:** surgery recovery (p. 119), pregnancy, teenagers and back pain.
- **Missing:** I found nothing on psychosis or epilepsy (search-inside returned no hits).

## What's distinctive
- **The only book in the set built to make itself unnecessary.** Rhyme, memory games and an eight-track weaning plan aim at practice without any recording. Lusk and Verma, by contrast, are script collections; Satyananda's fixed script is meant to be repeated.
- **A complete, memorisable nine-minute practice in rhymed first-person verse.** It is free to read in the *Psychologies* excerpt and to hear in two voices.
- **The most candid account of hypnosis in the lineage literature.** It names the trance devices and the posthypnotic logic of the returning intention (pp. 133–135). Satyananda and the iRest/Amrit camps tend to insist that it is not hypnosis (`08` debate 10).
- **A critique of the trademarked schools from inside the field, in a mass-market Hay House book.** It covers Satyananda, Swami Rama's Himalayan Institute, Amrit Desai's I AM, Miller's iRest, and offshoots it lists as Stanley, Stryker, Brody and Lusk. It includes an explicit **warning about abuses in all four main schools** (p. 47). Compare Satyananda (silent) and *Nidra Shakti* (the long form of the same critique).
- **Rotation turned upside down:** head-to-toe, downward-flowing, body-inclusive (pelvis named), adaptable for missing parts. The opposites are drawn from present experience.
- **Exiting treated as a skill.** Two chapters cover it, with timer buffers, a 21-point return and "wriggles". Most books give this a paragraph.
- **The labyrinth model**, with opposites at the centre. No other book I know diagrams the practice this way.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - The best model in the set of **invitational, permission-giving language** that still carries a clear structure.
  - Very practical on comfort, props, positions, timing and exits.
  - An honest **historical hybrid story**: Western relaxation from Call to Benson, plus Boyes's 1973 *Le yoga du sommeil éveillé* identified as the first European yoga nidra book (p. 103, n. 18).
  - Debunks the "20 minutes = 2 hours of sleep" myth (pp. 39–40), in line with `06` §6.3.
  - Trauma-aware treatment of imagery; candid on hypnosis and on abuse in the lineages.
  - Generous free audio.
  - Endorsed by Tracee Stanley, Charlie Morley and John Vosler.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **The research is oversold.** To the question of whether research proves yoga nidra works, the answer is "Yes, lots", with "60 studies in the past 50 years" (p. 11). The 2025–26 meta-analyses in `06` §6.0 find low-quality studies whose effects shrink against active controls.
  - **The brain-wave model goes beyond the evidence.** The book says all brain-wave states, beta through delta, are present during practice (p. 9), and it includes recipes for "remaining in delta" and inviting gamma (pp. 200–204). The EEG literature mostly shows alpha and theta; delta with awareness rests on the Swami Rama anecdote (`06` §6.1, §6.3).
  - **The history has inflations and slips that need checking:**
    - The *Devī Māhātmya*'s origins are said to "reach back over 10,000 years" (p. 38). The book itself dates the text to the sixth century on p. 38 but to "the fourth century" on p. 37.
    - The *Yoga Sūtra* is called "seventh-century" (p. 40). Most scholars place it several centuries earlier.
    - Swami Rama's 1971 brain-wave demonstration is placed "in a Texas laboratory" (p. 197). The standard account places it at the Menninger Foundation, Kansas (`06` §6.1).
    - Indigenous dream practices (Aboriginal, Toltec, Tibetan, First Nations, Xhosa) are presented as yoga nidra's "authentic" roots by family resemblance, not documented connection (p. 36).
    - Birch & Hargreaves is not cited (no search hit).
  - **Thin sourcing in places.** Insomnia statistics come from a commercial website (n. 23, p. 237).
  - **The publisher's blurb promises more than the text** ("overcome trauma"). The book itself is careful.
  - **Self-referential.** All the practices come from the authors' own encyclopedia and training manuals (p. 8), and the history ends by positioning Total Yoga Nidra as drawing on "the best of all the four main schools" (p. 44).
  - **Style.** Cooking, labyrinth, adaptogen and goddess metaphors are stacked on top of each other, and the book repeats itself. A Dutch Goodreads reviewer (2 stars) found it easy to read but long-winded and repetitive. Goodreads overall: 4.14 from 56 ratings, two text reviews (October 2026).
  - **No serious reviews found.** I found no reviews in journals or yoga magazines. The *Psychologies* piece is an excerpt, not a review. My web-search budget ran out before I could look further.
  - **Market context.** The "Made Easy" series lists sibling titles on Tarot, Wicca, Mediumship and Self-Hypnosis. That signals a Mind-Body-Spirit shelf, which may matter to clinically minded readers.
  - **Little on contraindications** beyond trauma, depression and anxiety.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The nine-ingredient sequence as a comparison grid.** Its split of sankalpa into an *opening* inner-listening slot and a *closing* "savoring" slot is a clean alternative to the resolve said three times. The three options are **simply being / intention inquiry / embodied intuition**. Together they let you keep the stage and drop the self-improvement pressure (`08` debate 8).
  - **"Settling is the most important ingredient."** Give it real length and FAQ support:
    - eyes may stay open
    - flat on the back is optional
    - pregnancy: side-lying
    - teens: curled positions
    - prop recipes
    - warm feet
    - practising in bed with a signal change
  - **"Ask, don't tell"** as an editing rule for your scripts: never tell readers what they feel.
  - **Opposites taken from what is present** (inside/outside, crown/soles, awake/asleep) rather than asking people to manufacture emotions. It is easier for beginners and safer.
  - **Optional imagery, with the trauma reason stated.**
  - **Exit craft:**
    - timer set 5–10 minutes after the recording ends
    - counting up
    - palming
    - having a drink ready
    - expecting grogginess after 30 minutes or more
    - knowing that leaving can take more than one attempt (p. 139)
  - **A weaning plan** if you want readers to self-guide, and the advice to record their own voice.
  - **Their sleep position:** reassurance (if you came back at the closing cue, you were practising) *plus* honest stay-awake tips. It is a nuanced middle line for `08` debate 3.
  - **The hypnosis passage (pp. 133–135)** as a model of candour for `08` debate 10.
  - **The anti-myth line** that 20 minutes may *feel* like two hours of sleep but is not.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **The research and brain-wave material.** Replace it with `06` §6.0 and its honest one-liner ("better than nothing, about as good as other relaxation"), and drop the delta and gamma "recipes".
  - **The deep-antiquity and Indigenous-roots framing.** Use Birch & Hargreaves, Coburn (whom the authors cite for the goddess) and Singleton for dates and continuity. Present cross-cultural dream practices as parallels, not ancestry. Check every date the book gives before you reuse it.
  - **Metaphors.** Pick **one** governing metaphor; this book shows how stacking them dilutes each one.
  - **The first-person rhymed "this body" voice.** Test it before adopting it. Some readers find it soothing and memorable; others find it sing-song or distancing. Offer it as one register, not the default.
  - **"No standard script" alongside a fixed nine-part order.** Be explicit in your own book about what is fixed and what is free (`08` debate 7).
  - **Safety.** Do not rely on it for contraindications. Add your own section on dissociation, trauma, psychosis and severe depression, with referral guidance.
  - **Marketing claims.** If you cite it, cite the book's careful text, not the publisher's blurb ("overcome trauma").
