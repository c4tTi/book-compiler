## In one paragraph
This is a practical beginner's manual. Its aim is that you can do yoga nidra **without a teacher, a trademarked method or, in the end, a recording**. The authors compress their Total Yoga Nidra training into a "nine-ingredient" cyclical process. They draw it as a labyrinth and teach it as a recipe that is "cooked" the same way each time but tastes different. Part I gives one chapter to each of the nine ingredients; Ch 1 combines a short history with ingredient 1 (preparing). Part II opens with a staged plan for practising solo (Ch 10), then applies the ingredients to sleep, stress and pain, and vitality and creativity (Chs 11–13), and closes with next steps (Ch 14). The voice is warm, invitational and anti-guru. Practices are written as first-person, rhyming verse and as questions ("ask, don't tell"). The audience is the general reader who is tired, stressed or curious, but the authors also address teachers who want a less prescriptive way to guide. The promise is that yoga nidra is a natural state you have already been in, and that the book only reminds you how to return to it.

## Core ideas
- **Yoga nidra is a state, not a technique.** You cannot "do" it; methods only set up the conditions for a state everyone has already met while falling asleep and waking (pp. 8–9). This is the "state" side of debate 2 in `08`.
- **A nine-part cyclical process in a fixed order.** Each ingredient always comes in the same place, but every practice "tastes" different (pp. 11–13). The labyrinth diagram puts **paradox, the opposites, at the centre** (p. 14). Note: this contradicts compendium `04` §4.4, which says Total Yoga Nidra treats the elements as "a menu, not a sequence". In this book the sequence is fixed and only the content of each ingredient varies.
- **No standard script.** The book teaches you to compose your own practice. A standard script is said to flatten the "adaptogenic" quality of the practice: the same practice gives different people what they need (pp. 19–20).
- **Ask, don't tell.** Facilitators should not tell people they are relaxed or blissful. They should ask and offer choices. Agency is the key difference from "linear" methods (pp. 22–23).
- **Get off the recording.** Memory aids include rhyme and repetition, a labyrinth finger-trace and a staged self-guiding plan. With them the reader should eventually be able to practise with no outside voice at all (pp. 22–25, 157–171).
- **Anti-trademark, anti-guru.** Yoga nidra cannot be owned. Brand histories that put one guru at the origin are, in the authors' view, untruthful. Every modern method is a **hybrid of Indian traditions and Western relaxation science** (pp. 10, 37, 43, 46).
- **The goddess comes first.** The book roots the practice in Yoganidrā Shakti Devī, the goddess of sleep in the *Devī Māhātmya* and *Rāmāyaṇa* stories (pp. 37–40). It sets this beside the Western relaxation lineage: Annie Payson Call 1891, Vittoz 1911, Coué 1924, Jacobson 1934, Benson 1975 (pp. 41–42).
- **Rest as a radical, social act.** The book frames rest as resistance to constant doing. It cites Tricia Hersey's Nap Ministry (p. 49) and describes an inherited fatigue: our ancestors once honoured natural cycles of rest and then learned to override them (p. 48).
- **Unusual candour about hypnosis.** Settling, body rotation and opposites are named as classic trance-induction devices. The returning intention (ingredient 7) is likened to a **posthypnotic suggestion**. The claimed difference is that in yoga nidra you guide yourself into trance (pp. 133–135). Nirlipta Tuli held a diploma in clinical hypnotherapy (p. 255), which helps explain the candour.

## The practice as this book teaches it
**Overall shape.** Nine ingredients, always in this order:

1. preparing
2. settling
3. inner listening / intention
4. body journey
5. opposites
6. imaginative capacity (optional)
7. inner listening / intention again
8. externalizing
9. completion

**Length.** About 15–20 minutes is typical for everyday use. The shortest practice is about 7 minutes; long ones run 45 or 90 minutes. The authors explain the effects through ultradian cycles: short practices leave you alert, while longer ones can leave you groggy unless you complete a full 90 minutes (p. 24). Practices over half an hour can leave you foggy for up to 10 minutes afterwards (p. 148). The opening read-aloud practice is billed as nine minutes; companion tracks run about 9–28 minutes.

**The nine-minute practice as a benchmark** ("Welcome home to my rested self", Introduction pp. 25–31; the same text is in the *Psychologies* excerpt, track 1.0). Word counts are my approximate counts from the excerpt's text layer, leaving out headings and page furniture:

**Posture and comfort (the "nidrā nest", Ch 2).**
- **Space:** clean, dry and warm, with room to stretch the arms.
- **Lying down:** you do not have to lie flat. Knees bent, side-lying, belly-lying or a curled position are all fine (pp. 55, 63).
- **Props:** a pillow or folded blanket under the head, tucked under the tops of the shoulders, and support under the thighs or knees (pp. 61–62).
- **Warmth:** a cover within reach, because body temperature drops, and especially **warm feet** (p. 56).
- **Eyes:** lights dimmed or an eye pillow if you like. The eyes may stay open with a soft gaze (pp. 57, 64).
- **Special groups:** propped side-lying is recommended in pregnancy (p. 52). Side-lying or a curled position often suits teenagers (p. 58).
- **Bed:** you can practise in bed if you make a small change that signals that this is not the same as going to bed to sleep, such as lying on the floor beside it (p. 54).
- **Time of day:** any time you have. The mid-afternoon dip is a good slot (pp. 56, 71).
- **Equipment:** just the book and a timer (p. 6). With a recording, set the timer **5–10 minutes after the recording ends**, as a buffer for slow returns (pp. 144, 149, 170).

**Language style.** Invitational and questioning. Typical forms are "I wonder…", "how about…", and self-questions about whether you are warm enough, especially your feet (p. 56). The core practice is in the **first person**, opening with the speaker pausing to lay "this body" down, and it refers to "**this** body" rather than "your body" throughout. It is metred, rhymed verse built to be memorised.

Here is how the body journey is guided in the nine-minute practice (ingredient 4 of the Introduction's practice, pp. 27–29, also in the *Psychologies* excerpt), paraphrased: rest is said to "flow" from the crown across brows, eyes, ears, nostrils, lips, tongue and throat. It then travels down the arms to each finger and the webbing between them, through belly, chest, heart, navel and pelvis, and down the legs to the spaces between the toes. Lines return again and again to refrains of rest ("…can rest now", "…nourishment of rest", "…in yoga nidrā now"), but the endings vary. The settling recipe is a string of gentle self-questions about warmth, contact and whether anything needs adjusting.

**Sankalpa handling** is summarised in row 3. The return in row 7 is meant to be curious, not a test of whether the resolve "took".

**Sleep.**
- **Yogic sleep is distinguished from sleep.** Awareness stays present (pp. 174, 180).
- **The sleep-equivalence myth is rejected outright.** The history chapter says the timelessness of the state is why a short practice can *feel* like hours of sleep, then adds at once that it is not, and promises an explanation in Ch 11 (pp. 39–40; see Short quotes). This agrees with `06` §6.3. I did not see the Ch 11 explanation.
- **Reassurance about drifting off.** If you "come back" when the closing instruction comes, you were practising yoga nidra, not asleep (p. 152).
- **There is no blanket permission to sleep.** The book offers stay-awake strategies: come well rested, do two practices back to back, or rest the forearms upright so that one drops if you nod off (p. 204).
- **Napping is honoured when needed.** If you are simply tired, the wise and kind choice is to recognise it and nap (p. 214).
- **Recordings at night.** Use one designed for sleep; one that ends by bringing you back up is jarring (p. 188).
- **Night practices** in Ch 11 include settling deeper into rest (p. 185) and a review of the day before sleep (pp. 186–188).

So the brief's "explicit permission to fall asleep" needs qualifying: the permission is real, but it comes with a clear line between yoga nidra and sleep.

**Self-practice (Ch 10).** Memory games: trace the labyrinth with a fingertip, one breath per ingredient (pp. 158–161). A nine-stage plan (p. 163) then gradually replaces the recording with your own guidance, with no rush and no need to do the stages on consecutive days. Stage 1 is "owning the start and the end"; Stage 3 is "inviting the inner guide to speak"; Stage 4 is "finding your own way around" (p. 164). Eight of the stages have audio tracks (10.1–10.8). The authors advise **recording your own voice** (pp. 52, 95, 126).

**Pain, stress and healing claims.**
- **Pain:** a long exhale and a simple "breathe into the area" practice (pp. 208–209); menstrual pain (pp. 209–210); an osteoarthritis story using a self-guided night rotation (p. 87). The book reports that many people find back-to-back practices help manage pain straight after surgery (p. 119). These rest on stories and reports, not trials.
- **Stress:** framed through brain-wave states and "resilience" (pp. 193–207).
- **Against the evidence in `06`:** for pain, Ghai & Ghai 2025 found large effects against passive controls but **no significant difference against active controls** (`06` §6.0). The trials in `06` §6.2 are small pilots. So "relieve stress and pain" is plausible as relaxation, but not shown to beat other relaxation methods. To be fair, the book (2022) predates the 2025–26 meta-analyses it is measured against here.

**Safety notes found.**
- **Trauma:** not all yoga nidra triggers trauma. It depends on the person and the practice. Invitational practices that let you negotiate your own safety are recommended (pp. 60–61), and imposed imagery is a known trigger (p. 115).
- **Depression and anxiety:** some practices help and some aggravate these states. Watch your own responses (p. 174).
- **Breath:** equal-length breaths for anxious people (p. 174).
- **Other situations covered:** surgery recovery (p. 119), chemotherapy and visualization (pp. 125–126, per the index), pregnancy, teenagers and back pain.
- **Missing:** I found nothing on psychosis or epilepsy (search-inside returned no hits).

## What's distinctive
- **The most systematic weaning plan in the set.** Rhyme, memory games and a graded nine-stage plan, with its own audio track for eight of the stages, aim at practice without any recording. Compare Stanley 2021, whose Conclusion teaches self-guiding and who asks for 40-day sādhanas with one memorised script (`stanley2021.md`), and Moore 2019, built around recording the scripts in your own voice (`moore2019.md`). Lusk 2021 and Verma are script collections; Satyananda's fixed script is meant to be repeated.
- **A complete, memorisable nine-minute practice in rhymed first-person verse.** It is free to read in the *Psychologies* excerpt and to hear in two voices.
- **Candour about hypnosis, shared with *Nidrā Shakti*.** It names the trance devices and the posthypnotic logic of the returning intention (pp. 133–135). *Nidrā Shakti* goes further and treats the practice as largely hypnotic (`dinsmoretuli2022.md`); *Made Easy* is the short, mass-market version of the same position. Nirlipta Tuli's training as a clinical hypnotherapist is the background. In `08` debate 10, the "No" side cites Satyananda 1976 and Moszeik 2023; this book belongs under "close cousin".
- **A critique of the trademarked schools from inside the field, in a mass-market Hay House book.** It covers Satyananda, Swami Rama's Himalayan Institute, Amrit Desai's I AM and Miller's iRest as the four main schools (pp. 43–44), and offshoots it lists as Stanley, Stryker, Brody and Lusk. On misconduct (p. 47), it warns readers to inform themselves about "unethical acts perpetrated by prominent teachers within all four main schools", says this is "not the place to go into details", and points to statements and investigations elsewhere. It does not say what happened in each school. Compendium `08` debate 6 documents misconduct only for Satyananda (Royal Commission) and Amrit Desai (1994); it has nothing on the Himalayan Institute or iRest, so the "all four" claim is unchecked here. Of the positions in debate 6, the book acknowledges briefly and builds its approach partly as a response (choice, autonomy, no guru). Satyananda's own 1976 book predates the revelations, so it cannot be compared on this point.
- **Rotation turned upside down:** head-to-toe, downward-flowing, body-inclusive (pelvis named), adaptable for missing parts. The opposites are drawn from present experience.
- **Exiting treated as a skill.** Two chapters cover it, with timer buffers, a 21-point return and "wriggles". Most books give this a paragraph.
- **The labyrinth model**, with opposites at the centre. This is shared with *Nidrā Shakti*, whose excerpt has the same hand-drawn nine-part labyrinth with paradox at the centre and a labyrinth rotation (Treasury no. 15) (`dinsmoretuli2022.md`). *Made Easy* is the short, mass-market place to meet it.

**Made Easy vs *Nidrā Shakti*.** Same authors, same nine-part labyrinth, same critique of the trademarked schools.
- **Length and access:** about 256 pp., Hay House paperback (£12.99 / $16.99) and e-book, in libraries via OverDrive. *Nidrā Shakti* is a 704-page £45 hardback sold direct by the Network (`dinsmoretuli2022.md`).
- **Audio:** *Made Easy* has 22 free tracks in two voices, keyed to the chapters. *Nidrā Shakti* has 108 transcribed practices from many facilitators; the transcripts are locked to buyers.
- **Audience:** *Made Easy* is for beginners and self-practice. *Nidrā Shakti* is a reference for teachers: cross-school interviews, histories, research.
- **Which to read:** *Made Easy* for the method itself and for a model of beginner language. *Nidrā Shakti* only if you need the Treasury or the interviews.
- **The p. 8 puzzle:** *Made Easy* says all its practices are drawn from *Nidrā Shakti* and from the Total Yoga Nidra training. But *Nidrā Shakti* was published in 2024 (`dinsmoretuli2022.md`). In 2022 it was still being written (the *Kith Review* excerpt speaks of the "seventh year" of writing), so p. 8 refers to the unpublished manuscript and training material.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - The best model in the set of **invitational, permission-giving language** that still carries a clear structure.
  - Very practical on comfort, props, positions, timing and exits.
  - An honest **historical hybrid story**: Western relaxation from Call to Benson, plus Boyes's 1973 *Le yoga du sommeil éveillé* identified as the first European yoga nidra book (p. 103, n. 18).
  - Rejects the "20 minutes = 2 hours of sleep" claim outright (pp. 39–40), in line with `06` §6.3.
  - Trauma-aware treatment of imagery; candid on hypnosis and on misconduct in the lineages.
  - Generous free audio.
  - Endorsed by Tracee Stanley, Charlie Morley and John Vosler.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **The research is oversold.** To the question of whether research proves yoga nidra works, the answer is "Yes, lots", with "60 studies in the past 50 years" (p. 11). The 2025–26 meta-analyses in `06` §6.0 find low-quality studies whose effects shrink against active controls. (The book predates them.)
  - **The brain-wave model goes beyond the evidence.** The book says all brain-wave states, beta through delta, are present during practice (p. 9), and Ch 12 includes recipes for "remaining in delta" and inviting gamma (pp. 200–204). The EEG literature mostly shows alpha and theta; delta with awareness rests on the Swami Rama anecdote (`06` §6.1, §6.3).
  - **The history has inflations and slips that need checking:**
    - The *Devī Māhātmya* is dated to the sixth century, but its origins are said to "reach back over 10,000 years" (p. 38). That figure is the inflation. On p. 37 the first hymns to Yoga Nidrā Shakti are placed in "Indian spiritual epics that date back to the fourth century" (the epics are not named).
    - The *Yoga Sūtra* is called "seventh-century" (p. 40). Most scholars place it several centuries earlier.
    - Swami Rama's 1971 brain-wave demonstration is placed "in a Texas laboratory" (p. 197). The standard account places it at the Menninger Foundation, Kansas (`06` §6.1).
    - Indigenous dream practices (Aboriginal, Toltec, Tibetan, First Nations, Xhosa) are presented as yoga nidra's "authentic" roots by family resemblance, not documented connection (p. 36).
    - Birch & Hargreaves is not cited (no search hit).
  - **Thin sourcing in places.** Insomnia statistics come from a commercial website, The Good Body (n. 23, p. 237).
  - **Pain and healing claims rest on stories** (pp. 87, 119, 210), not trials; see "Pain, stress and healing claims" above.
  - **The publisher's blurb promises more than the text** ("overcome trauma"). The book itself is careful.
  - **Self-referential.** All the practices come from the authors' own (then unpublished) encyclopedia and training manuals (p. 8), and the history ends by positioning Total Yoga Nidra as drawing on "the best of all the four main modern schools" (p. 44).
  - **Style.** Cooking, labyrinth, adaptogen and goddess metaphors are stacked on top of each other, and the book repeats itself. A Dutch Goodreads reviewer (2 stars) found it easy to read but long-winded and repetitive. Goodreads overall: 4.14 from 56 ratings, two text reviews (October 2026).
  - **No serious reviews found.** Two targeted web searches (3 Oct 2026) for reviews in *OM Yoga*, *Yoga Magazine*, *Kindred Spirit* and *Yoga Therapy Today* found none. The Network's "In the Media" page lists, for this book, only the *Psychologies* excerpt (July 2022), which is not a review. The page's other items are about Uma, *Nidrā Shakti* (*Yoga Magazine* review, Jan 2025; *Kith Review*, Dec 2022) or older articles. A search-engine summary attributed a one-line blurb to *Women's Fitness*; I could not find its source, so I do not rely on it.
  - **Market context.** The "Made Easy" series lists sibling titles on Tarot, Wicca, Mediumship and Self-Hypnosis. That signals a Mind-Body-Spirit shelf, which may matter to clinically minded readers.
  - **Little on contraindications** beyond trauma, depression and anxiety.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The nine-ingredient sequence as a comparison grid.** Its split of sankalpa into an *opening* inner-listening slot and a *closing* "savoring" slot is a clean alternative to the resolve said three times. The three options are **simply being / intention inquiry / embodied intuition**. Together they let you keep the stage and drop the self-improvement pressure (`08` debate 8).
  - **The nine-minute practice as a length benchmark:** about 1,400 words, with the rotation at roughly 40% (see the outline table).
  - **"Settling is the most important ingredient."** Give it real length and FAQ support:
    - eyes may stay open
    - flat on the back is optional
    - pregnancy: side-lying
    - teens: curled positions
    - prop recipes
    - warm feet
    - practising in bed with a signal change
  - **"Ask, don't tell"** as an editing rule for your scripts: never tell readers what they feel.
  - **Opposites taken from what is present** (inside/outside, crown/soles, awake/asleep) rather than asking people to manufacture emotions. It is easier for beginners and safer.
  - **Optional imagery, with the trauma reason stated.**
  - **Exit craft:**
    - timer set 5–10 minutes after the recording ends
    - counting up
    - palming
    - having a drink ready
    - expecting grogginess after 30 minutes or more
    - knowing that leaving can take more than one attempt (pp. 139–140)
  - **A weaning plan** if you want readers to self-guide, and the advice to record their own voice.
  - **Their sleep position:** reassurance (if you came back at the closing cue, you were practising) *plus* honest stay-awake tips (pp. 152, 204, 214). It is a nuanced middle line for `08` debate 3.
  - **The hypnosis passage (pp. 133–135)** as a model of candour for `08` debate 10.
  - **The anti-myth line** on sleep equivalence (pp. 39–40): it may feel like two hours of sleep, and the book says plainly that it is not.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **The research and brain-wave material.** Replace it with `06` §6.0 and its honest one-liner ("better than nothing, about as good as other relaxation"), and drop the delta and gamma "recipes".
  - **Pain and healing promises.** Present them as relaxation support, not treatment, in line with `06` §6.0 (pain: no difference against active controls).
  - **The deep-antiquity and Indigenous-roots framing.** Use Birch & Hargreaves, Coburn (whom the authors cite for the goddess) and Singleton for dates and continuity. Present cross-cultural dream practices as parallels, not ancestry. Check every date the book gives before you reuse it.
  - **Metaphors.** Pick **one** governing metaphor; this book shows how stacking them dilutes each one.
  - **The first-person rhymed "this body" voice.** Test it before adopting it. Some readers find it soothing and memorable; others find it sing-song or distancing. Offer it as one register, not the default.
  - **"No standard script" alongside a fixed nine-part order.** Be explicit in your own book about what is fixed and what is free (`08` debate 7).
  - **Safety.** Do not rely on it for contraindications. Add your own section on dissociation, trauma, psychosis and severe depression, with referral guidance.
  - **Marketing claims.** If you cite it, cite the book's careful text, not the publisher's blurb ("overcome trauma").
  - **"Post-lineage".** If you use the term, credit Wildcroft's research, not this book.
