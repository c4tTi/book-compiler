## In one paragraph
This is a "no mat required" manual for psychotherapists. It teaches yoga tools that can be used seated in the consulting room by a therapist with no yoga training: breath (pranayama), sound (mantra), hand gestures (mudra), imagery (bhavana) and affirmation (sankalpa), relaxation (yoga nidra and iRest) and self-inquiry. Each tool is matched to a mood, depressed or anxious, and illustrated with clinical stories. Yoga nidra is one module among many, not the centre of the book. Its nidra chapter ("Relax: Yoga Nidra & iRest") follows the imagery-and-affirmation chapter in the contents; whether the book presents LifeForce Yoga Nidra by name, or as the end of an imagery → intention → relaxation sequence, is not verified. For you it is one of the few clinical-publisher books in this project (with `shafer2024` and `miller2015a`) that put yoga nidra inside a psychotherapy context.

## Core ideas
(From the contents, the publisher, reviews and Weintraub's own articles, not from the book's text.)
- **Meet the mood, then lead it.** Practices are chosen to meet the client's present state first and then shift it. For an anxious, "rajasic" person she starts with something active. Putting such a person straight into a restorative pose with deep breathing can make them more anxious (*Yoga Teacher Magazine*, 2013).
- **Yoga as complement, not replacement.** Cobb's review stresses that the book presents yoga as complementary to psychotherapy, not an alternative to it.
- **Tools that strengthen the therapeutic bond.** Short rituals at the start of a session (a mudra, a breath, a bhavana of sanctuary) "clear the space" so that the client's intention can show itself (2013 blog).
- **Return to the body without saying so.** The therapist creates safety without announcing it: the 2010 *Networker* article puts it as not saying "It's safe here", because the mind may argue. The 2013 blog adds the concrete cueing: invite sensation in the face or hands, without ever saying "It's safe to feel your body".
- **Wholeness beneath the diagnosis.** Practices give glimpses of a wholeness the trauma has not touched. This is her Advaita framing, put in clinical language. (She praises the same move in Miller's iRest book: language that connects readers to their "wholeness ... beneath the story and the mood", IAYT review.)
- **Empower, don't prescribe.** Ask permission before touch, let the client modify or stop, take off "the expert's hat" (2013 interview).
- **Contraindications are named.** Some breaths can trigger anxiety, and some are contraindicated in bipolar disorder or eating disorders (*Yoga Journal*, 2013). Her free practices carry surgery, blood-pressure and pregnancy notes.

## The practice as this book teaches it
**Not seen in the book. Nothing in this section comes from the book's pages**, and the second source checked at revision (*Yoga for Depression*) did not change that: I could see only its contents page and foreword. One more secondary data point: YogaUOnline's 2009 piece on LifeForce Yoga says she recommends closing a practice with a long yoga nidra relaxation, so the body can integrate the session. That fits nidra as the *end* of a sequence, not a stand-alone opener. The following is reconstructed from Weintraub's and her institute's descriptions. Check every point against the chapter.
- **What LifeForce Yoga Nidra is, in its own words.** The institute calls it a form of **meditative self-inquiry**. You **welcome** sensation, breath, feeling and emotion as they arise in the present moment. The CD page says the long practice moves through all the **koshas**. When strong emotion comes, it is witnessed and welcomed, and the practitioner inquires into where it came from instead of reacting. This is iRest-style welcoming and witnessing, which fits the chapter title "Yoga Nidra & iRest".
- **Bhavana and sankalpa as the chapter before nidra.** In the book the imagery-and-affirmation chapter comes directly before the nidra chapter. The one bhavana practice that Weintraub says she "excerpted and revised" from the book (2014 blog) is done **seated**. The client calls up an image of "calm strength" (or simply the words if no image comes) and holds it during a 4:4:6 breath. The arms reach out in front of the solar plexus on the inhale, and the hands draw back on the exhale with *so hum*. Then the image is drawn into the heart. She deliberately **does not supply the image or phrase**. The client finds their own (2010 *Networker* article).
- **Sankalpa (from LifeForce, by Rose Kress, not from the book).** Short, present tense, positive, an I-statement, repeated three times, found through journalling questions. It is seen as arising from the heart and unchanging, and the posts say it is fine not to have found one yet.
- **Length and setting.** The recordings run about 41 and 21 minutes, with an 8-minute sleep track. The institute suggests daily practice, ideally between 2 and 4 pm, and using it in bed for insomnia instead of lying awake frustrated. Do not use it while driving.
- **Lying down is not automatic.** With one PTSD client whose breath was locked in the upper chest, Weintraub chose standing practice over lying down, because she judged the risk of **emotional flooding** to be high lying down (2013 interview). For a nidra book this is the key safety idea in her approach: active and upright practice first, rest later.
- **Language style.** As far as her published practices show, it is invitational ("if it's comfortable", "if an image doesn't readily appear…"), mixed with counted, directive breath cues.

## What's distinctive
- **Nidra within a therapist's toolkit.** In the table of contents the nidra chapter is chapter 9 of 11, after breath, sound, mudra and imagery/affirmation and before self-inquiry and a closing referrals/resources chapter; whether the book intends this as a practice sequence is unverified. The book as a whole is aimed at a therapist's office, not a yoga class (publisher, reviews).
- **Debates in `08`:** §4 spiritual vs secular — she sits on the "both" side: Advaita "wholeness" and self-inquiry, presented in clinical language for therapists. §5 directive vs invitational — mixed: invitational wording and client-chosen images, with counted, directive breath instructions. §11 what to promise — the marketing leans to big claims ("evidence-based"; the institute's nidra page says regular practice "has been shown" to raise self-esteem and reduce PTSD symptoms), while the review literature reports the book framing yoga as a complement to therapy.
- **Bhavana is a stage in its own right**, with the client's own image of a quality such as calm strength or peace. Satyananda uses rapid image lists, Miller uses the Inner Resource (`miller2015a.md`).
- **Compared with miller2015a:** Weintraub calls Richard Miller, the founder of iRest, a mentor (*Yoga Teacher Magazine*, 2013); she reviewed his PTSD book for IAYT (review PDF, read); and Miller endorses her book (book page). Her Confer speaker bio says she has been trained in iRest Yoga Nidra; I found no statement of iRest certification level. Her nidra appears to borrow iRest's welcoming and inquiry. What she adds is breath, mantra and mudra *around* the nidra, and the "meet the mood" logic. Miller gives a full protocol for one condition. Weintraub gives a toolkit across conditions, with nidra as one chapter.
- **Compared with shafer2024:** Shafer cites LifeForce Yoga (pp. 106, 108) and uses an I AM/Amrit script with tense-and-release and a painful-memory stage. Weintraub's published approach puts more weight on regulating first and on the client choosing (no supplied image, no forced supine posture). Both books are for therapists, and both say yoga practices complement psychotherapy and do not replace it. **Shared root:** Weintraub's first teacher training was at Kripalu (1992) and she studied at the Lakulish ashram near Baroda (2013 interview); Shafer's script comes from the I AM / Amrit Desai line, and Amrit Desai founded Kripalu Yoga and claims the Lakulish lineage (`desai2017.md`, `shafer2024.md`). So the two are less independent than they look. Amrit Desai left Kripalu in 1994 after misconduct (`01`; `08` §6); nothing I read connects Weintraub to that history beyond the 1992 training, but if you cite both, handle §6 consistently.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:** written for clinicians by someone who has trained them for years. Practices are matched to mood. It is clear on permission, touch and empowerment. It names contraindications for breath work. It treats lying down as a choice.
- **Limitations:**
  - Yoga nidra is **one chapter**. The book is not a nidra manual, and the full LifeForce nidra script may exist only in the institute's recordings.
  - The evidence framing is promotional. The publisher calls the practices "rooted in a firm, evidence-based foundation", and the institute's site (a Rose Kress post) claims regular yoga nidra has "been shown" to raise self-esteem and reduce PTSD symptoms. Against compendium `06`: **PTSD** — 06 has only uncontrolled or pilot iRest studies (Stankovic 2011; Pence et al. 2014, §6.2 table), and §6.3 says nidra is studied as a complement, not a replacement. **Self-esteem** — no study in 06, so treat it as unsupported. **Depression/anxiety** — the Ghai et al. 2026 meta-analysis (§6.0) reports effects but flags low methodological quality and likely inflated estimates.
  - Physiology is sometimes overstated. Her 2010 article says 60% of heart cells are identical to brain cells. Do not repeat that.
  - The **reviews I found are short and entirely positive** (Sharp: "intermediate-level", suggests "layering strategies"; Cobb: grounded, nondogmatic). Neither discusses the nidra chapter. I found no critical or scholarly review.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - The **"meet the mood" opening**. Before a lying-down nidra, offer 2–5 minutes of active practice for anxious or agitated students, or energising breath for flat, depressed ones. Make this an optional "arrival" block in your scripts.
  - **Supine as an option, not a default.** Add a sentence telling teachers that, for some trauma survivors, lying down and deep belly breathing can bring flooding, and to offer sitting or standing (see `07`).
  - A **"find your own image" bhavana**. Ask for an image of a quality (calm strength, peace), accept words if no image comes, and never supply the image. It works as a gentler alternative to rapid-image visualisation.
  - **Sankalpa with permission to have none yet**, which links to `08` §8 (necessary, optional or harmful?). (For §4, §5 and §11, see "What's distinctive".)
  - **Short practices that fit a 50-minute session** (a few minutes at the start). Useful if your book has a chapter for therapists or a "nidra in 10 minutes" section.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - Health claims phrased as "has been shown"; check them against `06`.
  - Chakra-physiology and heart-brain cell claims.
  - Do not describe "LifeForce Yoga Nidra" stages in your book until you have read the chapter or the recordings. I could not verify the sequence.
