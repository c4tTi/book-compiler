## In one paragraph
A short book by a French psychology lecturer, transpersonal writer and waking-dream analyst. It puts two lying-down practices side by side. The first is yoga nidra, which the publisher's text says was systematised by Swami Satyananda. The second is the *rêve éveillé* ("waking dream"), the French psychotherapy of guided imagery that goes back to Robert Desoille. The publisher presents yoga nidra as "yogic sleep": deep relaxation plus a widening of awareness, learned by grazing sleep without falling asleep, which lets images from the unconscious surface. It presents the waking dream as a dream without sleep that reaches symbols and archetypes. The 2023 bookseller presentation adds that the two share the "substance of their message" but **each offers a different practice**, so the book seems to set out two parallel methods rather than translate one into the other (not verified). It promises a lot: sleep without sleeping pills, mastery of one's thinking, healing of traumas and access to "light and Love". It is aimed at French-speaking yoga and personal-growth readers, not clinicians.

## Core ideas
Sources are marked: **[pub]** publisher's description; **[2005]** and **[2006]** the author's own web texts of those years (not the book); **[me]** my inference.
- **Yoga nidra means "yogic sleep"**, a quality of sleep that borders on meditation: deep relaxation plus an expansion of awareness [pub].
- **The target is a "twilight state".** The publisher's text calls the target an original state of expanded consciousness or "superconscious": the "twilight state" (*état crépusculaire*) [pub]. In 2005 he used the same term ("twilight-state, état crépusculaire"), called it the state of conscious sleep, and equated it with the "ALPHA" state or a trance state [2005]. Reading it as the hypnagogic threshold is my gloss [me].
- **Grazing sleep without falling asleep is the method.** In 2005 he describes the learning curve. At first you stay too alert to go deep. Then, once you trust the practice, you fall asleep. Then you have to walk a ridge between staying too awake and dropping off. Brief lapses are inevitable at that stage and not worth worrying about [2005].
- **Imagery from the unconscious is the point.** Only deep relaxation opens communication with the subconscious and sometimes the unconscious. Images then surface in an "exploratory phase", and other images can be *inserted* to "cleanse" it, which he calls "suggestopedia" and re-education [2005]. The publisher's text keeps the first half: images of the unconscious resurface and lead on to Awakening [pub].
- **Deep relaxation is a vital emergency.** Bodies made rigid by anxiety, work and stress need regular relaxation, which is the **precondition** for higher states of consciousness [pub].
- **The waking dream is a dream without sleep** that reaches symbols, archetypes and the divine [pub]. In his 2006 text he describes Desoille's waking dream as letting the unconscious run the story, and compares its free movement through inner space to the yoga technique of *chidākāśa* [2006]. That is his own bridge between the two practices, at least in 2006.
- **Same message, different practices** [2023 presentation]. This tempers the brief's expectation that he "maps" the visualisation stage onto Desoille's technique.
- **Ancient, then systematised.** The publisher calls yoga nidra a very old technique practised by "all renunciants", systematised by Swami Satyananda [pub]. In 2005 he also said it was described in the Tantras and called it a variant of shamanic trance [2005].
- **The Western form is going stale.** The ossification sentence in the publisher's text appears word for word in his 2005 article, so it is very probably his own wording. In 2005 he explained it this way: what is spread in the West is a lying or seated reverie that has become a mental exercise. People *think* "a mushroom" instead of seeing or smelling it. Teachers also repeat only the first lesson (or the first five) of Satyananda's sequence instead of a long, progressive training [2005].

## The practice as this book teaches it
**Verdict: not usable for your comparison grid until the book is read. It ranks low-to-medium on the buy list:** cheap and in print, but valuable mainly for the psychology chapter.

**What can be said, by source.** None of this is from the book itself:
- **Posture:** lying relaxation (*relaxation allongée*) [pub; 2005].
- **Length:** a session lasts "one to two hours" [2005]. His 2015–16 Paris yoga nidra series ran Saturday sessions of 13:45–16:00 (2016 home page). How long the book's exercises are is unknown.
- **Building a session:** a session develops only one or two aspects of yoga nidra for a set purpose. You build it from a few techniques, the way you build a hatha class from a few postures. The full sequence is a "summary" to be learned progressively [2005]. Whether the book keeps Satyananda's eight stages is **not known**.
- **Themed sessions:** he described the 2015–16 series as "creative visualisation in deep relaxation (yoga nidra)" for rediscovering one's inner images and "speaking to one's unconscious". It had nine stand-alone themed sessions, e.g. "Shame and Guilt", "The Garden of Delights", "The Inner Light" (2016 home page). This suggests the imagery stage is extended into themed journeys, but that is my inference about the book.
- **Sleep:** stay on the edge; occasional brief lapses are acceptable while learning [2005]. This puts him on the "stay awake" side of the falling-asleep debate (pointer: `08` debate 3).
- **Imagery:** images should be *seen and sensed*, not just thought. Spontaneous images come first, then deliberately inserted ones [2005].
- **Sankalpa:** **no information.** Nothing I saw, including the 2005 article, mentions a resolve or *sankalpa*.
- **Language style and a guided example:** **not available.** I found no guided passage from the book or the 2005 booklet. Amazon.fr showed a captcha and Babelio reset the connection, so "Feuilleter" could not be tried.

**Why the contrast matters (my analysis, not the book's):**
- **Yoga nidra is one-way; the waking dream is a dialogue.** In yoga nidra the teacher talks and the practitioner is silent. In the waking dream the *imager* talks and the guide follows, and the work on meaning happens afterwards with a therapist.
- **So the stages do not line up neatly.** Only stages 1 and 6 have clear waking-dream counterparts. In 2006 Descamps bridged the gap by likening the waking dream's movement through inner space to *chidākāśa*. Whether the book does the same, or keeps the practices apart, is the main thing to read for.
- **Desoille's own method is better read in the original.** Descamps's 2006 text describes its descents (old, forgotten images; a plunge into the unconscious) and ascents (arduous, purifying, sublimating). Desoille's own account, and his reading of the method as a regulation of the collective unconscious (the subtitle of his 1945 book, confirmed in the BnF catalogue), should come from Desoille's books (1945; 1961). I did not read them, and they are not yet in the project bibliography.

## What's distinctive
- **The only book in the set that puts yoga nidra next to a Western psychotherapy of imagery.** The others explain the practice in other ways: yogic psychology (*saṃskāras*, *chidākāśa*: Satyananda); non-dual inquiry (Miller 2005, Bonnasse); brainwave, kosha and karma maps (Desai 2017, per `desai2017.md`); sleep science and history for a teacher's craft (Wildcroft & Nolan 2026, per `wildcroft2026.md`); or a clinical PTSD protocol (Miller's iRest workbook, per `miller2015a.md`). Here a waking-dream psychoanalyst sets the imagery stage beside the French waking-dream tradition. That gives your psychology chapter a European comparison that no English yoga nidra book offers.
- **A French transpersonal voice on a small French shelf.** It differs from Boyes's 1973 relaxation-class manual [@boyes1973], Bonnasse's Advaita and tantra handbook [@bonnasse2015fr], and André Riehl's "Nidra Yoga" strand (France/Italy; see `sesma2022.md`; Riehl co-authored [@zaccaro2021]). Mathieu's book-plus-CD (`mathieu2018.md`) also leans on a "dream" framing: one CD session lets you live the session's process "in dream". `mathieu2018.md` already points back to Descamps.
- **Some uptake abroad.** Ana Sesma's Spanish synthesis lists Descamps among her sources and groups him with Riehl as the French strand (`sesma2022.md`). That is the only evidence of influence I found, beyond 1 Goodreads rating.
- **The "twilight state" vocabulary.** *État crépusculaire*, "superconscious", and in 2005 "alpha state". Caution: in older psychiatry, "twilight state" also names a pathological clouding of consciousness, so the term needs care in translation (my note).
- **An insider-outsider critique of Western standardisation (2005, repeated in 2015).** He calls Western yoga nidra a "mental" reverie that repeats the first lesson. He writes from outside the lineages but as a yoga teacher, unlike Dinsmore-Tuli's critique from inside them [@dinsmoretuli2022].
- **A history lead.** His 2005 list of Satyananda's first Western students includes Denis Boyes, and he also wrote *Histoire du hatha-yoga : en France, passé et présent* (Almora, 2011). His account of French yoga nidra's origins may be in either book. Not checked.

## Strengths and limitations
**Strengths (as far as can be judged without reading it)**
- A unique angle: imagery and the unconscious, from a psychologist who spent decades on directed dreaming and who taught yoga nidra himself (2015–16 Paris series).
- He takes imagery seriously as sensory experience (seen and smelled, not just thought). That is a useful teaching point.
- Compact: 157 pp., with practical exercises, a glossary and a bibliography. In print (2023 reprint) and cheap.

**Limitations and critiques**
- **No serious reception found.** No journal or magazine review turned up. Goodreads has 1 rating (4/5) and no written review. Babelio and Amazon.fr could not be checked. Sesma's citation is the only uptake found.
- **"Very ancient technique practised by all renunciants"** (and in 2005, "described in the Tantras") is the strong form of the antiquity claim. Historians reject it (pointer: `08` debate 1; [@birch2015]), and his own credit to Satyananda points to a modern system.
- **"Heal your traumas" is a risky promise.** Imagery work on trauma belongs in therapy with a trained clinician. In the waking dream itself, a therapist follows the images and works on them in later sessions. A group yoga nidra recording has no such frame. For flashbacks and dissociation as known risks, see `07` (pointer).
- **Inserting images to "cleanse" the unconscious and "suggestopedia"** [2005] is openly suggestion-based. It pulls yoga nidra toward the "close cousin of hypnosis" side (pointer: `08` debate 10). Whether the book keeps this language is unknown.
- **"Sleep without sleeping pills"** is not supported as a comparison with medication. For the current sleep evidence, see `06` §6.0 and [@singh2026] (pointer; not checked for this summary).
- **"Alpha state", unconscious, superconscious, archetypes, the divine.** These are his frameworks, not findings. For what EEG studies report, see `06` §6.1. For sleep-onset imagery as **hypnagogia**, see `03` §3.9 and [@mavromatis1987]. For the PET study of a visualisation stage, see [@lou1999] (`06` §6.1). These are pointers to other chapters, not evidence checked here.
- **Spiritual promises** ("access to light and Love") place it firmly on the spiritual side (pointer: `08` debate 4).

## For your own book
**Take**
- **The comparison itself, as a framing for your psychology chapter.** Yoga nidra's imagery stage can be explained against the French waking-dream tradition: both use a relaxed, lying, eyes-closed state to invite images.
  - **The difference that matters:** the waking dream is a dialogue, and its interpretation comes later with a therapist. Yoga nidra is a monologue with no interpretation.
  - **The teaching rule that follows:** offer images lightly, let them go, and don't interpret students' images in class.
  - **Sources:** build this section from primary sources. Read Desoille 1945 and 1961, and Fabre & Maurey 1985 for the "see, live, verbalise" criteria (none of these is in the project bibliography yet). For hypnagogia use [@mavromatis1987], and for the visualisation-stage PET data use [@lou1999]. Cite Descamps as the yoga-world author who made the comparison.
- **"Think a mushroom vs. see and smell it."** His 2005 point that imagery must be sensed, not just named, is a clear teaching cue. Paraphrase it with attribution.
- **"A session is built like an asana class".** Pick one or two techniques for a purpose instead of reciting the whole sequence. This is a useful line for your chapter on scripts (pointer: `08` debate 7).
- **The threshold vocabulary.** "Grazing sleep without falling asleep" and "the ridge path" are vivid French images (they pair with Boyes's "sommeil éveillé"). Translate *état crépusculaire* as "the threshold" or "hypnagogic state", not "twilight state".
- **For your history chapter:** his 2005 list of Satyananda's early Western students (Devatmananda, Boyes, Bhaktananda, c. 1968) and his *Histoire du hatha-yoga : en France, passé et présent* (Almora, 2011). These are leads, not checked.

**Skip or treat critically**
- **Don't cite the book for any specific claim until you have read it.** Everything above comes from the publisher's text, catalogues and his earlier web texts.
- **Don't assume it maps nidra onto Desoille stage by stage.** The 2023 presentation suggests two parallel practices.
- **Don't repeat the publisher's promises as fact** (antiquity, trauma healing, sleep without pills). Say "claimed", and use `06` and `07`.
- **Don't import the waking dream, or "inserted" corrective images, into a group class.** Asking students to speak their images, steering them into descents, or planting images to "cleanse" the unconscious is psychotherapy or suggestion. Unless you are trained in it, keep imagery brief, neutral and optional, and offer an eyes-open option (pointer: `07`).
- **Treat Jungian or transpersonal claims (archetypes, superconscious, the divine) as one interpretive frame among others**, not as an explanation of how yoga nidra works.
- **When citing:** describe the author as a former Paris V psychology lecturer (*maître de conférences*), not "Sorbonne professor". Cite the edition you actually read (2015 first printing or 2023 reprint, same text).
