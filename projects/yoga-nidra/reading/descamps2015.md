## In one paragraph
A short book by a French psychology lecturer and transpersonal writer. It puts two lying-down practices side by side: yoga nidra, which he presents in the Satyananda line, and the *rêve éveillé* ("waking dream"), the French psychotherapy of guided imagery that goes back to Robert Desoille. The publisher's text describes yoga nidra as "yogic sleep":
- a deep relaxation with a widening of consciousness, which lets images surface from the unconscious and leads toward awakening;
- in practice, an apprenticeship in a "twilight state" (*état crépusculaire*) that he also calls expanded consciousness or the "superconscious" (*surconscient*);
- a way of coming close to the "no-mind" state, by grazing sleep without falling asleep.

The waking dream is described as a dream without sleep that reaches symbols and archetypes and connects with the divine. The bookseller's presentation of the 2023 printing adds that the two are "identical in the substance of their message" but that **each offers a different practice**. So the book seems to present two parallel methods, not a stage-by-stage translation of one into the other (not verified). The promises are broad:
- good sleep without sleeping pills;
- mastery of one's own thinking;
- healing from traumas;
- direct access to "light and Love".

It is written for French-speaking yoga and personal-growth readers, not for clinicians. The publisher also warns that yoga nidra as it is spreading in the West is ossifying and losing its power.

## Core ideas
These come from the publisher's description, unless marked as my inference.
- **Yoga nidra means "yogic sleep":** a quality of sleep that borders on meditation. It is deep relaxation *plus* an expansion of awareness.
- **The target state is a "twilight state".** He calls the hypnagogic threshold an original state of expanded consciousness, the "superconscious", reached by grazing sleep while lying relaxed but **not falling asleep**.
- **Imagery from the unconscious is the point, not a side-effect.** Yoga nidra is said to make images of the unconscious resurface and to lead on to Awakening. This is the psychodynamic reading of the imagery stage that Satyananda already sketched with his "subconscious" vocabulary (`03` §3.7), now framed by a psychologist.
- **Deep relaxation is a vital emergency.** Bodies are "imprisoned", made rigid by anxiety, work, stress and nervous fatigue. Regular relaxation is presented as an urgent necessity and as the **precondition** for higher states of consciousness.
- **The waking dream is a dream without sleep.** It reaches symbols and archetypes (Jungian vocabulary) and connects with the divine. This is a **spiritualised, transpersonal** use of the waking dream, not the clinical, analytic version taught in today's French waking-dream schools (my inference from the wording; check in the book).
- **Same message, different practices.** The two methods share a goal but differ in technique (2023 presentation). This tempers the brief's expectation that he "maps" the visualisation stage onto Desoille's technique.
- **Ancient, then systematised.** The publisher calls yoga nidra a very old technique practised by "all renunciants" (yogis, sadhus, aghoris, tantrikas), systematised by Swami Satyananda.
- **The Western form is going stale.** The book apparently argues that yoga nidra as now spread in the West is ossifying and losing its power. It is a 2015, lineage-independent critique, not seen in detail.
- **Practical promises:** better sleep without sleeping pills, mastery of thought, healing of traumas, and access to light and love. The light motif runs through the author's near-death-experience writing, which suggests (my inference) that the "light" here is meant literally and spiritually.

## The practice as this book teaches it
**What can be said from the sources seen:**
- **Posture:** a lying-down relaxation (*relaxation allongée*).
- **Format:** the yoga nidra side is presented as Satyananda's systematised method. Whether Descamps reproduces his eight stages, shortens them or rewrites them is **not known**.
- **Sleep:** stay on the edge, but don't fall asleep. This puts the book on the "stay awake" side of debate 3 (`08`), although it also promises better night sleep.
- **Aim of the imagery:** to let images of the unconscious surface (yoga nidra), and to reach symbols and archetypes (waking dream).
- **Exercises:** "numerous", but their form, length, wording and voice (directive or invitational) are not seen.
- **Sankalpa:** **no information.** Nothing I saw mentions a resolve, intention or *sankalpa*. Check whether he keeps it, reframes it as an image-goal (which would be natural for a dream-worker), or drops it.

**Why the contrast matters (my analysis, not the book's):**
- **Yoga nidra is one-way; the waking dream is a dialogue.** In yoga nidra the teacher talks and the practitioner is silent. In the waking dream the *imager* talks and the guide follows, and the work on meaning happens afterwards with a therapist.
- **So the stages do not line up neatly.** Only stages 1 and 6 have clear waking-dream counterparts. How Descamps bridges that gap is the main thing to read for: does he add spoken imagery to yoga nidra, or keep the two practices apart?
- **Desoille's method is better read in the original.** Its classic directed "ascent" and "descent" imagery, and his own reading of it as a regulation of the collective unconscious (the subtitle of his 1945 book), are better taken from Desoille's own books than from Descamps.

## What's distinctive
- **The only book in the set that puts yoga nidra next to a Western psychotherapy of imagery.** The others either use yogic psychology (*saṃskāras*, *chidākāśa*: Satyananda), non-dual inquiry (Miller, Bonnasse), or brainwave and sleep science (Desai, Wildcroft). Here a psychologist sets the imagery stage beside the French waking-dream tradition. That gives your psychology chapter a European comparison that no English yoga nidra book offers.
- **A French transpersonal voice.** It differs from Boyes's 1973 relaxation-class manual [@boyes1973] and from Bonnasse's Advaita and tantra handbook [@bonnasse2015fr]. Descamps comes from academic psychology, body psychotherapies, dream direction and near-death studies. With those two, he completes a small French shelf.
- **The "twilight state" vocabulary.** He names the threshold the *état crépusculaire* and calls it a "superconscious" state. Caution: in older psychiatry, "twilight state" also names a pathological clouding of consciousness, so the term needs care in translation (my note).
- **An early critique of Western standardisation (2015).** He comes from outside the lineages, unlike Dinsmore-Tuli's critique from inside them [@dinsmoretuli2022].
- **An author who also wrote a history of hatha yoga in France (2011).** His view of where French yoga nidra came from, including whether he credits Boyes, may be in that book or in this one. Not checked.

## Strengths and limitations
**Strengths (as far as can be judged without reading it)**
- A unique angle: imagery and the unconscious, from a psychologist who spent decades on directed dreaming.
- Compact: 157 pp., with practical exercises, a glossary and a bibliography.
- In print (2023 reprint) and cheap.

**Limitations and critiques**
- **No serious reception found.** No journal or magazine review turned up. Goodreads has 1 rating (4/5) and no written review. I could not check Babelio or Amazon.fr.
- **"Very ancient technique practised by all renunciants"** is the strong form of the antiquity claim. Historians reject it, and Descamps's own credit to Satyananda points to a modern system (`08` §1; Birch & Hargreaves).
- **"Heal your traumas" is a risky promise.** Imagery work on trauma belongs in therapy with a trained clinician. In the waking dream itself, a therapist follows the images and works on them in later sessions. A group yoga nidra recording has no such frame. `07` lists flashbacks and dissociation as known risks of closed-eye, imagery-rich practice.
- **"Sleep without sleeping pills."** The sleep evidence for yoga nidra is of very low certainty (Singh 2026 meta-analysis, `06` §6.0) and is not compared with medication.
- **Images of the unconscious, the superconscious, archetypes, the divine.** These are Jungian and transpersonal frameworks, i.e. metaphors, not findings (`03` §3.7). Imagery at sleep onset is well described as **hypnagogia** [@mavromatis1987] (`03` §3.9). A 1999 PET study found more posterior and sensory activity during a visualisation stage [@lou1999], but nothing shows that the images come from "the unconscious" in a depth-psychology sense.
- **Hypnosis question.** Putting yoga nidra next to a guided-imagery psychotherapy pulls it toward the "close cousin of hypnosis" side of debate 10 (`08`). Whether Descamps discusses suggestion is not known.
- **Spiritual promises** ("access to light and Love") place it firmly on the spiritual side of debate 4 (`08`).

## For your own book
**Take**
- **The comparison itself, as a framing for your psychology chapter.** Yoga nidra's imagery stage can be explained against the French waking-dream tradition: both use a relaxed, lying, eyes-closed state to invite images.
  - **The difference that matters:** the waking dream is a dialogue, and its interpretation comes later with a therapist. Yoga nidra is a monologue with no interpretation.
  - **The teaching rule that follows:** offer images lightly, let them go, and don't interpret students' images in class.
  - **Sources:** build this section from primary sources: Desoille 1945 and 1961 [@desoille1945; @desoille1961], Fabre & Maurey 1985 for the "see, live, verbalise" criteria, Mavromatis 1987 for hypnagogia, and Lou 1999 for the visualisation-stage PET data. Cite Descamps as the yoga-world author who made the comparison.
- **The "threshold" vocabulary.** "Grazing sleep without falling asleep" is a vivid French way to say what the practice does (it pairs with Boyes's "sommeil éveillé"). Translate *état crépusculaire* as "the threshold" or "hypnagogic state", not "twilight state".
- **The critique of a rigidified Western yoga nidra**, as one more voice that the practice need not be a frozen script. Read it before you cite it.
- **For your history chapter:** look at his *Histoire du hatha-yoga en France* (Almora, 2011) for how French yoga nidra developed (Boyes, sophrology). This is a lead, not checked.

**Skip or treat critically**
- **Don't cite the book for any specific claim until you have read it.** Everything above comes from the publisher's text and catalogues.
- **Don't assume it maps nidra onto Desoille stage by stage.** The 2023 presentation suggests two parallel practices.
- **Don't repeat these as fact:**
  - "very ancient, practised by all renunciants";
  - "heals traumas";
  - "sleep without sleeping pills".

  Say "claimed", and point to `06` and `07`.
- **Don't import the waking dream as a group technique.** Asking students to speak their images, or steering them into "descents", is psychotherapy. Unless you are trained in it, keep imagery brief, neutral and optional, and offer an eyes-open option (`07`).
- **Treat Jungian or transpersonal claims (archetypes, superconscious, the divine) as one interpretive frame among others**, not as an explanation of how yoga nidra works.
- **When citing:** describe the author as a former Paris V psychology lecturer, not "Sorbonne professor", and cite the edition you actually read (2015 first printing or 2023 reprint, same text).
