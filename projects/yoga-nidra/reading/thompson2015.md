## In one paragraph
A cross-cultural philosophy of the self, organised around the Upaniṣadic map of waking, dreaming, deep sleep and pure awareness, arguing that the self is a process that comes apart and re-forms across these states. Written for educated general readers and philosophers. For you it matters in one place: **ch. 8 asks whether we are conscious in dreamless sleep.** Thompson sets out the Indian debate, argues sleep science does not rule out such consciousness, distinguishes *ordinary* from *lucid* dreamless sleep, reviews two meditation-sleep EEG studies, and proposes studying contemplatively trained sleep. Yoga nidrā ("yoga of sleep", "sleep meditation") is his example, and he tries it himself.

## Core ideas
Yoga-nidra-relevant ideas only. Page numbers are the Open MIND article's; fragments from the book are marked.
- **The "default view" is too quick.** Neuroscientists often define consciousness as what vanishes in dreamless sleep. Thompson says that is a definition, not a finding.
- **The Indian debate.** Yoga and Advaita say awareness is present in dreamless sleep, citing the waking memory "I slept peacefully and knew nothing". Nyāya says that memory is really an inference. Thompson finds Advaita's reply strong but not decisive.
- **Three kinds of sleep, defined.**
  - *Ordinary dreamless sleep*: no mentation; peaceful awareness without an object, a kind of awareness in darkness.
  - *Lucid dreamless sleep*: the same, plus a non-conceptual witnessing meta-awareness whose clarity can be recalled on waking.
  - *Lucid dreaming*: knowing you are dreaming, a different thing again.
- **Vyāsa's three sleeps.** Vyāsa's commentary on the Yoga Sūtras distinguishes peaceful, disturbed and heavy sleep, recalled on waking and tied to whichever guṇa dominates (sattva, rajas, tamas). The blissful deep sleep of Vedānta is the sattvic kind (Open MIND p. 14; the same passage turns up in a book search fragment).
- **Forgetting is not proof of absence.** Instead of asking sleepers about contents, ask how the sleep *felt*: peaceful and clear, or agitated, restless, sluggish (p. 12).
- **A naturalised witness.** If anything is aware in deep sleep, it may be an embodied, minimal sense of being alive, not a transcendental Self.
- **Sleep is trainable.** In the Yoga view, regular blissful dreamless sleep depends on a calm, ethical life, daily meditation, and treating falling asleep and waking as meditation (p. 15). Sleep science, he argues, should not take the sleep of modern Western lab subjects as the only norm, and should include contemplatively trained sleep (p. 16).
- **The evidence is suggestive and thin** (p. 16). In Ferrarelli et al. 2013, long-term Buddhist meditators showed more parietal-occipital gamma in NREM sleep, rising with years of practice. In Mason et al. 1997, long-term TM practitioners reporting "witnessing" sleep showed alpha and theta over delta in slow-wave sleep. He draws no firm conclusion and proposes testing whether trained meditators' waking reports track brain measures more closely. A book fragment suggests he doubts lucid dreamless sleep could be a *purely* slow-wave state.
- **His history of yoga nidra (book fragments).** He places the modern practice's emergence in twentieth-century neo-Vedānta while tracing its roots to much older Tantra, and lists its means as breathing, concentration, visualisation, attention to the body and emptying the mind.

## The practice as this book teaches it
The book teaches no practice: no stages, script, sankalpa or posture. What it contains:
- **A first-person attempt (fragments).** In a Himalayan setting where, he notes, yogis still meditate in caves, Thompson decides to practise yoga nidrā to watch his mind cross from waking into sleep, a wish he traces to childhood. I could not read how it went (see "For your own book").
- **Conditions for lucid dreamless sleep:** a way of life, not a technique (Open MIND p. 15).

## What's distinctive
- The only **rigorous philosophical treatment of awareness in deep sleep** among this project's books, the claim behind the Himalayan "conscious deep sleep" [@swamirama], Miller [@miller2005] and Stanley [@stanley2021]: where it comes from and what would count as evidence.
- **Sleep studies the yoga nidra books don't cite** (Mason 1997; Ferrarelli 2013). My searches found no Swami Rama/Menninger story, but the search tool is unreliable, so this is unconfirmed.
- A **naturalised, embodied witness** that keeps the depth of the practice without Vedānta metaphysics.

## Strengths and limitations
- Strengths:
  - Careful and fair; open about what is speculative.
  - Albahari calls ch. 8 "highly innovative" (*NDPR*). A 2015 *Choice* Outstanding Academic Title.
  - Goodreads 4.08 from 505 ratings and 64 reviews (October 2026; review counts on the page vary between loads).
- Limitations / critiques:
  - **Albahari's qualm about ch. 8:** trained "sleep yogis" may not show that *ordinary* deep sleep is conscious; their practice may create a mode of awareness not normally present (*NDPR*).
  - **Thompson's own concession** (reply to Windt): meditative sleep states may be too remote from ordinary sleep to justify revising the standard sleep taxonomy.
  - **Neither study is of yoga nidra**: TM and Buddhist long-term practitioners; neither measured awareness directly.
  - **His history is brief.** The neo-Vedānta-plus-Tantra line omits the Western relaxation ancestry [@singleton2005; @boyes1973]. I saw only fragments.

## For your own book
- Take:
  - **The three definitions** for your chapter on states of consciousness, to state "conscious deep sleep" precisely as the *tradition's aim*.
  - **The honest status line:** not ruled out by science, not shown by it, a live research question. This matches `06` §6.3. If you want evidence beyond the Swami Rama anecdote, name Mason 1997 and Ferrarelli 2013, with both caveats: not yoga nidra, long-term meditators.
  - **The "how did it feel?" debrief:** ask students about the quality of the time they can't remember, not its contents. A gentle answer to "is falling asleep failure?". Vyāsa's peaceful/disturbed/heavy sleep (YS 1.10 commentary, as Thompson presents it, Open MIND p. 14) gives a traditional frame for those reports; check a translation of Vyāsa before quoting it.
  - **Sleep as trainable through lifestyle and bedtime practice**: a scholarly anchor for a "falling asleep consciously" chapter and the state-versus-technique debate (`08` §2).
  - **The embodied witness** for mixed readers (`08` §4).
  - **Couture 1999** (JIP 27, pp. 35–47) for the history chapter, next to Birch & Hargreaves [@birch2015].
  - **If you want his experiential account,** read pp. 264–271 in a library copy or via De Gruyter/JSTOR institutional access.
- Skip or treat critically:
  - **Don't cite Thompson as evidence that yoga nidra produces conscious deep sleep.** He argues only that the possibility is open, from other practices' data. Pair him with Albahari's caution.
  - **Don't build on "aware in pure delta".** His discussion points, if anywhere, to a distinct mixed state in trained meditators (faster rhythms over delta, or more gamma). Check pp. 264–71 first.
  - **Don't use his one-line history as the full story**; add the Western relaxation strand (`01` §1.2, `08` §1).
  - **Don't read the whole book.** Read ch. 8 (42 pp.) and the start of ch. 4, or just the free Open MIND article.
