## In one paragraph
This is a cross-cultural philosophy of the self, organised by the Upaniṣadic map of four states: waking, dreaming, deep sleep and pure awareness. It moves through attention, the hypnagogic state, dreaming, lucid dreaming, out-of-body experiences, dreamless sleep and dying. Its thesis is that the self is a process that comes apart and re-forms across these states, not a thing and not an illusion. It is written for educated general readers and philosophers. For you, it matters in one place: **ch. 8 asks whether we are conscious in dreamless sleep.** Thompson sets out the classical Indian debate. Yoga and Advaita Vedānta say a subtle witnessing awareness persists; Nyāya says consciousness is absent. He argues that nothing in sleep science rules out a form of consciousness in dreamless sleep. He distinguishes *ordinary* from *lucid* dreamless sleep, reviews two meditation-sleep EEG studies, and proposes that sleep science make room for sleep trained through contemplation. Yoga nidrā, which he glosses as the "yoga of sleep" or "sleep meditation", is his example of such a practice. He also tries it himself.

## Core ideas
These are the yoga-nidra-relevant ideas only. They come from the article versions, unless a point is marked as a search fragment from the book.
- **The "default view" is too quick.** Neuroscientists often define consciousness as what disappears in dreamless sleep (Tononi, Searle). Thompson argues this is a definition, not a finding.
- **The Indian debate.** Yoga and Advaita say awareness is present in dreamless sleep. Their evidence is the waking memory "I slept peacefully and knew nothing", read as a memory and not an inference. Nyāya says consciousness is absent and the "memory" is an inference. Thompson presents Advaita's reply to Nyāya as strong but not decisive.
- **Three kinds of sleep, defined.** These are useful definitions for you:
  - *Ordinary dreamless sleep*: no mentation, described as peaceful awareness without an object. The tradition calls it the "sleep of ignorance", awareness in darkness.
  - *Lucid dreamless sleep*: the same, plus a non-conceptual meta-awareness, the "witness", of being in that state, whose clarity can be recalled on waking.
  - *Lucid dreaming*: knowing that you are dreaming. This is a different thing again.
- **Forgetting is not proof of absence.** Anaesthesia can block memory while sparing awareness, and the sleep lab's wake-up question ("what was going through your mind?") asks about *contents*, not about the felt quality of the state. He suggests asking instead what the sleeper was *feeling* just before waking. Was it peaceful, clear, agitated, sluggish?
- **Science partly sides with Yoga against Advaita.** Memory consolidation does go on in deep sleep, which fits Yoga's view that the mind keeps working subliminally. But it is thought to happen without consciousness, so it does not prove the witness.
- **A naturalised witness.** He suggests keeping Advaita's idea while dropping its metaphysics. If anything is aware in deep sleep, it may be an *embodied*, minimal sense of being alive, not a transcendental Self.
- **Sleep is trainable.** In the Yoga view, regular blissful dreamless sleep depends on how you live (calm, non-violence, daily meditation) and on treating falling asleep and waking up as times to meditate. Sleep science, he argues, should study sleep trained this way and not only "the way twentieth-century Americans and Europeans sleep in the sleep lab".
- **The evidence is suggestive and thin.** He cites two studies. In Ferrarelli et al. 2013, long-term Buddhist meditators showed more parietal-occipital gamma during NREM sleep, rising with years of practice. In Mason et al. 1997, long-term TM practitioners who reported "witnessing" sleep showed alpha and theta superimposed on delta in slow-wave sleep. He says no clear conclusions follow and proposes a testable hypothesis: in trained meditators, reports on waking should correlate more closely with brain measures. A search fragment from the book suggests he doubts lucid dreamless sleep could be a *purely* slow-wave NREM state.
- **His history of yoga nidra, in one sentence.** Search fragments show he calls the modern practice something that "seems to have emerged" in twentieth-century neo-Vedānta while tracing its origin to the much older Tantra. He lists its means as breathing, concentration, visualisation, attention to the body and emptying the mind.

## The practice as this book teaches it
The book teaches no practice: no stages, no script, no sankalpa, no posture guidance. What it does contain:
- **A first-person attempt.** In a scene the fragments place within view of the Himalayas, "where yogis still meditate in caves", Thompson decides to practise yoga nidrā, or "sleep meditation", to watch his mind cross from waking into sleep. He describes it as a wish he has had since childhood to catch the exact moment sleep arrives. I could not read how the attempt went.
- **The yogic conditions for lucid dreamless sleep.** These are a way of life, not a technique: an ethical, calm life, daily meditation, and going to sleep and waking up done as meditation (Open MIND p. 15).
- **An implicit teaching tool.** His alternative wake-up question asks about the *quality* of the state, not its contents. It translates directly into how a teacher can debrief students after yoga nidra.

## What's distinctive
- Among the books in this project, it is the only **rigorous philosophical treatment of "awareness in deep sleep"**, the claim behind the Himalayan tradition's "conscious deep sleep" [@swamirama; see `reading/vedabharati2015.md`], Miller's awareness-itself [@miller2005] and Stanley's turīya gateways [@stanley2021]. It shows where the claim comes from (Advaita, Yoga and Vyāsa, against Nyāya) and what would count as evidence.
- It gives **precise vocabulary**: ordinary versus lucid dreamless sleep, phenomenal versus access consciousness. With it you can say exactly what you are and aren't claiming.
- It brings in **sleep studies the yoga nidra books don't cite** (Mason 1997; Ferrarelli 2013). It does so without the Swami Rama/Menninger story. My searches found no trace of that story, but the search tool is unreliable, so this is not confirmed.
- Its **naturalised, embodied reading of the witness** gives you a way to keep the depth of the practice without asking readers to sign up to Vedānta metaphysics.

## Strengths and limitations
- Strengths:
  - Careful, fair and empirically informed. It is open about what is speculative.
  - The author is a central figure in contemplative science.
  - Reviewers call ch. 8 "highly innovative" (Albahari, *NDPR*). The book was a 2015 *Choice* Outstanding Academic Title.
  - Reader reception is good: Goodreads 4.08 from 505 ratings and 64 reviews (October 2026).
- Limitations / critiques:
  - **Albahari's qualm about ch. 8 matters for you.** The meta-awareness of trained "sleep yogis" may not show that *ordinary* deep sleep is conscious. It may instead create a mode of awareness that is not normally present (*NDPR*, 2015).
  - **Thompson's own concession.** Replying to Windt, he agreed that meditative sleep states may be too remote from ordinary sleep to justify revising the standard taxonomy of sleep states.
  - **Neither study is of yoga nidra.** One is of TM practitioners and the other of Buddhist meditators. Both are of long-term practitioners, and neither measured awareness directly.
  - **His history is brief and incomplete.** The neo-Vedānta-plus-Tantra line leaves out the Western relaxation ancestry the compendium documents [@singleton2005; @boyes1973]. I saw only fragments of it, though.
  - Other philosophers' criticisms (Albahari on the no-self chapter; Garfield's charge, answered in Thompson's 2016 response, that he treats consciousness as a thing) concern chapters you don't need.
  - Dense: 450 pages of philosophy for about 10 pages on yoga nidra.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The three definitions** (ordinary dreamless sleep / lucid dreamless sleep / lucid dreaming) for your chapter on states of consciousness. They let you state the "conscious deep sleep" aim of the Himalayan and Satyananda traditions precisely, as the *tradition's aim*.
  - **The honest status line.** It is not ruled out by science, not shown by science, and is a live research question. This matches `06` §6.3 ("yoga nidra reaches delta while aware" is a claim, not a finding), and you can now cite a philosopher of mind for it. If you want evidence beyond the Swami Rama anecdote, Mason 1997 and Ferrarelli 2013 are the studies to name. Give both caveats: they are not yoga nidra, and the subjects were long-term meditators.
  - **The "how did it feel?" debrief.** After practice, ask students about the quality of the time they can't remember (peaceful, clear, heavy, restless), not what they thought about. This is a gentle answer to debate 3 ("is falling asleep failure?"). Vyāsa's three kinds of sleep (peaceful, disturbed, heavy), which Thompson discusses, give you a ready-made traditional frame for those reports.
  - **Sleep as trainable through lifestyle and bedtime practice.** This is a scholarly anchor for a bedtime or "falling asleep consciously" chapter, and for the state-versus-technique debate (`08` §2). The tradition makes the state depend on a whole way of life, not on a script.
  - **The embodied witness.** Use it if you write for mixed spiritual and secular readers (`08` §4). You can speak of a minimal, bodily sense of being alive without asserting a transcendental Self.
  - **Couture 1999** for the history chapter, next to Birch & Hargreaves [@birch2015], on what *Yoganidrā*'s name meant.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Don't cite Thompson as evidence that yoga nidra produces conscious deep sleep.** He argues only that the possibility is open, and his data come from other practices. Quote Albahari's caution with him.
  - **Don't build on "aware in pure delta".** His discussion points, if anywhere, to a *distinct, mixed* sleep state in trained meditators: faster rhythms over delta, or more gamma. That is not ordinary delta with awareness added. Check this on pp. 264–71 before you write it.
  - **Don't use his one-line history as the full story.** "Neo-Vedānta, rooted in Tantra" echoes the lineage's own account. Put the Western relaxation strand next to it (`01` §1.2, `08` §1).
  - **Don't let advanced-meditator findings stand in for beginners using a recording.** The same caution `06` gives about the 2024 fMRI study applies here.
  - **Don't read the whole book.** Read ch. 8 (42 pp.) and the first pages of ch. 4, or read the free Open MIND article, which covers most of ch. 8's argument.
