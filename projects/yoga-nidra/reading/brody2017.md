## In one paragraph
*Daring to Rest* is a self-help programme book for exhausted women, especially mothers and women in leadership. Brody argues that chronic tiredness has come to seem normal and that it holds women back. She uses yoga nidra as the main tool to "take back rest" over **40 days**, in three phases:
- **Rest:** physical exhaustion.
- **Release:** mental and emotional exhaustion.
- **Rise:** "life-purpose exhaustion".

The tone is personal and enthusiastic. She calls herself a yoga nidra "cheerleader" shaking her pompoms, and addresses the reader as "Sister". The book is built on her own story: postpartum sleep loss, a family history of panic attacks, anti-anxiety medication started after a panic attack in a supermarket (the interview adds that she was still on it three years later), and recovery through a weekly yoga nidra class; the Introduction says she came off the pills in her first year of yoga nidra. It promises better sleep and health, but also clearer intuition, creativity and the courage to lead "from a well-rested place". Yoga nidra is the engine. Around it sit journaling, intuition and boundary practices that make it a life-change programme rather than a manual of technique.

**How the 40 days map onto it (reported, not seen in the book):**
- **Rest = 15 days.** The **Rest meditation (about 20 min)** daily, with Chapters 4–6.
- **Release = 15 days.** The **Release meditation (about 30 min)**, with Chapters 7–9.
- **Rise = 10 days.** The **Rise meditation (about 40 min)**, with Chapters 10–11.
- One chapter is read **every five days**.

This schedule comes from a Sounds True staff member who did the programme (Day 2018). It fits the table of contents exactly: 8 chapters × 5 days = 40.

The phase chapters follow the five *kośas*. Phase One covers intention plus the physical and energy bodies; Phase Two the mental, wisdom and bliss bodies; Phase Three takes the work into leadership and daily life. That reading is my inference from the chapter titles and the interview. The Introduction allows for real life: you may skip some days.

## Core ideas
- **Exhaustion has layers.** Sleep fixes only the physical layer. Mental and emotional depletion, and a sense of not living one's purpose, need deeper rest. That is the logic of Rest, then Release, then Rise.
- **Rest is not recreation.** Brody separates true rest (non-doing, turning inward) from the "self-care" most women name: films, wine with friends, bike rides, even knitting or reading. Those keep the senses and mind busy. She leans on David Whyte's five "states" of rest.
- **Rest follows natural rhythm.** She uses Kleitman's 90-minute basic rest–activity cycle (BRAC), and quotes Rubin Naiman (ch. 1) for the view that depression comes from excessive activation not balanced by rest. Overriding the body's down-cycles with caffeine and willpower keeps the sympathetic nervous system switched on, and she links this to burnout, insomnia and depression.
- **Women's exhaustion is cultural and historical.** She describes the "worn-out woman" as the dominant model of womanhood, and (in the 2017 interview, not seen in the book sample) women as "natural caretakers" who put themselves last. In the interview she tells the history of the 19th-century "rest cure": women such as Gilman and Woolf rightly refused rest that was meant to silence them, a "good bargain" that has since "gone bad". Rested women, she argues, lead better.
- **Yoga nidra is lying-down meditation.** She calls it "meditation with a cherry on top". It guides you from waking through dreaming and deep sleep into a thoughtless "fourth state" (*turīya*). Every practice passes through all five bodies (*kośas*) to uncover the authentic self, which she calls your "internal power switch".
- **An intention planted in deep rest takes root.** The *sankalpa* is a short, positive, present-tense vow. She claims the subconscious accepts it without resistance in the deepest state. She says it is not a way to "get what you want" but to live more in line with who you are.
- **Listen to the soul.** "Soul whispers" are words or images that arise in practice. She treats them as signals and records them. The "wild woman" (Clarissa Pinkola Estés) and intuition grow as the wisdom body opens.
- **Chuck perfect; know your yes and no.** Rest loosens perfectionism. A body-based yes/no check (her "full-bodied yam") is offered as the antidote to over-commitment.
- **Rest for life.** The last phase and appendices turn the 40 days into a permanent practice. She suggests a home "Rest Cave", putting your own oxygen mask on first (the publisher excerpt's heading is "Love Yourself First"; in the interview Tami Simon quotes a recap line from near the end of the book, "Serve yourself first, then others"), and daily yoga nidra "naps".

## The practice as this book teaches it
I did not see the scripts (Appendix 1). The stage sequence below is put together from Chapter 2 of the sample, the 2017 interview (where she walks through her "map" stage by stage), Day 2018 (about the book's three recordings) and Cobb 2019 (about the audio programme).

| # | Stage | How Brody does it (as reported) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Setting up | Lying on the back, or sitting if preferred. Day used a mat, a blanket and a sleep mask in a dedicated corner (the "Rest Cave"). (The blankets, eye pillows and bolsters under the knees in the interview describe the class Brody first walked into, not instructions she gives.) Cobb, reviewing the separate audio programme, describes an optional **touchstone** (small stone or crystal) held in the **left, "receiving" hand**; not seen in the book sample or in Day. | sample ch. 2; Day; touchstone: Cobb 2019 (audio programme) only |
| 1 | **Intention / sankalpa** | Comes **first**. Short, positive, present tense, "not outcome-dependent" (e.g. "I am calm, joyful, and carefree"). It may be distilled from the soul whispers you have collected. All of Ch. 4 is about it. Repeated **at the start, often in the middle, sometimes at the end**; Cobb says beginning, middle and end. | sample ch. 2; interview; Cobb |
| 2 | Physical body (*annamaya*) | Rotation of attention around the body, which she links to *marma* points. She compares this to acupuncture done without needles. Her quick everyday version starts at the **third eye**, then the throat, down the right side, then down the left side, then breath. That is not Satyananda's right-thumb-first order. | interview |
| 3 | Energy body (*prāṇamaya*) | Guided breathwork: breathe in life force, breathe out "whatever needs to die". Breathe in through the heart, follow the breath to where it "lands", and wait for a **soul whisper**. | interview; Day |
| 4 | Mental body (*manomaya*) | **Pairs of opposites** (hot, then cold, then both at once), plus visualisation, affirmations and guided imagery to loosen beliefs and emotions. The audio programme frames this as "meeting your darkness". | interview; sample ch. 2; audio track list |
| 5 | Wisdom body (*vijñānamaya*) | Becoming the **witness** of thoughts and emotions; intuition rises (her "wild woman"). | interview; ToC ch. 8 |
| 6 | Bliss body (*ānandamaya*) | A felt sense that "everything is going to be OK", connection to source. For trauma survivors she suggests this may come before joy does. | interview; ToC ch. 9 |
| 7 | "Fourth state" | Thoughtless rest "below delta". She says the intention lands here. | sample ch. 2; interview |
| 8 | Return | Guided back to waking. (Separately, the sample says yoga nidra helps women fall asleep and get back to sleep at night, ch. 2; that is a benefit, not a closing instruction.) Day reports that every practice ends with the words "Be good to yourself." | Day; sample ch. 2 |
| 9 | After practice | **Free writing**: write down the soul whisper and clear the mind. Then do the chapter's supporting practice (e.g. "council of women", yes/no check, loving-kindness, "wonder dates"). | Cobb; Day; publisher excerpt |

- **Recurring device:** attention to the space between the eyebrows comes back again and again (sample, ch. 2).
- **Length:**
  - The book's recordings are about 20, 30 and 40 minutes (Day).
  - Track lengths of the separate audio programme: not visible in the sample player.
  - In the interview she suggests a 15–20 minute practice at lunchtime, after work or to "bookend" the day.
- **Sleep:** sleep is welcome but not the aim. In the interview (not checked in the book) she says falling asleep is common in the first few sessions; you "needed the sleep", and part of you still hears the guidance. The ideal is a "trace of awareness". If you keep sleeping, she suggests practising sitting in a chair a few times. Debates file 08 §3 lists her under "sleep is fine". In fact she sits between that position and Miller's "awareness is present anyway".
- **Voice:**
  - The prose is warm, chatty and permission-giving ("crazypants", pompoms, "Sister"). The phase chapters invite rather than command: "pick and choose" practices, skip days.
  - She openly describes her guiding as speaking to the subconscious, and she likens the sankalpa to an affirmation. That is suggestion-forward; see debates file 08 §10 on hypnosis.
- **Her own guiding voice, close paraphrase (interview, not book script):** for a quick practice she walks the listener through it in plain second person: begin a little rotation; start at your third eye, the space between your eyebrows; go to your throat; go down the right side of the body, then down the left side; start breathing; feel yourself sinking deeper, not sleeping but deeply relaxed. In the energy-body stage she describes the breath as inviting in life force and releasing anything in your life that "needs to die". Compare this line-by-line with Satyananda's, Miller's or Stanley's wording; the book's own Appendix 1 scripts may differ.
- **Example of a stage, paraphrased (Day 2018, a staff member's report of the book's recordings):** in the breath part of the practice, she asks you to breathe in through your heart, let the breath travel, notice where it settles, and wait for a word or image. After the practice you write that image down as the day's soul whisper.

## What's distinctive
- **The only real programme book in the set.** Satyananda, Miller and Desai teach a method. Lusk and Verma give script collections. Dinsmore-Tuli is an encyclopedia. Stanley offers a series of practices. Brody gives a **calendar**: 40 days, three phases, eight five-day blocks, a chapter every five days, and recordings that grow from 20 to 30 to 40 minutes. She tried the format first in online 40-day "nap quests" (interview).
- **The *kośa* model becomes the curriculum.** Miller uses the sheaths as layers inside one practice. Brody also gives each sheath its own chapter, so the whole book becomes one big yoga nidra, as Cobb says of the audio version.
- **Written only for women, with a feminist leadership argument for rest.** This came five years before Hersey's *Rest Is Resistance* (2022) and four before Stanley's *Radiant Rest* (2021). Her politics are about empowerment and leadership (caretaking, perfectionism, the rest-cure history). They are not about race or capitalism. Since then her site has moved towards "motherline" and "ancestral" exhaustion: helping women become the first rested woman in their family line.
- **A large toolkit off the mat:** soul whispers, free writing, a touchstone (reported for the audio programme by Cobb; not seen in the book), the Rest Cave, a "council of women", a So-Hum-based yes/no body check, loving-kindness, "wonder dates", "chucking perfect". Desai and Stanley offer some of these. Nobody else ties them to a day-by-day schedule.
- **Built-in permission to break the plan**, and a closing chapter on keeping the practice after the 40 days.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - A clear, doable structure with a natural rhythm of reading and practice, and recordings included. Goodreads readers call it well taught and easy to follow.
  - Low barrier and warm. The "permission to rest" framing speaks to exactly the people who give up on meditation.
  - A frank personal story, including going back to the practice after trauma.
  - It distinguishes rest from leisure and puts the practice inside daily life (rhythm, boundaries, after-care), not only on the mat.
  - The one in-depth review I found is by a reviewer with complex PTSD who used the audio programme. She reports lasting benefit, while saying honestly that she noticed little at the "wisdom" and "bliss" levels (Cobb 2019).
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - *Pop neuroscience* (sample, ch. 2):
    - A beta → alpha → theta → delta → "fourth state below delta" staircase. She measures the stages in "thoughts per second", treats delta as the stage where "organs regenerate", and compares it to anaesthesia and coma.
    - Attention at the third eye is said to stimulate the pineal gland and melatonin.
    - The claim that 45 minutes equals three hours of sleep appears in the book, hedged. Her website states it flatly as 30 minutes = 3 hours, and adds an unsourced "language learning in one-fifth the time".
    - How these compare with `06`: the sleep-equivalence claim matches the "1 hour = 4 hours of sleep" row in `06` §6.3 (no evidence). Delta and "below delta" while aware match the delta row in §6.3 and the EEG notes in §6.1 (the literature mostly shows alpha/theta). The pineal/melatonin claim and "organs regenerate in delta" are **not covered in `06`**; I found no support for them, but they need their own check.
  - *History and terms:*
    - She says Satyananda developed the practice "in the West ... in the 1960s"; he worked from Munger, and his book is from 1976 (see `01`).
    - Tantric roots are asserted without nuance (debates `08` §1).
    - The Sanskrit is misspelled in the ebook sample ("nysaa", "sankulpa").
  - *Branding and lineage (cf. debates `08` §9):* a practice drawn from Indian tantric and yogic tradition is sold under registered marks (Daring to Rest®, The Rested Path® on her site) and a branded programme, while the account of its lineage is thin (a short Satyananda/Amrit/Miller sketch in ch. 2). A teacher writing her own book may want to decide where she stands on this before adopting the programme-brand model.
  - *Health claims:*
    - Chapter 1 says many of her clients are "virtually cured" of insomnia, anxiety and depression.
    - Her own story includes coming off anti-anxiety medication.
    - In the interview she calls medication a possible "bridge", but I saw no medical caveat in the sampled pages.
  - *Gender:*
    - Essentialist lines such as women as "natural caretakers" (2017 interview, not seen in the book).
    - One Goodreads reviewer objects that it is targeted at women only and defines womanhood as "boss-woman or mother". (The publisher blurb itself addresses "modern women".)
  - *Trauma content:*
    - The Bliss chapter recounts an armed home robbery in Tanzania, including the rape of a household member (she tells the story in the interview).
    - One reader asks for a stronger content warning.
    - "Meeting your darkness" work also needs trauma-sensitive framing.
  - *Breadth:*
    - A Goodreads reviewer finds the touchstones, soul whispers and free writing a distraction from the practice itself, and calls the book "just a starter" with little on going deeper.
    - I found no trade or scholarly review (Publishers Weekly lists none; the NetGalley reviews page was not accessible). Most published praise I found comes from the publisher's own channels.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The programme architecture.** Fixed blocks of days per recording; a chapter every few days; recordings that lengthen as capacity grows (20 → 30 → 40 min, per Day); explicit permission to miss days; a closing "after the programme" chapter. This is the best model in the set if you want a structured course.
  - **Making the five-*kośa* map the table of contents.** It gives readers a clear sense of progress. Consider the same idea with your own chosen layers.
  - **A capture ritual after each practice.** Brody uses free writing plus a one-word or one-image note. It is cheap, it builds self-observation, and it gives readers material to shape their sankalpa from. That last idea is a useful middle path between Satyananda's fixed resolve and Miller's heartfelt desire.
  - **Her honest line on sleep:** welcome it, it still helps, and try sitting up if it happens every time. That spares beginners guilt without giving up on awareness. (From the 2017 interview; check whether the book includes it.)
  - **The portable mini-practice** (third eye, throat, sides, breath) for anxious moments in daily life. This is a "nidra-in-a-queue" idea most books lack. (From the 2017 interview; check whether the book includes it.)
  - **The rest-cure history as a hook** for a chapter on why rest is political. Check the history in primary and scholarly sources first: S. Weir Mitchell's rest cure, Gilman's "The Yellow Wallpaper".
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **The brainwave staircase, the pineal and "third eye" physiology, and any sleep-equivalence numbers.** Use the honest summary in `06` instead. Readers who know the research will lose trust.
  - **Her version of the history.** Use Birch & Hargreaves, Singleton and `01`, and spell the Sanskrit correctly (*nyāsa*, *saṅkalpa*).
  - **Medication anecdotes and "cured" language.** If you tell recovery stories, add a clear "talk to your prescriber" note and stay within the "complement, not replacement" line (`06` §6.3).
  - **Gendered essentialism.** If you write for women, write about women's circumstances (caring load, cultural expectations) rather than women's "nature". If you write for everyone, Brody's material needs re-voicing.
  - **Trauma content and "darkness" work.** Put content notes before hard stories. Offer choice and an anchor or resource (iRest's Inner Resource is a good model, `07`).
  - **The "speak to the subconscious" framing.** Be transparent about suggestion (debates `08` §10).
  - **Her branded vocabulary.** Daring to Rest® and The Rested Path® are registered marks on her site, and "soul whisper" is her signature term. Name your own devices, and think about the wider question of branding a practice from Indian tradition and how you credit its roots (debates `08` §9).
