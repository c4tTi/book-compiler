## In one paragraph
This is the course book for SVYASA's **SMET** (Self Management of Excessive Tension) programme. The preface says it is meant to deepen the understanding of people who have taken the course and to introduce it to new readers. It sets the Selye-style stress model (eustress vs. distress) beside Patañjali's kleśa model of stress (ignorance → I-ness → likes/dislikes → hurried instinctive action). It argues that yoga reduces stress gradually, by weakening its roots. It then presents its core method: **alternating stimulation and relaxation**. In this method yoga postures alternate with guided supine relaxations, and the sample pages name two of them, the Quick and Deep Relaxation Techniques (QRT, DRT). SVYASA later studied this kind of cycle as *Cyclic Meditation*. Later chapters cover executive growth, expansion of awareness, group work, "progress in tune with nature" and a holistic lifestyle. As far as I could check, it is **not a yoga nidra book**: I found no sign that it uses the term (see Basis). For a yoga nidra writer, its value is as **an early (1988) SVYASA course text whose SMET sequence names QRT and DRT**. Those practices recur in Indian research, and the 2009 CM review cites the book (1997 ed.).

## Core ideas
- Stress is the non-specific stress response (Selye and Levi are cited). It can be growth-enhancing (eustress) or harmful (distress), and even prolonged eustress can be dangerous (p. 9).
- The yogic model: Patañjali's five kleśas are the "comprehensive" theory of stress, running from the subtlest (avidyā) to the grossest (driven, instinctive action). YS 2.2 is read as saying that yoga's purpose is to *weaken* the kleśas, so stress comes down **gradually**, not by force (pp. 41–42).
- **Core method: stimulation followed by relaxation.** A cycle of stimulating then relaxing lets a person settle step by step to lower stress levels. Fig. 11.1 shows this as a descending staircase of cycles (p. 103). SVYASA traces the principle to the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad, according to its SMET page and the 2009 CM review; I did not see the book itself say this.
- The cycle principle is presented as natural law: the book cites orbits, seasons, sleep, breath and menstrual cycles, and alternating activity and rest (p. 103).
- "Na haṭhāt na balāt", not by brute will or force, is given as yoga's approach (p. 102).
- Relaxation is a skill. With practice it becomes spontaneous, until awareness of the body drops away and the mind is in *alert* rest (p. 70; see the quote below).
- Health claim: the SMET practice controls tension-related diseases such as high blood pressure and insomnia (p. 70). The book says this; on the pages I saw it does not cite evidence for it.

## The practice as this book teaches it
**What I saw (pp. 69–70):** a lettered practice sequence. It runs: coming up to Tāḍāsana with Bhrāmarī humming → relaxation and centring in Tāḍāsana → Ardhakaṭicakrāsana on both sides → coming down to Śavāsana via the right side → **(F) QRT** → Daṇḍāsana → Vajrāsana → Śaśāṅkāsana → Ardha Uṣṭrāsana/Uṣṭrāsana → leg-stretch sitting → back down to Śavāsana → **(M) DRT** → sit up (Vajrāsana preferred) → close with the *sarve bhavantu sukhinaḥ* prayer. So the DRT is the **final relaxation** of a moving cycle, not a stand-alone session. The book gives no durations on these pages. (In SVYASA's current modules the DRT is the longer of the two: the 2021 thesis module lists QRT at 2 min and DRT at 10 min.) The guidance is short and in the imperative. It asks for slow movements and deep, slow, continuous breathing, with the eyes gently closed and a relaxed, smiling face. At some steps it names the quality of awareness to notice: a sharp, linear kind of awareness while lying on the right side, and "surface awareness" on rolling into Śavāsana. I did not see the full QRT or DRT scripts in the book's sample pages, and IRT is not named on them.

**DRT as SVYASA teaches it today** (not from this book: structural outline only, from the 2021 thesis appendix; read the thesis at the link above for the full script):
1. Lower body, region by region from the toes upward, with small tense-and-let-go movements, then chanting **A** felt in that region.
2. Middle body (abdomen with breath awareness, chest, back, arms, neck), then chanting **U**.
3. Face and head, then chanting **M**.
4. Whole body, with **A-U-M** chanted in one breath.
5. Seeing the body from outside, as a fully slack form lying on the floor.
6. Expansion into an image of open sky, ending in silence.
7. Return to the body with one Om.
8. Gentle movement and coming up via the side.

**Compared with Satyananda** (this compares the 2021 thesis DRT, not the book's text, with Satyananda's yoga nidra):
- The body sweep goes **toes to head** by regions. It does not rotate through named points starting from the right thumb.
- **Chanting takes the place of silent naming.**
- There is **no sankalpa** in the script I read.
- There are no opposites and no rapid image series. The visualization is a single expansion image (the sky) that points toward merging, not toward the chidākāśa witness.
- The supine relaxations are **interleaved with āsana**, so the method is not one long lying practice.

In the parts of the book I saw (retailer text and eight sample pages) and in the SVYASA sources I read, the term **"yoga nidra" does not appear**, nor does the word "nidra". I could not check the rest of the book. In the research literature SVYASA calls these practices guided, deep or "yoga-based" relaxation.

## What's distinctive
- It is **an early text of the SVYASA relaxation school**, whose SMET sequence names QRT and DRT. Cyclic Meditation was developed by Nagendra in the same school (CM review), but I did not see the book use that name. This is the main Indian "scientific yoga" relaxation lineage outside Bihar and DSVV, and none of the project's other summaries covers it.
- It offers a **different theory of why relaxation works**: alternating stimulation and relaxation ("cyclicity"). SVYASA traces the stimulate-relax principle to the Māṇḍūkya (SMET page; CM review), and the CM review (Subramanya & Telles 2009) presents it as a way to avoid both restlessness (rajas) and sleepiness (tamas). The book's sample pages justify cyclicity with analogies from nature (p. 103). This makes it a useful counterpoint to `08` §3 ("is falling asleep failure?"): SVYASA's answer is to **prevent** the pull toward sleep by putting activation before rest.
- **Where it sits between Western relaxation and Satyananda.** DRT's body sweep with small tense-and-let-go movements resembles the *shortened* tense-and-release form of progressive relaxation rather than Jacobson's original, which `jacobson1929.md` notes was a slow, months-long training in noticing and dropping tension. The stimulation–relaxation cycle also differs from Benson's relaxation response, a single passive sitting session of 10–20 minutes (`benson1975.md`). SVYASA puts movement first and then a guided, chanted relaxation. That places it between Western progressive relaxation and Satyananda's long lying nidra.
- It grounds stress in the **kleśas** rather than in Satyananda's saṃskāras and the unconscious.
- **Research footprint, via later papers rather than this book.** All are SVYASA studies of male volunteers using self-as-control designs, cited in the CM review:
  - Telles, Reddy & Nagendra 2000 (*Appl Psychophysiol Biofeedback* 25:221–7): oxygen consumption fell 32.1% after CM vs 10.1% after Śavāsana (n = 40).
  - Sarang & Telles 2006 (*Appl Psychophysiol Biofeedback* 31:143–53): oxygen consumption was 19.3% below baseline after CM vs 4.8% after supine rest (n = 50).
  - Patra & Telles 2009 (*Med Sci Monit* 15:CR375–81): more slow-wave sleep on the night after CM than after supine rest (n = 30).
  - Separately, a SMET course was associated with EEG coherence changes in 72 executives (Ganpat et al. 2011; only the delta change was significant, p = 0.03).

  None of the abstracts reports blinding, and none of these studies compares CM with yoga nidra.
- **Sleep-equivalence claim in a new form.** SVYASA's SMET page claims that half an hour of practice gives three times the rest of six hours' good sleep (quoted below). This is the SVYASA version of the "1 hour = 4 hours" lore in `06` §6.3, and it appears to stretch the oxygen-consumption findings (my inference; the page cites no study).

## Strengths and limitations
- Strengths: a clear, teachable two-part theory (yogic kleśa model plus Selye) aimed at a secular professional audience; a reproducible lettered sequence whose QRT/DRT components still appear in SVYASA intervention modules (e.g. the 2021 IAYT thesis module); relaxation tied to movement, which suits restless beginners; Om chanting used as a physical, vibrational anchor in relaxation (in the current DRT).
- Limitations / critiques: I found no independent review. The book is a short course manual with dated science (the pp. 102–103 framing of fuel depletion and the "cyclicity" analogies is rhetorical, not evidential). The health claims on p. 70 are not referenced on those pages. The retailer blurb says the last chapter evaluates the programme, but the 2018 TOC shows no evaluation chapter, so any evaluation data may be in an earlier edition only. The later evidence comes almost entirely from SVYASA-affiliated authors, and the institutional website overstates it (the "thrice as much rest" claim). Publication history is muddled: 1986 is unconfirmed, and dated editions are 1988, 1997, 2000 (revised) and 2018.

- Take: **why this matters for the "no difference vs. active controls" finding in `06`.** Ghai & Ghai 2025 [@ghai2025pain] found yoga nidra beat passive controls for pain but showed no significant difference against active controls. A structured guided relaxation like DRT is exactly such an active control. When yoga nidra is compared with SVYASA-style relaxation, a small or null difference is a plausible outcome, and it says more about the shared ingredients (lying down, guided body awareness, slowed breathing) than about the failure of either method. (This is my inference; `06` also lists [@ghai2026], which reports effects vs. active comparators for stress, so the pattern is not uniform.)
- Take: the **stimulate-then-relax** idea as a practical teaching option for students who fall asleep or can't settle. For example, offer 5–10 min of slow standing movement with humming (Bhrāmarī) before your nidra, and frame it as a different answer to `08` §3.
- Take, if it fits your voice: **sound as a body anchor**. Chanting A in the lower body, U in the chest and M in the head is an easy, embodied alternative or supplement to point-by-point rotation, and you can contrast the two in a "variations" section.
- Take: the **kleśa model of stress** (avidyā → asmitā → rāga/dveṣa → abhiniveśa) as a compact yogic bridge between "stress" and "practice", if your book needs one.
- Skip or treat critically: the "half an hour = three times six hours of sleep" claim (put it in your §6.3-type myths list next to "1 hour = 4 hours"); calling DRT "yoga nidra" (SVYASA doesn't, in what I saw); and citing this book for evidence. Cite the CM review (Subramanya & Telles 2009) and the primary studies instead, and note that they are in-house.
