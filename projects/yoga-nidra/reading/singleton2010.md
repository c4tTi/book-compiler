## In one paragraph
*Yoga Body* asks how āsana became the core of "yoga" between the mid-19th century and the Second World War, when it had rarely been the main point of earlier Indian yoga. Singleton's answer: today's posture practice is a hybrid of Indian nationalist physical culture, European gymnastics and bodybuilding, Western esotericism and women's "harmonial" gymnastics, filtered through manuals and photography, with Krishnamacharya's Mysore teaching as the final case study. It is written for scholars but widely read by teachers. **As far as I could find, it says nothing about yoga nidra by name**: the Google Books frequent-terms list has no "nidra", "Satyananda" or "relaxation", and no chapter or section title mentions relaxation. The relevant material is indirect: chapter 7 on harmonial gymnastics (pp. 143–162) and the "New Thought yogis" sections of chapter 6. The relaxation argument proper is in Singleton's 2005 article "Salvation through Relaxation" [@singleton2005].

## Core ideas
- **Āsana was marginal before.** Postures were auxiliary in premodern yoga and not aimed at fitness; early teachers in the West (Vivekananda onward) avoided them (book and ch. 3 abstracts; *YJ* 2011). Singleton later stressed that he never claimed āsanas were recently invented (2015 preface, as reported by Remski).
- **Posture returned through physical culture.** Swedish gymnastics, bodybuilding and the YMCA shaped Indian nationalist exercise, which reworked āsana as an indigenous system (chs. 4–6 abstracts).
- **Harmonial gymnastics (ch. 7).** The abstract describes "spiritualized" methods of movement and dance, largely practised by women and associated with Hindu yoga by the end of the 19th century, and claims that today's urban "hatha yoga" classes recapitulate their philosophical, practical and demographic circumstances. Separately, in his *YJ* essay Singleton adds that these traditions used posture, breath and relaxation.
- **New Thought supplied the vocabulary** of positive thinking and healing through harmony (ch. 6 headings; Singleton 2007 abstract).

## The practice as this book teaches it
Not applicable: the book teaches no practice. Its use for yoga nidra is **genealogy**, best seen in the free primary sources chapter 7 draws on (whether the book discusses these specific exercises, I could not check):
- **Stebbins, *Dynamic Breathing and Harmonic Gymnastics* (1892), Exercise IV "Yoga Breathing" (pp. 86–87).** Lying relaxed, the student breathes **strongly, with a vigorous surging motion**, while imagining the breath drawn through the feet, arms, knees, elbows, hips, shoulders, abdomen, solar plexus, chest, then head and whole body. Stebbins calls it "concentrated-will breathing" and "galvanic respiration" meant to **invigorate**, so it is a stimulating will-and-imagery exercise, not a relaxation. Her chapter X "Relaxation Exercises" includes a "Perfect Relaxation": lying prone with the head turned to one side, release every voluntary muscle, breathe rhythmically, sense yourself as separate from and superior to the body, and let the imagination play dreamily with any natural object nearby, without dwelling on real people or mental problems (pp. 92–93). Note the prone position: not the supine śavāsana.
- **Call, *Power through Repose* (1891).** Lying with eyes closed, think each limb heavy in turn, then trunk and head, then breathe quietly through the nose (pp. 103–104 of the 1892 printing).
- **Mollie (Mary) Bagot Stack** (1883–1935) founded the Women's League of Health and Beauty in London in 1930, a mass keep-fit movement; drawing on a short stay in India (where she noticed how women moved in looser Indian clothing) and some study of hatha yoga, she built a system of exercise sequences (Wikipedia, which cites *Yoga Body* p. 143 for her). Wikipedia says nothing about relaxation; whether her method included relaxation work is **unverified** until checked in ch. 7. Singleton's *YJ* essay names Stack among the heirs of the "harmonial" movement, whose traditions he says used "posture, breath, and relaxation". She is a possible bridge from harmonial gymnastics to mass women's exercise classes; verify in ch. 7 what Singleton says and on which pages.

**Against the standard yoga nidra stages** (my own mapping, parallels only): Stebbins's "Yoga Breathing" ≈ rotation of consciousness / breath awareness, but energising rather than calming; Call's limb heaviness ≈ autogenic heaviness / body scan; Stebbins's "separate from the body" ≈ witness stage. None has a sankalpa, guided visualization sequence or formal ending stage.

## What's distinctive
Among the project's sources, *Yoga Body* is the standard reference for "posture-based yoga is largely a 20th-century synthesis" and **the standard source for the 1890s–1930s women's harmonial-gymnastics strand**. Neighbours:
- Singleton 2005 [@singleton2005] gives the relaxation genealogy itself.
- De Michelis 2004 [@demichelis2004] gives the typology; `reading/demichelis2004.md` reports (via Wikipedia) that Singleton critiques this typology; the reviewer could not find that wording or the pp. 18–19 reference in current Wikipedia text, so treat it as **unverified; check pp. 18–19 in a library copy**.
- Newcombe 2019 [@newcombe2019] carries the British, mostly female yoga and relaxation-class story from the 1960s on.
- Birch & Hargreaves [@birch2015] and Mallinson & Singleton [@mallinson2017] cover premodern *yoganidrā*, which *Yoga Body* does not.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:** archival depth, including interviews with "very elderly" figures in the 1920s–30s postural revival (publisher abstract); Konecki calls it a "marvelous historical analysis"; endorsed by both scholars and practitioners (OUP page); Matthew Remski, a yoga teacher, called it "a watershed moment in the history of global asana culture" (as quoted on Wikipedia's *Yoga Body* page).
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **Similarity is not influence** (Konecki): some links rest on resemblance, not documented transmission.
  - Singleton softens the headline himself: modern postures were *interpreted through* Western gymnastics more than *derived* from them (*YJ* 2011).
  - **Contested reception (from secondary sources).** Remski (2015) records ad hominem attacks on Singleton as a "debunker" and "pro-colonial revisionist", and Singleton's 2015 preface (as quoted by Remski) blames a "hostile climate" in which readers with a "political or religious agenda" misread the book, and notes that innovation is usually presented as the transmission of ancient teachings. Remski glosses these as saffronisation and the marketing of antiquity. The book appeared the same year as the Hindu American Foundation's "Take Back Yoga" campaign (2010); I found no source that ties the two directly. See 08 debate 1.
  - **Later scholarship qualifies the premodern side (from secondary sources).** Mallinson's 2011 response and *Roots of Yoga* (2017) document many medieval non-seated āsanas, while agreeing that few standing poses are old (Wikipedia's account). Birch 2018 on late-medieval āsana I could not open.
  - Āsana-centred: relaxation and śavāsana are side issues; yoga nidra is absent as far as I could see.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The 1890s layer for your history chapter.** The compendium's Western ancestors start in the 1920s–30s (`01` §1.2); ch. 7 points 40 years earlier. Stebbins (1892) already called a lying-down breathing exercise that moves imagined breath through the body part by part "yoga", and her "Perfect Relaxation" asks for a witness-like separation from the body. Write "these exercises resemble", not "yoga nidra comes from", and note that "Yoga Breathing" was meant to invigorate.
  - **The gender and audience point.** The ch. 7 abstract says today's urban "hatha yoga" classes recapitulate the philosophical, practical and demographic circumstances of early 20th-century women's physical culture classes. Extending that to rest and relaxation classes is my inference, not the abstract's. It is a strong frame for why rest practices are taught and bought mostly by women; I suggest it as a **new item for `08-debates.md`** (who yoga nidra is for), or as context for debate 9 (cultural appropriation and NSDR: how the practice is named and to whom it is marketed). Read pp. 143–162 first.
  - **"The latest grafts onto the tree of yoga" for debate 1 (is it ancient?).** Singleton's non-polemical way to say a practice is modern without calling it fake (*YJ* 2011).
  - **New Thought for sankalpa and debate 10 (is it hypnosis?).** Ch. 6 and his 2007 "Suggestive Therapeutics" show suggestion vocabulary entering yoga early in the 20th century.
  - **Use the 2005 article for relaxation.** Its Crossref reference list cites a Satyananda title (Yoga Publications Trust, 1976/2001, very probably *Yoga Nidra*; the entry has no title), Call, Jacobson, Schultz, Coué, Vittoz, Benson, the *Sivananda Yoga Teachers' Training Manual*, Vishnudevananda, and mid-century relaxation manuals. Get it through a library.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - Don't cite *Yoga Body* for yoga nidra, Satyananda or "relaxation therapies entering yoga"; that is the 2005 article. Keep [@singleton2010] for "posture-based yoga is largely a 20th-century synthesis" (`01` §1.2, `02` §F).
  - No documented line runs from Stebbins or Call to Satyananda.
  - Don't extend the āsana argument to yoga nidra wholesale: *yoganidrā* is genuinely premodern [@birch2015; @mallinson2017].
  - Check any page reference in a library copy before printing it.
