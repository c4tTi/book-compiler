## In one paragraph
*Yoga Body* asks how āsana became the core of "yoga" between the mid-19th century and the Second World War, when it had rarely been the main point of earlier Indian yoga. Singleton's answer: today's posture practice is a hybrid of Indian nationalist physical culture, European gymnastics and bodybuilding, Western esotericism, and early 20th-century **women's "harmonial" gymnastics**, all filtered through English-language manuals and photography, with Krishnamacharya's Mysore teaching as the final case study. It is written for scholars but has been widely read by teachers. **How much of it concerns yoga nidra: as far as I could find, nothing by name.** The Google Books frequent-terms list has no "nidra", "Satyananda" or "relaxation", and no chapter or section title mentions relaxation. The yoga-nidra-relevant material is indirect: chapter 7 on harmonial gymnastics (pp. 143–162, about 10% of the text) and the "New Thought yogis" sections of chapter 6 (within pp. 113–142). The relaxation argument itself is in the 2005 article "Salvation through Relaxation" [@singleton2005], not in this book.

## Core ideas
- **Āsana was rarely the centre of premodern yoga.** Postures were auxiliary to breath, concentration and sound practices, and were not aimed at health or fitness (Singleton, *YJ* 2011; book abstract).
- **Early "export" yoga avoided posture.** Vivekananda and the first teachers in the West disdained the posture-performing yogin and taught breath, meditation and positive thinking instead (abstract of ch. 3; *YJ* 2011).
- **Posture returned through physical culture.** Ling's Swedish gymnastics, Sandow's bodybuilding and the YMCA shaped Indian nationalist "man-building", which reworked āsana as an indigenous exercise system (chs. 4–6 abstracts).
- **A second, women's lineage: harmonial gymnastics (ch. 7).** "Spiritualized" movement, breath and relaxation methods, taught largely by and for women and linked to Hindu yoga by the 1890s. Named figures: Genevieve Stebbins (American Delsartism), Cajzoran Ali, and in Britain Mollie Bagot Stack (*YJ* 2011). The chapter argues that today's urban "hatha yoga" class repeats the philosophy, practice and **demographics** of these early 20th-century women's classes (official ch. 7 abstract).
- **New Thought supplied the vocabulary.** Positive thinking, personal power and healing through harmony with the universe became part of how yoga was explained (ch. 6 headings; Singleton 2007, abstract).
- **Images made the modern yogic body.** Photography and print fixed what yoga "looks like" (ch. 8 abstract).
- **Modern yoga is a "dialogical exchange", not a direct descendant** of hatha yoga (p. 5, as quoted by Konecki).
- **"Authentic or fake" is the wrong question.** Singleton's own conclusion is that modern practices are the latest grafts onto a very old tree, and that knowing this history is an exercise in discernment (*viveka*) (*YJ* 2011).

## The practice as this book teaches it
Not applicable. The book teaches no practice: no stages, no scripts, no sankalpa. What it offers yoga nidra is **genealogy**, and the most useful part is in the primary sources chapter 7 is built on, which are free and which I read:
- **Stebbins, *Dynamic Breathing and Harmonic Gymnastics* (1892).** An exercise she calls **"Yoga Breathing"** (pp. 86–87) has the student lie relaxed and imagine the breath flowing in and out through a sequence of body parts: feet and legs, hands and arms, knees, elbows, hips, shoulders, abdomen, solar plexus, chest, then head and the whole body. Her chapter X, "Relaxation Exercises" (pp. 91 ff.), has a **"Perfect Relaxation"** lying on the floor: lying face down, let every voluntary muscle go, breathe rhythmically, sense yourself as a being separate from and superior to the body, and let the imagination drift dreamily over natural objects.
- **Call, *Power through Repose* (1891).** Lying on the floor with eyes closed, think one leg heavy, then the other, then each arm, then trunk and head, until the whole body feels too heavy for the floor; then breathe deeply and quietly through the nose, as if the lungs filled by themselves (pp. 103–104 of the 1892 printing). She also has a chapter "Rest in Sleep" (p. 15).

These are **parallels, not proven ancestors** of yoga nidra (see below). Whether *Yoga Body* describes these particular exercises, I could not check.

## What's distinctive
Among the project's sources, this is the standard reference for the claim that **modern yoga practice is largely a 20th-century synthesis**, and the only one that documents the **women's harmonial-gymnastics lineage**, in which posture, breath and relaxation were already presented as spiritual practice and linked to "yoga" before Indian āsana teachers arrived. It sits between its neighbours:
- Singleton 2005 [@singleton2005] gives the relaxation genealogy itself.
- De Michelis 2004 [@demichelis2004] gives the typology and the reading of the class as ritual. Singleton (pp. 18–19) calls her typology a useful entry point but a poor starting point for history (as quoted on Wikipedia, see `reading/demichelis2004.md`).
- Birch & Hargreaves [@birch2015] and Mallinson & Singleton [@mallinson2017] cover the premodern *yoganidrā* side, which *Yoga Body* does not.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Archival depth: rare manuals and magazines from Indian, British and US archives, plus interviews with elderly students of the 1930s Mysore revival (publisher description).
  - Konecki calls it a "marvelous historical analysis" that overturns the stereotype of an unbroken ancient lineage.
  - Endorsed across the divide between scholars and practitioners: the anthropologist Joseph Alter, and the yoga-school founder John Friend (OUP page).
  - Goodreads: 3.89 average from about 670 ratings, high for an academic monograph.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **Similarity is not influence.** Konecki's main reservation: some links (e.g., between an Indian strongman's āsana display and English public-school discipline) rest on resemblance, not documented transmission. He also notes that Singleton accuses others of reading the present into the past while reading physical culture into it himself.
  - Singleton himself softens the headline: it is not that modern postures *derive* from Western gymnastics (though some do), but that they were *interpreted through* those traditions (*YJ* 2011).
  - Āsana-centred: relaxation and śavāsana are side issues here, and yoga nidra is absent as far as I could see.
  - Dated 2010. Later Haṭha-text scholarship (e.g., Birch 2018, "The Proliferation of Āsana-s in Late-Medieval Yoga Texts", in *Yoga in Transformation*) documents more premodern postures; I could not open it, so check how far it qualifies the "no precedent" claim.
  - Konecki notes that Sjoman's earlier, similar argument about the Mysore palace gymnastics was ignored or attacked by those invested in ancient origins. Expect the same reaction from some readers if you lean on this book; I found no specific published rebuttal of *Yoga Body* in this session.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The 1890s layer for your history chapter.** The compendium's Western ancestors start with Coué, Jacobson and Schultz (1920s–30s, `01` §1.2). Singleton's chapter 7 points 40 years earlier. Stebbins's lying-down "Yoga Breathing" through a sequence of body parts (1892) and Call's limb-by-limb heaviness (1891) are free to read and quotable (public domain). The striking detail: **in 1892 an American manual already called a lying-down, part-by-part body-awareness exercise "yoga"**, and a lying "perfect relaxation" already asked the student to feel separate from the body, a witness stance. Write it as "these exercises resemble", not "yoga nidra comes from".
  - **The gender and audience point.** The ch. 7 abstract says today's classes repeat the demographics of early 20th-century women's relaxation and stretching classes. It is a strong frame for why rest practices are taught and bought mostly by women, and for debate 9 (who yoga nidra is for). Cite the chapter, and read pp. 143–162 first.
  - **The "graft on an old tree" framing for debate 1 (is it ancient?).** It is Singleton's own non-polemical way to say a practice is modern without calling it fake (*YJ* 2011). It fits the compendium's "ancient roots, modern form" line.
  - **New Thought for sankalpa and debate 10 (is it hypnosis?).** Chapter 6's New Thought sections and his 2007 article "Suggestive Therapeutics" show that positive-thinking and suggestion vocabulary entered yoga early in the 20th century. It is useful context for why a resolve repeated in deep relaxation sounds like autosuggestion.
  - **Use the 2005 article for anything about relaxation.** Its reference list (Crossref) cites Satyananda's *Yoga Nidra*, Call, Jacobson, Schultz, Coué, Vittoz, Benson, the *Sivananda Yoga Teachers' Training Manual* and Vishnudevananda's *Complete Illustrated Book of Yoga*, as well as mid-century relaxation manuals (Boome 1938, Rippon 1940, Colson 1956, Crisp 1970). That is where yoga nidra is actually discussed. Get it through a library (Taylor & Francis is closed access).
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Don't cite *Yoga Body* for yoga nidra, Satyananda, or "proprioceptive relaxation therapies entering yoga".** That is the 2005 article's thesis. The project's brief and candidate notes attach it to this book; I found no evidence the book develops it. In the compendium, keep [@singleton2010] only for "posture-based yoga is largely a 20th-century synthesis" (`01` §1.2, `02` §F), which is accurate.
  - **No documented line runs from Stebbins or Call to Satyananda.** The parallels are cultural background. Apply Konecki's own warning about resemblance versus influence.
  - **Don't extend the āsana argument to yoga nidra wholesale.** The book is about posture. The word *yoganidrā* and some of its ideas are genuinely premodern [@birch2015; @mallinson2017], so "it's all modern" would be as wrong as "it's all ancient".
  - Check any page reference in a library copy before printing it. Only the chapter ranges and the p. 5 phrase (via Konecki) are confirmed here.
