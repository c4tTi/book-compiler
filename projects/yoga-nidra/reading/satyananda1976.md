## In one paragraph
This is the book that turned "yoga nidra" into a named, step-by-step guided practice. Almost every later book either follows it or reacts against it. Satyananda presents yoga nidra as **relaxation with awareness kept on**, a "psychic sleep" on the threshold between waking and sleep. Here the mind is unusually receptive, and the unconscious can be both reached and reprogrammed. He classifies it as a form of *pratyāhāra* (withdrawal of the senses) that can lead on to concentration, meditation and *samādhi*. The book is for yoga students, teachers and therapists. It has three main parts. The theory part combines yoga, tantra, Freudian and Jungian psychology and 1970s brain science. The practice part is a graded kit of complete scripts you can read aloud or adapt. The "scientific investigations" part, with its appendices, applies the method to stress, sleep, education, psychosomatic illness and heart disease. Its central promise is large. With a resolve (*sankalpa*) planted at the right moment, the practice can relax body, emotions and mind, train the will, speed up learning, and help heal illness and change one's whole life.

Notes on the structure:
- **The scripts are graded.** Practices 1 to 5 run 8 to 12 printed pages each, so 4 and 5 are the longest. The "Complete Scheme" (p. 132) appears to tabulate them stage by stage. With the two class transcriptions, that gives seven complete practices.
- **The research is a third of the book.** From p. 169 to the end, about 90 pages are research reports and appendices. A 2001 lineage article cites two pieces from inside the 6th edition: a study on sleep-onset insomnia (Shealy) and the appendix "Yoga Nidra – Altered State of Consciousness" by Sannyasi Mangalteertham (Dr A.K. Ghosh), who reports alpha dominance. These reports are the oldest, most dated part.
- **Earlier editions were organised differently.** The 1978 *YOGA* compilation was "divided into two sections, theory and practice", with complete practices plus material for building more. It also had sections that are **not chapter titles in the 6th edition**: "Psychological Aspects", "Hypnotism" and "Preparing for Yoga Nidra". The 1976 first edition had only 91 pages. The Introduction also seems to date from the early 1980s, since it describes a boy taught in yoga nidra as "now twenty-one". The 1998 6th edition is therefore a much expanded and reorganised book, and you should always cite it with the edition.

## Core ideas
- **Relaxation means awareness, not collapse.** The Introduction sets yoga nidra against ordinary "relaxing" (coffee, television, a drink), which Satyananda calls mere sensory diversion. True relaxation, he says, requires staying aware. He renders the term as "psychic sleep" or "dynamic sleep". In early talks he explains that *nidrā*, properly understood, means "relaxation" rather than sleep. The 1964 talk defines yoga nidra as neither sleep, nor waking, nor dream.
- **Three kinds of tension.** Muscular, emotional and mental tension, borrowed from "modern psychology", cause most modern illness. Yoga nidra releases them in order: body rotation and breath for the body, opposites for the emotions, visualization for the mind (1978 compilation).
- **A *pratyāhāra* technique.** The practice withdraws the senses one at a time. It lets the mind wander outward to sounds until it loses interest, removes touch with the posture, rotates attention through the body until the mind turns inward, then breath, feelings and images. It is the "front door" to *dhāraṇā*, *dhyāna* and *samādhi* (TOC chapters "Process of Pratyahara", "Beyond the Body and Mind", "Emerging into Samadhi"; 1978 "Yoga and the Mind").
- **The receptive threshold and the subconscious.** On the border of sleep the "censor" of the conscious mind (Freud's ego) goes off duty. *Saṃskāras* (stored impressions, which he equates with complexes and repressions) surface as images and can be witnessed and "exhausted". Suggestions planted then sink deep and later act on behaviour. Satyananda calls this state the **"hypnayogic state"**, his own coinage alongside the psychologists' "hypnagogic" (Introduction).
- **The sankalpa is the engine of change.** It is a short, positive, unchanging resolve made at the start and repeated at the end. The 6th edition, as quoted by a lineage author (Bhushan 2001), compares the first to sowing a seed and the second to watering it, and claims a resolve made sincerely in yoga nidra never fails.
- **Derived from *nyāsa*.** The body rotation is a de-ritualised form of tantric *nyāsa*, the placing of mantras on parts of the body. The Introduction gives the hand *nyāsa* mantras for each finger as an example and says the modern form gives "the full benefits" of *nyāsa* to people of any religion. In a 1982 talk he names *aṅga-nyāsa* and *kara-nyāsa* specifically.
- **Learning during sleep.** The book's origin story and "experiments" claim that the sleeping or near-sleeping mind can take in knowledge directly. Hence the educational chapter ("Educating the Whole Mind") and the claims about learning languages fast.
- **Big therapeutic and practical claims.** These include stress-related and psychosomatic illness (asthma, hypertension, heart disease), insomnia, addiction, delinquency and education. According to the 6th edition as quoted in 2001, it is prescribed by doctors in many countries and **"a single hour of yoga nidra is as restful as four hours of conventional sleep"**. Satyananda had already said this in Japan in 1969, and the August 1978 *YOGA* editorial repeated it.

## The practice as this book teaches it
**Sources for this section.** The six-plus-two stage outline is the book's own ("Outline of the Practice", pp. 69–73). Birch & Hargreaves count it as seven parts, with the sankalpa inside "Resolve" at both start and end; most teachers count eight stages. Details marked *(1978)* come from the August 1978 *YOGA* compilation. Details marked *(p. 122)* come from the book page I saw.

| Stage | What the book does | Notes for comparison |
|---|---|---|
| **0. Before the practice** | Room quiet, closed, softly lit, not draughty; mosquito-proof (!). Best times are 4–6 a.m. or just before bed. Not within 2 hours of a heavy meal. At home, first do about 20 min of āsanas, or 5–10 rounds of sun salutation, or tense-and-release the whole body 3–5 times (*1978*). | Very practical, written for Indian and 1970s conditions. In a talk of about 1977 Satyananda instead tells insomniacs to practise **right after** meals, so the lineage is not consistent. |
| **1. Preparation** | **Śavāsana**: arms away from the body, palms up, legs apart, eyes closed, thin pillow allowed; cushion under the lower back if it hurts; hands on chest if injured; light blanket because the body cools (*1978*). Settle into stillness. Then **antar mauna**-style listening: move from sound to sound outside as a witness until the mind loses interest. Then feel the body and its points of contact with the floor. The practitioner is told to keep the attitude "I am not going to sleep" (Introduction). | Supine only; few alternatives. The listening-to-sounds opening is distinctive: the senses are used up rather than shut off. |
| **2. Sankalpa** | One short, simple, positive statement, **the same words every time until it comes true**, repeated mentally with feeling (lineage sources: three times). Turn negatives into positives: not "I will overcome fear" but a resolve to develop courage. Aim at a whole-life goal rather than a single bad habit, which may only be a symptom (*1978*). Examples are **future tense, "I will..."**: perfect health, becoming positive and dynamic, being true to oneself, remembering God, finding truth (*1978*); Birch & Hargreaves cite "I will be successful in all that I undertake" from the 2009 printing. | **Not present tense.** The "I am..." present-tense rule is a later development; the lineage's own 1999 sankalpa article already advises "I am developing...". Elsewhere Satyananda *does* use resolves against habits (hashish, bed-wetting), so his advice varies. |
| **3. Rotation of consciousness** | Brisk, rhythmic naming of about **76 points**. The practitioner mentally repeats each name, feels or visualizes the part, and does not move. Fixed order starting at the **right-hand thumb**: fingers, palm, back of hand, wrist, forearm, elbow, upper arm, shoulder, armpit, side, waist, hip, thigh, knee, calf, ankle, heel, sole, top of foot, toes one to five. Then the **left side** the same way. In Yoga Nidra 5, p. 122 (the page I saw), **front** follows: from the top of the head down through the facial features one by one, collarbones, chest, navel, abdomen, groin, and down to the toes. Then **back**, from toes and soles up to the back and top of the head. Then **inner organs**: brain, nasal passage, tongue, teeth, palate, eardrums, throat, food passage, lungs, heart (feel the heartbeat), stomach, liver, kidneys, small and large intestine. Then the round is repeated "faster". Two or three rounds suffice (*1978*). | The first round is fast, to catch a busy mind, and later rounds slower (*1978*). The order varies inside the lineage: Amritananda's 1981 class goes up the legs alternately right and left. The right-thumb start is justified by the hands' large brain map and sensitivity (Muktananda 1979, probably the basis of the chapter "Yoga Nidra and the Brain"). |
| **4. Breath awareness** | Natural breath, never controlled. Watch it at the nostrils, chest, abdomen, or moving between navel and throat. Counting is usually added (*1978*). Pauses here can be long, up to about five minutes (*1978*). | Counting **backwards** (e.g. 27 to 1) is widely reported as the Bihar standard; I did not see the numbers in the book itself. |
| **5. Feelings and sensations (opposites)** | Recall and intensify a sensation, then its opposite: **heaviness/lightness, heat/cold, pain/pleasure**. In deeper practices also emotions such as love/hate, fear, anger, jealousy. Done by remembering a past experience or by spreading the feeling through the body part by part (*1978*). The aim is will-training and a "purge" of emotional charge. Heaviness and lightness can grow out of the floor-contact awareness. | The emotional opposites are presented as optional and advanced. The 1969 talk boasts that you can learn to feel cold in a hot room. |
| **6. Visualization** | **Rapid images**: many objects named fast and rhythmically (landscapes, oceans, flowers, temples, mountains, saints), chosen for "universal" symbolic force. **Or a guided story.** **Or *chidākāśa*,** watching the dark space in front of the closed eyes and letting images rise, as a detached witness who does not analyse. Usually ends with a spiritual or calming symbol for concentration, often the **golden egg** (*1978*). The 6th edition adds a **chakra visualization** practice (p. 151). | The heart of the "unconscious" theory: images stir up *saṃskāras*, which are watched and so lose their force. Includes a safety note (see below). |
| **7. Sankalpa again** | The same words, repeated at the moment the mind is said to be most receptive. | This is the stage where Satyananda locates the suggestion effect. |
| **8. Ending (externalization)** | Slowly bring awareness back to breath, body, room and sounds, then movement. **Never end abruptly**: headaches or fear afterwards show the ending was too quick. If that happens, lie down again and watch the breath (*1978*). | In the Introduction's anecdote, the closing words "Hari Om Tat Sat" serve as the cue to get up. |

**Length.** In 1964 Satyananda describes a series of 15–30 minutes. In 1969 he recommends a full hour. The book offers five graded practices and a short and a long class, and teaches you to fit the time by **adding or removing whole stages, not by speaking faster** (*1978*). That is a useful design principle.

**Voice and style.** Directive and second-person: short imperatives and lists of body parts with almost no explanation inside the script. The practitioner plays a "double role", instructing themself and following at the same moment. The teacher may slip in reminders like "no sleeping please". Satyananda wanted the recording fixed: in 1969 he says the words, emphasis, tone and timing should never change from the first day to the last. The 1978 compilation admits the instructions are given authoritatively and directively, but denies that the method relies on suggestion. It says the only suggestive element is the practitioner's own sankalpa, which the teacher never supplies.

**Sleep.** The book and its lineage are **not consistent**, which is worth knowing because the "never fall asleep" rule is usually pinned on Satyananda:
- **1964 talk:** if you fall asleep it is not yoga nidra, and your *saṃskāras* are not cleared.
- **About 1977 (Bogotá talk):** if you fall asleep, the benefits are lost.
- **1978 compilation:** if you fall asleep, your body needed it; if you stay awake, you deepen meditation.
- **6th-edition Introduction:** don't worry if you sleep, it may even be more powerful for planting impressions; but keep the thought "I am not going to sleep", because sleep is not yoga nidra; if you slept through a tape, replay it on waking.
- **1982 talk:** stay "on the border line", aware of the fan and the people on the balcony. Going too deep is wrong too, because then it becomes meditation.

**Teaching cautions in the lineage material (1978).** Choose images carefully: falling, deep water, fire and overhanging objects can trigger phobias, so add reassurance. Never judge students' experiences. Never say "don't worry if you don't see this". Discourage chatting about experiences. Learn from a qualified teacher if you can; otherwise use a tape, or have a family member read the script.

## What's distinctive
- **It is the template.** The sequence (sankalpa – rotation – breath – opposites – visualization – sankalpa – return), the right-thumb rotation, "rapid images", *chidākāśa* and the "I will..." sankalpa all start here. In `04` §4.1 the compendium's eight stages are this book's outline. Boyes (1973) shows that a French relaxation method called "yoga nidra" existed earlier, but Satyananda's is the one that spread.
- **The founder's own origin story and lineage claims.** The Introduction gives Satyananda's own account of discovering the method in Rishikesh, which no other author can give first-hand. The **nyāsa derivation**, with the actual mantras, appears nowhere else either.
- **A graded kit, not one script.** Five escalating practices plus classes, a children's version and a chakra practice, with advice on assembling your own. Miller, Desai, Stanley and Lusk give finished scripts; Satyananda gives the parts.
- **Directive and goal-oriented**, the opposite of Miller's "welcoming" (iRest), Dinsmore-Tuli's "menu not sequence" with an optional sankalpa, and Stanley's sankalpa as a truth you discover. Here the sankalpa is an order to the subconscious, and the practice is "training the mind".
- **Sleep-learning and suggestion as a mechanism.** No other major yoga nidra book builds so openly on hypnopaedia (learning in sleep) and suggestion, while also insisting that the method is not hypnosis.
- **A built-in 1970s–90s research section**, now useful mainly as history: what the lineage thought counted as evidence.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - The clearest statement of the classic structure, with enough complete scripts to compare stage by stage and enough rules to build new practices.
  - Sound practical teaching craft: varying length by whole stages, a fast first rotation, gradual endings, care with images, never invalidating experiences.
  - The *pratyāhāra* logic (use each sense up, then move inward) is coherent and teachable.
  - Gourley (2017) calls it an extremely valuable reference for yoga nidra teachers, if dry and repetitive in the middle. Goodreads readers rate it highly (4.32 from 1,124 ratings, 70 reviews, October 2026). Reviewers especially value the scripts and its use as a reference.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **History.** Birch & Hargreaves (2015) show that the multi-stage technique is not found as such in medieval texts. *Nyāsa* plausibly supplied a basic sequence, which Satyananda elaborated himself. The resolve stage looks like an innovation drawn from Western auto-suggestion, and much of his "stress and tension" language comes from Western relaxation therapy (via Singleton 2005). Shearer (quoted on Wikipedia) calls the links to ancient texts "vague at best". The lineage tells two different origin stories: the 1978 editorial says Satyananda found the technique while revising tantric texts and began using it in 1962, while the Introduction tells the Rishikesh sleep story.
  - **Science.** Judging from the chapter titles and the book's references as cited by Bhushan (2001), the research section rests on small, mostly uncontrolled studies from the 1970s to the 1990s. Lineage writers built on the same kind of literature: a 2001 *YOGA* article suggests yoga nidra may help cure cancer (citing Simonton and Meares) and repeats the homunculus "clearing the nerve pathways" idea, and the 1978 compilation calls the relaxation deeper than sleep. Readers on Goodreads, including a psychologist, single out the "Scientific Investigations" as speculative, and one calls the passing hint that yoga nidra might cure cancer irresponsible.
  - **The 1 hour = 4 hours claim.** It comes from Satyananda himself (1969, 1978, 6th edition), not from apps.
  - **Authority and tone.** Readers complain that it presents one way as the only right way and claims normative authority it hasn't earned. Some also find the imagery and religious framing foreign.
  - **Ethics.** The Introduction approvingly reports "experiments" on sleeping children and a disciple without any sign of consent. The 1978 Bogotá talk adds a bed-wetting girl given commands while asleep. Since the 2014 Royal Commission hearing and its 2016 report on the lineage's Australian ashram (registered as `rc2016`), these passages read very differently. Matthew Remski (2014) asked teachers to stop using Satyananda's books and methods until reparations are made. His reason: "leading people into a vulnerable, trance-like state" with this founder's technique had become intolerable to him. The 1964 talk's insistence on complete reverence for the guru belongs in the same discussion.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The skeleton, as a shared vocabulary.** Name the stages the way readers will meet them elsewhere, credit Satyananda for the modern sequence, and note Boyes (1973) as an earlier relaxation "yoga nidra".
  - **The modular design rule:** lengthen or shorten a practice by adding or dropping whole stages, never by talking faster. Do the first rotation briskly and later ones slowly. This is good craft for a book of scripts.
  - **The *pratyāhāra* sequence as your explanation of why the order works:** sounds, then touch and posture, then body map, breath, feelings, images. It is classical in category, easy to teach and doesn't need brain claims.
  - **The teaching cautions (1978):** image selection and phobias, gradual endings, never invalidating experiences, banning "don't worry if you don't see this". They anticipate today's trauma-sensitive guidance and give you a lineage precedent for it.
  - **Sankalpa craft:** short, positive, unchanging, felt rather than recited, and aimed at a whole-life quality rather than a single habit. You can keep these rules while choosing your own tense and framing.
  - **Primary sources to cite exactly:** the 1964 talk (freely online in the *YOGA* archive), the 1969 talk and the Introduction's origin story. Quoted carefully, they make a vivid, honest history section: Satyananda himself calls the method both ancient and his own discovery.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **"One hour = four hours of sleep"** and **"relaxation deeper than sleep".** No evidence (`06` §6.3). If you mention them, say they come from Satyananda himself.
  - **Learning in sleep**: the eleven-language boy, the Bulgarian language experiments and the instruction-while-snoring anecdote. Present them as lineage stories, not data, and never as a model. Giving instructions to sleeping children without consent is something your book should explicitly *not* recommend.
  - **"The sankalpa never fails" / "the subconscious obeys".** Untestable, and it can make readers blame themselves. Pair your sankalpa chapter with the debate in `08` §8.
  - **The medical chapters and appendices.** Replace them with the 2025–26 meta-analyses (`06` §6.0): yoga nidra beats doing nothing and is roughly comparable to other relaxation. Don't reproduce the cancer, asthma or heart-disease claims except as history.
  - **Freud/Jung and homunculus explanations.** Use them as metaphor at most.
  - **Origins.** Present *nyāsa* as Satyananda's stated inspiration, not as an unbroken tradition (Birch & Hargreaves; `08` §1).
  - **The fixed, directive voice.** If you write invitationally (`07` §7.4), you can keep the order and still change the grammar. Explain why you depart from the template, because informed readers will notice.
  - **The sleep rule.** The founder himself said different things at different times, so you can take any position in `08` §3 and cite him for it.
  - **Naming and the lineage.** "Satyananda Yoga Nidra" is trademarked. Decide how you will acknowledge the misconduct findings (`08` §6). A sentence that credits the method's origin and names the 2016 findings is the minimum informed readers will expect.
