# Yoga Nidra — Swami Satyananda Saraswati (1976)

> **Basis of this summary:** Sample chapters / preview read, partial only. The book text I actually saw is the **Introduction** (posted as an excerpt on a retailer's page; about pp. 1–10) and **one printed page of a script (p. 122, which falls inside "Yoga Nidra 5")**, plus the table of contents (2017 printing) and the publisher's description. I did **not** read the full book. To fill gaps I read in full the specific *YOGA* magazine articles listed below (the Bihar School of Yoga's free official archive, yogamag.net) and skimmed others. They include the August 1978 issue, which the editors call a compilation of Satyananda's lectures and classes on yoga nidra. ## In one paragraph
This is the book that made Satyananda's step-by-step yoga nidra method widely known. The method itself appears in his talks from 1964 on and in print in *Meditations from the Tantras* (1974). Many later books follow it or react against it (`02`, `04`). Satyananda presents yoga nidra as **relaxation with awareness kept on**, a "psychic sleep" on the threshold between waking and sleep. Here the mind is unusually receptive, and the unconscious can be both reached and reprogrammed. He classifies it as one aspect of *pratyāhāra* (withdrawal of the senses) that can lead on to concentration and *samādhi* (Introduction). The book is for yoga students, teachers and therapists, and has three main parts. The theory part combines yoga, tantra, psychology and brain science (chapter titles). The practice part is a graded set of complete scripts you can read aloud or adapt. The "scientific investigations" part, with its appendices, applies the method to stress, sleep, education, psychosomatic illness and heart disease (table of contents). Its central promise is large. With a resolve (*sankalpa*) planted at the right moment, the practice is meant to relax body, emotions and mind, train the will, speed up learning, help heal illness and change one's whole life.

Notes on the structure:
- **The scripts are graded.** Practices 1 to 5 run 8 to 12 printed pages each, so 4 and 5 are the longest. The two-page "Complete Scheme" (p. 132) probably tabulates them stage by stage (not seen). With the two class transcriptions, that makes seven scripted practices, plus the chakra and children's practices.
- **The research is a third of the book.** From p. 169 to the end, about 90 pages are research reports and appendices. Bhushan's 2001 lineage article cites two pieces from inside the 6th edition: a study on sleep-onset insomnia (Shealy) and the appendix "Yoga Nidra – Altered State of Consciousness" by Sannyasi Mangalteertham (Dr A.K. Gosh, as Bhushan spells it), who reports alpha dominance. I did not read this section, so its quality is unverified.
- **How the book changed over time is only partly known.** The 1978 *YOGA* compilation (a magazine issue, not an edition of the book) was "divided into two sections, theory and practice", with complete practices plus material for building more. It also had sections that are **not chapter titles in the 6th edition**: "Psychological Aspects", "Hypnotism" and "Preparing for Yoga Nidra". Whether the 1978 3rd edition of the book was organised the same way I cannot tell, because I did not see it. What the records do show is growth: 91 pp. in 1976, 284 pp. by 1984. The Introduction seems to date from around 1980 or later. It says the formative experience was "about 35 years ago", during his years with Sivananda (1943 to 1955, per the publisher's author note), which gives roughly 1978 to 1990. It also calls a boy taught in yoga nidra "now twenty-one"; if that boy is Niranjanananda, born 1960 (see Confidence), that points to about 1981. Always cite the book with its edition.

## Core ideas
- **Relaxation means awareness, not collapse.** The Introduction sets yoga nidra against ordinary "relaxing" (coffee, television, a drink), which Satyananda calls mere sensory diversion. True relaxation, he says, requires staying aware. The Introduction calls the state "psychic sleep" and "dynamic sleep" and glosses *nidrā* simply as sleep. The 1969 talk (and the 1978 editorial) say instead that *nidrā* properly means relaxation, with "sleep" its corrupt sense. The 1964 talk defines yoga nidra as neither sleep, nor waking, nor dream.
- **Three kinds of tension.** Muscular, mental and emotional tension, which the 1978 compilation says "modern psychology and Indian philosophy" both enumerate (Bhushan 2001 says much the same), cause most modern suffering. Yoga nidra releases them in order: body rotation and breath for the body, opposites for the emotions, visualization for the mind (1978 compilation, "Yoga Nidra").
- **A *pratyāhāra* technique.** The practice withdraws the senses one at a time. It lets the mind wander outward to sounds until it loses interest, reduces touch through the posture, rotates attention through the body until the mind turns inward, then moves to breath, feelings and images (1978, "Yoga Nidra"). It leads from *pratyāhāra* into *dhāraṇā* and eventually *dhyāna* (1978, "Yoga and the Mind"). The Introduction calls it one aspect of *pratyāhāra* leading to higher concentration and *samādhi* (2009 printing p. 2, per Birch & Hargreaves). The theory chapter titles ("Process of Pratyahara", "Beyond the Body and Mind", "Emerging into Samadhi") follow the same arc.
- **The receptive threshold and the subconscious.** On the border of sleep the mind is exceptionally receptive. Languages and other subjects can be learned fast, and suggestions given then remove unwanted habits. Satyananda calls this the **"hypnayogic state"**, his own variant of the psychologists' "hypnagogic" (Introduction). The 1978 compilation adds the psychology. The conscious mind's "censor" (Freud's ego) goes off duty during the practice ("Psychological Aspects", 1978). *Saṃskāras*, the stored impressions that the 1978 compilation equates with Western complexes, inhibitions and repressions, surface as images. They are to be witnessed without analysis and so "exhausted" ("Yoga and the Mind", 1978). That issue mixes Satyananda's material with practices by other Bihar swamis, so read these as the lineage's framing, not necessarily his own words.
- **The sankalpa is the engine of change.** It is a short, positive, unchanging resolve made at the start and repeated at the end (1978). The 6th edition, as quoted by a lineage author (Bhushan 2001), compares the first resolve to sowing a seed and the second to watering it, and claims a resolve made sincerely in yoga nidra always bears fruit. Amritananda's 1981 class tells students the same: it never fails.
- **Derived from *nyāsa*.** The body rotation is a de-ritualised form of tantric *nyāsa*, the placing of mantras on parts of the body. The Introduction gives the hand *nyāsa* mantras for each finger as an example and says the modern form gives "the full benefits" of *nyāsa* to people of any religion. In a 1982 talk he names *aṅga-nyāsa*, *kara-nyāsa* and *varṇa-nyāsa*.
- **Learning during sleep: the origin story and the "experiments" (Introduction).** Satyananda traces yoga nidra to his time with Sivananda in Rishikesh. Set to guard a Sanskrit school overnight, he slept through the boys' early-morning chanting. Later, at an ashram function, he recognised verses he had never studied, and the boys' teacher told him he had heard them in his sleep. He concluded that the mind can take in knowledge without the senses, and only then, he says, turned to the tantric texts to build a simpler system. The Introduction goes on to report experiments on himself, a dog, disciples and many children, all given instruction while asleep. A difficult boy at the ashram was read a chapter of the Gita shortly after falling asleep and could recite it within a week. Over two years he was taught scriptures and languages the same way; he is "now twenty-one", speaks eleven languages and never went to school. In another class of thirty, while about ten were snoring, he told everyone to get up at the closing words "Hari Om Tat Sat"; at the end all of them, snorers included, sat up. **The Bulgarian language experiments are not in the Introduction.** They come from the 1978 editorial, which says a method like yoga nidra taught foreign languages in weeks in Bulgaria. Bhushan (2001) cites Ostrander & Schroeder's *Psychic Discoveries Behind the Iron Curtain* (1973) for a similar claim. Hence the educational chapter ("Educating the Whole Mind") and the claims about learning languages fast.
- **Big therapeutic and practical claims, from different layers.** The 6th edition's chapter titles cover stress, psychosomatic disease, cardiovascular disease, the brain and education. Its research section includes a study on insomnia (Shealy, per Bhushan). As quoted by Bhushan (2001), the 6th edition says doctors in many countries prescribe yoga nidra for the early stages of stress-related disease, and that one hour of it rests you as much as four hours of ordinary sleep. Asthma, hypertension and heart disease are named in Bhushan's own article with his own citations, not in material I saw from the book. Addiction (hashish), bed-wetting and juvenile delinquency come from the Bogotá talk. The one-hour-equals-four claim is older than the book: Satyananda made it in Japan in 1969, and the August 1978 *YOGA* editorial repeated it.

## The practice as this book teaches it
**Sources for this section.** I did not see the book's own "Outline of the Practice" (pp. 69–73). Birch & Hargreaves (2015), citing those pages in the 2009 printing, report it as seven parts: Preparation, Resolve, Rotation of Consciousness, Awareness of Breath, Feelings and Sensations, Visualisation, and Ending the Practice. The closing sankalpa falls under Ending. Gourley's review and Wikipedia count eight stages, with the repeated resolve as a stage of its own. The table below uses the compendium's eight stages (`04` §4.1) plus a stage 0 for the setting, for comparison with other books. Details marked *(1978)* come from the August 1978 *YOGA* compilation, *(1981)* from Amritananda's lineage class, *(p. 122)* from the one book page I saw, and *(Introduction)* from the book's Introduction.

| Stage | What the book and its lineage do | Notes for comparison |
|---|---|---|
| **0. Before the practice** | Room quiet, closed, softly lit, not draughty; mosquito-proof (!). Best times are 4–6 a.m. or just before bed. Not within 2 hours of a heavy meal. At home, first do about 20 min of āsanas, or 5–10 rounds of sun salutation, or tense-and-release the whole body 3–5 times (*1978*). | Very practical, written for Indian and 1970s conditions. In the Bogotá talk (undated; published *YOGA* Jan. 1978) Satyananda instead tells insomniacs to practise **right after** meals, so the lineage is not consistent. |
| **1. Preparation** | **Śavāsana**: arms away from the body, palms up, legs apart, eyes closed, thin pillow allowed; cushion under the lower back if it hurts; hands on chest if injured; light blanket because the body cools (*1978*). Settle into stillness. A short body awareness and/or **antar mauna**-style listening: move from sound to sound outside as a witness until the mind loses interest (*1978*). The Introduction asks the practitioner to hold the inner intention of not falling asleep. | The book teaches only the supine form. Yet Satyananda himself told a Copenhagen audience (*YOGA* Feb. 1975) that yoga nidra can also be done sitting or standing, eyes open or closed, and that he was offering one form out of many (see also `satyananda1974`). The listening-to-sounds opening is distinctive: the senses are used up rather than shut off. |
| **2. Sankalpa** | One short, simple, positive statement, **the same words every time until it comes true**, repeated mentally with feeling (three times in Bhushan 2001 and in the 1981 class). Turn negatives into positives: not "I will overcome fear" but a resolve to develop courage. Aim at a whole-life goal rather than a single bad habit, which may only be a symptom (*1978*). Examples are **future tense, "I will..."**: perfect health, becoming positive and dynamic, being true to oneself, remembering God, finding truth (*1978*). Birch & Hargreaves report future-tense examples from the 2009 printing about awakening one's spiritual potential and succeeding in everything one undertakes. | **Not present tense** in any example I saw. The lineage's own 1999 sankalpa article suggests "I am developing..." phrasing, then says either tense works (its author prefers "I will"). Elsewhere Satyananda *does* use resolves against habits (hashish, bed-wetting), so his advice varies. |
| **3. Rotation of consciousness** | Brisk, rhythmic naming of about **76 points**, two or three rounds (*1978*). The practitioner takes attention to each part, pictures it and silently repeats its name, without moving (*1981*). The order starts at the **right-hand thumb** (*1981*; 1982 talk; Muktananda 1979). On p. 122 the **left side** runs: thumb and each finger, palm, back of hand, wrist, lower arm, elbow, upper arm, shoulder, armpit, side, waist, hip, hamstring, thigh, kneecap, calf, ankle, heel, sole, top of foot, toes one to five. (The right side, on the page before, was not seen.) Then the **front**, from the top of the head through the facial features one by one, jaw, throat, collarbones, chest, navel, abdomen, groin, and down the legs to the toes. Then the **back**, from toes and soles up to the back and top of the head. Then the **inner parts**: brain, nasal passage, tongue, teeth, palate, eardrums, throat, food passage, lungs, heart (feel the heartbeat), stomach, liver, kidneys, small and large intestine, each with a brief note on where it sits. Then the inner-parts sequence is repeated "faster" (*p. 122*). | The 1978 advice is a fast first round to catch a busy mind, with later rounds slower. The "faster" repeat on p. 122 runs the other way. The order also varies inside the lineage: p. 122 goes front then back; Bhushan's 2001 summary goes back then front; Amritananda's 1981 class goes up the legs in right–left pairs. The right-thumb start is justified by the hands' large brain map and sensitivity (Muktananda 1979, probably the basis of the chapter "Yoga Nidra and the Brain"). |
| **4. Breath awareness** | Natural breath, never controlled. Watch it at the nostrils, chest, abdomen, or moving between navel and throat. Counting is usually added (*1978*). Pauses here can be long, up to about five minutes (*1978*). | **No counting numbers in anything I read.** The 1978 compilation and Bhushan 2001 mention counting without numbers, and the 1981 class does not count at all (and puts breath before the sankalpa). The 27 → 1 or 54 → 1 in `04` §4.1 needs checking in the book. |
| **5. Feelings and sensations (opposites)** | Recall and intensify a sensation, then its opposite: **heaviness/lightness, heat/cold, pain/pleasure**. In deeper practices, if desired, also emotions such as love/hate, fear, anger, jealousy. Done by remembering a past experience or by spreading the feeling through the body part by part (*1978*). The aim is will-training and a purge of emotional charge. Heaviness and lightness can grow out of awareness of the body's contact with the floor (*1978*). | The emotional opposites are presented as optional and advanced. The 1969 talk boasts that you can learn to feel cold in a hot room. |
| **6. Visualization** | **Rapid images**: many objects named fast and rhythmically (landscapes, oceans, flowers, temples, mountains, saints), chosen for "universal" symbolic force. **Or a guided story.** Images may arise spontaneously as in ***chidākāśa*** (awareness of the inner space in front of the closed eyes), watched as a detached witness without analysis. Usually ends with a spiritual or calming symbol for concentration, often the **golden egg** (*1978*). Birch & Hargreaves list chakras, the liṅga and the cross among the images in the 2009 printing. The 6th edition includes a **Chakra Visualization** practice (p. 151); whether earlier editions did is unknown. | The heart of the "unconscious" theory: images stir up *saṃskāras*, which are watched and so lose their force. Includes a safety note (see below). |
| **7. Sankalpa again** | The same words, repeated at the moment the mind is said to be most receptive. In the book's own Outline this falls under Ending (Birch & Hargreaves). | The 1978 compilation puts the suggestion effect here. |
| **8. Ending (externalization)** | Slowly bring awareness back to breath, body, room and sounds, then movement. **Never end abruptly**: headaches or fear afterwards show the ending was too quick. If that happens, lie down again and watch the breath (*1978*). | In the Introduction's anecdote, the closing words "Hari Om Tat Sat" serve as the cue to get up. The 1981 class ends sitting up and chanting Om. |

**Length.** In 1964 Satyananda speaks of a series lasting 15 to 30 minutes, and of one-hour sessions three times a day for the mentally ill. In 1969 he recommends a full hour. The book offers five graded practices and a short and a long class. The 1978 compilation teaches you to fit the time by **adding or removing whole stages, not by speaking faster**. That is a useful design principle. Nothing I saw says how long the book's own scripts run. **A rough estimate (mine, not the book's):** p. 122 carries about 300–350 words, and Practices 1–5 take 8 to 12 pages each. At a slow guiding pace of about 100 words a minute, that gives very roughly 25–40 minutes of speech per practice before pauses, if the pages are mostly script. As a lineage benchmark, Amritananda's 1981 class has about 1,200 words of guidance, roughly 12 minutes of speech plus unmarked pauses, and it has no visualization stage. **The progression:** the one practice page I saw (p. 122, Practice 5) runs a front and back rotation and the inner organs after the side rounds. What Practices 1–4 contain, and what each adds, is unknown (see the Complete Scheme, p. 132).

**Voice and style.** Directive and second-person. The script is short imperatives and lists of body parts, with almost no explanation inside it; on p. 122 the few asides are short directions (go to the back; feel the heartbeat) and notes on where the inner organs sit. Two habits I saw only in the lineage material, not in the book pages I read. First, the practitioner plays a "double role", instructing themself and following at the same moment (1969 talk; 1978). Second, in a class the teacher may slip in reminders like "no sleeping please" (1978). Satyananda wanted the recording fixed: in 1969 he says the words, emphasis, tone and timing should never change from the first day to the last. The 1978 compilation admits the instructions are given authoritatively and directively, but denies that the method relies on suggestion. It says the only therapeutic content is the practitioner's own resolve, which the instructor does not give. The lineage also softens its own directive voice. The transcripts in *Meditations from the Tantras* build in permissions (if you cannot see the image, imagine it; if it vanishes, that is fine; `satyananda1974`). The 1978 compilation says images may at first only be "imagined". In 1982 Satyananda tells students to listen casually and not to try to go deep. See `07` §7.4 and `08` §2 and §7.

**One stage, paraphrased (rotation; after Amritananda's 1981 class and p. 122).** The guide first says what will happen. Parts of the body will be named; you take your attention to each, picture it and say its name silently, without moving. Then the names follow each other with no room for comment. Right thumb, each finger in turn, then all five fingers together. Palm, back of the hand, wrist, and on up the arm, down the side and leg to the toes. Each toe is named, then all the toes together. The left side follows in the same words. Then the voice climbs the body in right–left pairs, adding "both together" after each pair. It moves over the face and head, down the back, and finally gathers the parts into larger wholes: the whole right side, the whole left side, both arms, both legs, the whole body. On p. 122 the inner organs get a short note on where each one sits the first time through, then are run through again faster, by name only.

**Sleep.** The book and its lineage are **not consistent**. That is worth knowing, because the "never fall asleep" rule is usually pinned on Satyananda:
- **1964 talk:** if you fall asleep it is not yoga nidra, and your *saṃskāras* are not cleared (though staying simply awake is not yoga nidra either).
- **Bogotá talk (undated; published *YOGA* Jan. 1978):** if you fall asleep, the benefits are lost.
- **1978 compilation:** if you fall asleep, your body needed it; if you stay awake, you deepen meditation.
- **6th-edition Introduction:** ambivalent. Sleeping is not a failure, and he suggests it may even help impressions take root. But the practitioner should still intend to stay awake, because sleep is not yoga nidra, and a recording slept through should be played again after waking.
- **1982 talk:** stay "on the border line", aware of the fan and the people on the balcony. Going too deep is wrong too, because then it becomes meditation.

**Teaching cautions in the lineage material (1978).** Choose images carefully: falling, deep water, fire and overhanging objects can trigger phobias, so add reassurance. Never judge students' experiences. Never say "don't worry if you don't see this". Discourage chatting about experiences. Learn from a qualified teacher if you can; otherwise use a tape, or have a family member read the script.

## What's distinctive
- **It fixed the template.** It is the book that made Satyananda's step-by-step method widely known (Wikipedia). The method itself is described in his talks from 1964 on. The sequence (sankalpa – rotation – breath – opposites – visualization – sankalpa – return), the right-thumb rotation, "rapid images" and the "I will..." sankalpa come from Satyananda's teaching and were standardised in this book. *Chidākāśa* already existed as a separate practice (1978 compilation; *Meditations from the Tantras*). The compendium's eight stages in `04` §4.1 follow this book's sequence (seven parts in its own Outline, eight as commonly counted). Boyes (1973) published a French relaxation method called "yoga nidra" earlier, which Wikipedia (citing Birch & Hargreaves) calls the first known modern use of the term. But Satyananda's is the one that spread.
- **The first dedicated monograph, not the first print appearance.** *Meditations from the Tantras* (1974; `satyananda1974`) already teaches yoga nidra in a chapter of about 30 pages, as one tantric tool next to antar mouna and trataka. That book pairs written instructions with class transcripts, but its yoga nidra chapter has not been seen. Read 1976 for the system and its rationale, and 1974 for the earliest Bihar print setting.
- **The founder's own origin story and lineage claims.** The Introduction gives Satyananda's own account of how the method began in Rishikesh (see Core ideas), which no other author can give first-hand. The **nyāsa derivation**, with the actual mantras, appears nowhere else either.
- **Complete scripts and a kit.** Satyananda gives both complete graded scripts (five practices, two classes, plus chakra and children's practices) and, judging by the 1978 compilation and the chapter titles "General Suggestions" and "Complete Scheme", guidance on building new ones. Compare the other books, as the project summaries describe them. Miller's scripts live mainly in the audio transcripts (`miller2005`). Stanley prints six full scripts with audio (`stanley2021`). Lusk's are described as short and modular (`02`). Desai's printed scripts were not seen (`desai2017`).
- **Directive and goal-oriented**, the opposite of Miller's "welcoming" (iRest), Dinsmore-Tuli's "menu not sequence" with an optional sankalpa, and Stanley's sankalpa as a truth you discover. Here the sankalpa is an order to the subconscious, and the practice is "training the mind".
- **Sleep-learning and suggestion as a mechanism.** No other major yoga nidra book builds so openly on hypnopaedia (learning in sleep) and suggestion, while also insisting that the method is not hypnosis. The lineage was never shy of the word. Satyananda speaks of practising with auto-suggestion in 1969. The 1978 compilation calls the resolve auto-suggestion and advises using it intelligently. *Meditations from the Tantras* names autosuggestion openly (`satyananda1974`). Yet the 1978 "Hypnotism" article says the method as a whole does not depend on suggestion, and in 1982 Satyananda says flatly that yoga nidra is not hypnotism. His own stories (the sleeping boy, the Bogotá bed-wetting girl) show him giving suggestions to others. See `08` §10 and `03` §3.12.
- **A built-in 1970s–90s research section**, now useful mainly as history: what the lineage thought counted as evidence.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - The clearest statement of the classic structure, with enough complete scripts to compare stage by stage and enough rules to build new practices.
  - Sound practical teaching craft in the lineage material: varying length by whole stages, gradual endings, care with images, never invalidating experiences.
  - The *pratyāhāra* logic (use each sense up, then move inward) is coherent and teachable.
  - Gourley (2017) calls it extremely valuable and recommends it as a reference for yoga nidra teachers, though repetitive in the middle and with dry spots. Goodreads readers rate it highly (4.32 from 1,124 ratings, 70 reviews, October 2026). Reviewers especially value the scripts and its use as a reference.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **History.** Birch & Hargreaves (2015) describe the method as Satyananda's own systemisation of techniques from different traditions, modern and medieval, with precedents for some parts but not for the whole (Wikipedia sums this up as not found in ancient or medieval texts). *Nyāsa* plausibly supplied a basic sequence, which Satyananda elaborated himself. The resolve stage looks like an innovation drawn from Western auto-suggestion and relaxation therapy. Much of his "stress and tension" language also comes from Western relaxation therapy (via Singleton 2005). Shearer (quoted on Wikipedia) says the links to ancient texts "seem vague at best". The origin accounts differ in emphasis. The 1978 editorial stresses discovery while revising tantric texts (in use from 1962, the date Satyananda also gives in 1975). The Introduction puts a Rishikesh sleep-learning experience first and the tantric study after it.
  - **Science.** I did not read the research section. Bhushan (2001) identifies two items from it: Shealy on sleep-onset insomnia, and Mangalteertham's appendix reporting alpha dominance. Its age (1970s–90s studies in a 1998 edition) is clear from the chapter titles and citations; its methodological quality is unverified. Compared with today's evidence (`06`):
    - Alpha dominance fits the alpha/theta pattern in `06` §6.1. Nothing there supports the 1978 compilation's "deeper than sleep" or claims of delta.
    - The homunculus rationale for the rotation order is listed in `06` §6.3 as a plausible metaphor, not tested.
    - The book's claim that the opposites balance the brain's two hemispheres (2009 printing p. 72, per Birch & Hargreaves) is not tested in anything in `06`.
    - If the appendix "Pictures of the Brain's Activity During Yoga Nidra" shows the Danish PET work (Lou et al. 1999; Kjaer et al. 2002), note that the dopamine study used 8 experienced practitioners and no ordinary-rest control (`06` §6.1). Note too that a 1998 edition predates both papers.
    - Lineage writers built on the same kind of literature. Bhushan's 2001 article suggests yoga nidra may help cure cancer, citing Simonton (whom he describes as running controlled trials) and Meares, and repeats the homunculus "clearing the nerve pathways" idea.
    - Readers on Goodreads, including a psychologist, single out the "Scientific Investigations" as speculative, and one calls the passing hint that yoga nidra might cure cancer irresponsible.
  - **The 1 hour = 4 hours claim.** It comes from the lineage itself (1969 talk; 1978 editorial; the 6th edition as quoted by Bhushan), not from apps.
  - **Authority and tone.** Readers complain that it presents one way as the only right way and claims normative authority it hasn't earned. Some also find the imagery and religious framing foreign.
  - **Ethics.** The Introduction approvingly reports "experiments" on sleeping children and disciples, with no sign of consent. The Bogotá talk (undated; published *YOGA* Jan. 1978) adds a bed-wetting girl given commands while asleep. The Royal Commission into Institutional Responses to Child Sexual Abuse held a public hearing in December 2014 on the lineage's Australian ashram at Mangrove Mountain. It released its report on 14 September 2016 (media release; registered as `rc2016`). Since then these passages read very differently. In December 2014 Matthew Remski asked teachers to stop using Satyananda's books and methods until reparations are made. His reason: "leading people into a vulnerable, trance-like state" with this founder's technique had become intolerable to him. The 1964 talk's insistence on complete reverence for the guru belongs in the same discussion.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The skeleton, as a shared vocabulary.** Name the stages the way readers will meet them elsewhere, credit Satyananda for the modern sequence, and note Boyes (1973) as an earlier relaxation "yoga nidra".
  - **The modular design rule:** lengthen or shorten a practice by adding or dropping whole stages, never by talking faster (1978). Decide your own rule on rotation speed: the 1978 advice (fast first, then slower) and p. 122 (repeat faster) differ.
  - **The *pratyāhāra* sequence as your explanation of why the order works:** sounds, then touch and posture, then body map, breath, feelings, images. It is classical in category, easy to teach and doesn't need brain claims.
  - **The teaching cautions (1978):** image selection and phobias, gradual endings, never invalidating experiences, banning "don't worry if you don't see this". They anticipate today's trauma-sensitive guidance and give you a lineage precedent for it.
  - **Sankalpa craft:** short, positive, unchanging, felt rather than recited, and aimed at a whole-life quality rather than a single habit. You can keep these rules while choosing your own tense and framing.
  - **Satyananda's own flexibility (1975 Copenhagen speech):** yoga nidra sitting, standing or with eyes open, and many forms rather than one. Useful if you teach seated or chair-based versions (`08` §2 and §7).
  - **Primary sources to cite exactly:** the 1964 talk (freely online in the *YOGA* archive), the 1969 and 1975 talks and the Introduction's origin story. Quoted carefully, they make a vivid, honest history section: Satyananda himself calls the method both ancient and his own discovery.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **"One hour = four hours of sleep"** and **"relaxation deeper than sleep".** No evidence (`06` §6.3). If you mention them, say they come from the lineage itself.
  - **Learning in sleep**: the sleeping boy who learned eleven languages, the Bulgarian language experiments (1978 editorial) and the snorers who got up on cue (all described under Core ideas). Present them as lineage stories, not data, and never as a model. Giving instructions to sleeping children without consent is something your book should explicitly *not* recommend.
  - **The claims that the sankalpa never fails and that the subconscious simply obeys.** Untestable, and they can make readers blame themselves. Pair your sankalpa chapter with the debate in `08` §8.
  - **"Not hypnosis" as a flat claim.** The lineage's own use of "auto-suggestion" makes it hard to defend. The compendium's framing, a cousin of hypnosis with the emphasis on staying aware (`03` §3.12), is closer to the sources.
  - **The medical chapters and appendices.** Replace them with the 2025–26 meta-analyses (`06` §6.0): yoga nidra beats doing nothing and is roughly comparable to other relaxation. Don't reproduce the cancer, asthma or heart-disease claims except as history.
  - **Freud/Jung and homunculus explanations.** Use them as metaphor at most.
  - **Origins.** Present *nyāsa* as Satyananda's stated inspiration, not as an unbroken tradition (Birch & Hargreaves; `08` §1).
  - **The fixed, directive voice.** If you write invitationally (`07` §7.4), you can keep the order and still change the grammar. Explain why you depart from the template, because informed readers will notice. The lineage's own softer moments (above) give you precedent.
  - **The sleep rule.** The founder himself said different things at different times, so you can take any position in `08` §3 and cite him for it.
  - **Naming and the lineage.** "Satyananda Yoga Nidra" is trademarked. Decide how you will acknowledge the misconduct findings (`08` §6). A sentence that credits the method's origin and names the 2016 Royal Commission report is the minimum informed readers will expect.
