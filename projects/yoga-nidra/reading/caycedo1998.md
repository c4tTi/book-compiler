## In one paragraph
This is the standard French textbook of Caycedian sophrology, written by Patrick-André Chéné (physician, director of the Académie de sophrologie de Paris since 1991, per Inserm p. 40), prefaced by Caycedo and revised five times as Caycedo kept rebuilding his method. The publisher's description of the 6th edition calls sophrology a "science of consciousness" that harmonises body and mind, uses precise protocols to activate a wider consciousness, and has established itself in medicine, sport, education, gerontology and existential difficulties. The book promises the origins, foundations and "all the fundamental techniques". For a yoga nidra author, four things in it matter, as reported by the sources that quote it: the theory of a **"sophroliminal" level** at the edge of sleep; the **basic sophronisation**, a guided body-region relaxation; the doctrine of the guide's voice, the **terpnos logos**; and its account of what Caycedo **took from yoga, Tibetan Buddhism and Zen**.

## Structure
**Table of contents not available.** As reported:
- **Publisher's description (6th ed.):** origins and foundations, development, the fundamental techniques, and an introduction to the fields of application.
- **Catalogues:** Caycedo's preface and code of ethics (from the 3rd ed.), illustrations, bibliography (1998: pp. 415–423).
- **Secondary sources:** the history of sophrology and Caycedo's journey (Wikipedia cites pp. 34–62 of the 5th/6th ed.); states and levels of consciousness, drawn as a fan diagram (Klipfel, 2001 ed., whose figure is credited "Chéné, 2001"; Inserm p. 13 also describes the fan, but cites "Chéné, 2014", which its bibliography (p. 88) resolves to Chéné's *Initiation à la sophrologie*, not tome 1, and its figure comes from N. Caycedo 2018); the principles; the degrees of Caycedo's Dynamic Relaxation (RDC) and the "Isocay systems" of the body (Klipfel, 2001 ed.); the sophronisation as access to the level where activation techniques are applied (Inserm p. 34, citing Chéné & Caycedo 2014).
- **Edition warning:** the sources disagree on when the 12-degree method was built. Inserm (p. 9, note 7) dates RDC degree 4 to 1984 and degrees 5–12 to 1988–89. Garnier-Petit (p. 11) dates a fourth degree to 1985 and says eight more were added during the 1990s and 2000s. Klipfel (p. 21) says Caycedo gave the 12 degrees their definitive structure between 1995 and 2001. The 1998 printing may therefore already describe all 12 degrees. The secondary sources describe the Isocay body systems only from the 2001 edition onward (Klipfel); whether the 1996/1998 text already uses them is unknown. Expect the 1998 text to differ from what the secondary sources describe.
- **Citation note:** in the Inserm report only "(Chéné & Caycedo, 2014)" refers to tome 1. "(Chéné, 2014)" (for example pp. 13, 15, 34–35) is Chéné's *Initiation à la sophrologie* (Inserm bibliography, p. 88).

## Core ideas
As reported by Inserm (quoting the 2014 printing, plus other authors where marked), Klipfel (citing 2001), Garnier-Petit and Wikipedia. Not checked against the 1998 text.
- **A discipline of consciousness, not just a relaxation.** Consciousness is described in three states: ordinary, pathological and "sophronic" (serene, harmonious). The aim is to make the passing sophronic state more lasting (Garnier-Petit p. 12, not citing the book).
- **Levels are not states.** "Levels" measure vigilance (waking, sophroliminal, sleep); "states" describe quality. The two vary independently.
- **The sophroliminal level** is the zone between waking and sleep, in three layers (upper, middle, lower at the edge of sleep). It is the working level at the start of training. The book is quoted as claiming that memory, imagination and perception are heightened there.
- **The sophronisation is the way in:** a guided process that lowers the level of consciousness to the sophroliminal zone, where "activation" exercises are done. Inserm (p. 35) lists examples: body awareness, projection into a positive future, recalling a positive past, mobilising a value (N. Caycedo 2018, via Inserm p. 35; not from tome 1).
- **Four principles** (Inserm p. 17):
  - **positive action:** any positive action on consciousness echoes through the whole person (this wording is N. Caycedo 2018, via Inserm p. 17);
  - **the body schema as lived reality:** all techniques work through the body (Chéné & Caycedo 2014);
  - **objective reality:** the sophrologist must perceive their own state of consciousness in order to guide (Chéné & Caycedo 2014), and must know where their competence ends (N. Caycedo 2018, via Inserm p. 17);
  - **adaptability:** techniques adapt to the person, not the reverse (Chéné & Caycedo 2014).
- **Phenomenology as method.** Inserm (p. 14) attributes to the book the borrowed Husserlian steps: "return to the thing itself", suspension of judgement, bracketing. For the background (Caycedo's 1963–64 stay with Ludwig Binswanger at Kreuzlingen), Wikipedia cites both *L'aventure de la sophrologie* (1979) and tome 1 (pp. 34 and 83 of the 6th ed.; p. 83 gives a Husserl → Heidegger → Binswanger → Caycedo filiation). In practice this gives the **phenodescription**: after each session the person describes what they felt, without interpretation.
- **"Sources, but not confused with them."** Inserm (p. 11), citing the book, says sophrology draws on hypnosis, relaxation techniques and the "mental procedures" of yoga, Buddhism and Zen. Chéné presents sophrology as born from doctors who wanted to go beyond hypnosis (Wikipedia, p. 62).

## The practice as this book teaches it
**What Caycedo took from yoga.**
- **The journey:** in the mid-1960s (sources differ: 1964–68), Caycedo spent about two years in Asia: India among yogis, Tibetan Buddhist communities in northern India, then Japanese Zen (Wikipedia, citing Esposito; Sofrocay; Maruéjouls; Klipfel).
- **What each tradition gave,** according to Chéné as cited by Wikipedia (p. 44): Raja yoga as the origin of *dhyāna* meditation, Tibetan Mahāyāna Buddhism, and Japanese Zen. Secondary accounts link yoga to the **first** degree of Dynamic Relaxation (discovering the body), Buddhism to the **second**, and Zen to the **third** and to a seated posture.
- **Kept secular.** Sophrology's spokespeople insist Caycedo took exercises, not beliefs (Inserm pp. 11–12).
- **Later distance from Eastern practice.** Inserm (p. 92) describes the Isocay posture as meant to avoid any alteration of consciousness, "unlike hypnosis or certain Oriental procedures". Caycedo's own words, quoted there from a Sofrocay text (not this book), say only that he studied Zen and yoga postures to design one "adapted to Western man".
- **No yoga nidra in any of this.** Nothing I saw mentions yoga nidra, Satyananda or the Bihar School.

**Basic sophronisation (the "body scan").** Reconstructed from Klipfel 2011 (pp. 106–108, a script modelled on the Caycedian method; he cites the 2001 edition for the technique), Garnier-Petit 2017 (p. 18, an induction from Raoul 2010, an obstetric sophrology manual published by InterEditions) and Inserm. **The wording is theirs, not Chéné's.**
1. **Pre-sophronic dialogue.** The guide explains what the session will involve and builds the "sophronic alliance".
2. **Settling.** Seated, back against the chair. The person notices the room, then closes the eyes, sets worries aside (Klipfel's script: they will be found again at the end, "perhaps seen differently"), and notices the points of contact, the light through the eyelids and the calm breath.
3. **Descent, region by region, head to feet.** In Klipfel's version the regions are the five Isocay "systems", each with a focus point: head and face (forehead); neck, shoulders and the outer arms (thyroid area); chest and inner arms (sternum); abdomen (above the navel); lower belly, pelvis and legs (below the navel). In each, the guide names the parts to **release** and then asks the person to sense the region's **form** "without tension". For the head, Klipfel's wording is quoted below. The whole body is then gathered at the navel. Garnier-Petit's induction adds a low, slow breath with a longer out-breath and "let yourself go as if at the very edge of sleep".
4. **A pleasant image or situation,** chosen by the person, lived for a few moments.
5. **Integration pause** (*pause de totalisation*): sensations are welcomed without judgement. Garnier-Petit's script marks it as 2 minutes of silence.
6. **De-sophronisation.** Return in **reverse order**, legs to head, with movement, stretching and yawning, then opening the eyes.
7. **Phenodescription.** The person describes their experience aloud or in writing. In groups, sharing is never compulsory (Sofrocay, via Inserm).

**Length.** No source gives timings for each stage of the basic sophronisation. What the sources do say: the integration pause is about 2 minutes (Garnier-Petit's script); each of the three Caycedian "key techniques" takes about ten minutes (Klipfel p. 115); the practical part of an antenatal sophrology session is about twenty minutes in all (Garnier-Petit p. 20); a whole session lasts 20–60 minutes one to one and 1–1.5 hours in groups (Inserm p. 33). Home practice is ideally 10–15 minutes a day, and some sophrologists give the client a recording of the session (Inserm p. 36).

**Posture.** Sophrology is practised **seated or standing**, or even lying down depending on the school (Inserm p. 33). The Isocay posture (sitting forward on the chair, back straight, light tone in the lower belly) is meant to prevent the drop into sleep and keep the person in the middle layer of the sophroliminal level (Klipfel p. 108).

**Terpnos logos (the guide's voice).** The term comes from Plato's *Charmides*. Caycedo explains the borrowing in *L'aventure de la sophrologie* (1979, p. 94, according to Wikipedia). As described in the sources, the voice is:
- calm, gentle, monotone; it must not stir emotion or direct the intellect, so that the person enters and stays at the sophroliminal level (Garnier-Petit p. 16, citing Chéné's *Initiation à la sophrologie caycédienne*, 2012, not tome 1);
- with words that change from session to session and whose content is left to the person's free choice, which Gaumier (1993, a midwifery dissertation, via Garnier-Petit p. 16) says is what distinguishes it from suggestion.

Whether tome 1 states this doctrine, and the "not suggestion" claim, in these terms is **unverified**.

**Comparison with Satyananda's yoga nidra** (my synthesis for your comparison chapter):

| Element | Basic sophronisation (as reported) | Satyananda yoga nidra (`02`, `04`) |
|---|---|---|
| Target level | The "sophroliminal" level, between waking and sleep | The hypnagogic threshold; "stay awake" |
| Posture | Seated or standing (lying in some schools) | Lying in *śavāsana* |
| Body work | 5 broad regions, head to feet, each **released** and then felt as a form | Rapid **naming** of many points in a fixed order |
| Intention | "Positive action"; later, positive future projects | Sankalpa at the start and end |
| Imagery | One pleasant image of the person's choosing | Rapid images given by the teacher |
| Ending | Reverse-order return, then the person describes their experience | Externalisation; no sharing |
| Voice | Terpnos logos: monotone; content left to the person | Directive and formulaic |
| Length | No stage timings in the sources; about 10–20 min of guided practice reported | Usually 20–45 min |

## What's distinctive
- **The only text in the project from yoga nidra's French twin:** a voice-led, body-region practice aimed at the edge of sleep, built in the same decades as Satyananda's method, from hypnosis, Schultz, Jacobson and phenomenology plus yoga, Tibetan Buddhism and Zen.
- **A named theory of the threshold.** The three-layer sophroliminal level is a more explicit model than any yoga nidra book offers, though it is a model, not a finding.
- **A named doctrine of the guide's voice** (the terpnos logos).
- **A built-in post-practice step**, the phenodescription.
- **A method that moved away from sleep.** The later Isocay material uses a posture built to prevent drowsiness: a useful contrast for debate 3 in `08` ("Is falling asleep failure?").

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:** authoritative for its school (written by a physician who has run a Paris sophrology school since 1991, prefaced by the founder, the text Inserm quotes for sophrology's theory); precise vocabulary ("sophroliminal", "sophronisation", "vivance", "terpnos logos"); openly declares its Eastern and Western sources.
- **Limitations and critiques:**
  - **Evidence.** Inserm (p. 2) found the underlying theory "largely speculative", with too few convincing studies and results too mixed to affirm or refute efficacy.
  - **Is it just hypnosis?** The psychiatrist Léon Chertok argued that sophrology is renamed hypnosis (via Wikipedia). Sophrologists' claim that the terpnos logos is not suggestion (for example Gaumier 1993 via Garnier-Petit, and a practitioner site quoted by Inserm p. 11) is the same claim yoga nidra lineages make, and just as contested (`08` §10). An expert commentary in the Inserm report (p. 129) notes that a sophrology study used phrases such as imagining the body relaxing muscle by muscle, and judges that sophrology in practice relies on suggestion. Whether tome 1 itself makes the "not suggestion" claim is unverified.
  - **Claims of scientific validation, which Inserm calls misleading.** The book claims (Chéné & Caycedo 2014, quoted by Inserm p. 85) that EEG, physiological, pharmacological, psychological and clinical studies have confirmed its "Fan of Consciousness"; that research has "validated the postulates" of sophrology; and that its efficacy against pain is now accepted. Inserm calls this a "discours trompeur" (misleading discourse) that risks drawing patients away from conventional care (p. 85), and judges the underlying theory largely speculative (p. 2). Until you see the studies the book has in mind, treat the sophroliminal level as a model, not a measurement, as you would yoga nidra's hypnagogic claims: in `06` (EEG section) the yoga nidra EEG literature mostly shows alpha and theta, and the "delta while aware" claim rests on one anecdote. Any EEG material in Étévenon & Santerre (see leads below) needs the same check.
  - **Partisan.** It presents Caycedian sophrology as the authentic one and, per Inserm p. 85, its own efficacy and scientific standing as settled. Inserm, quoting Esposito (2018), notes that there is "no longer one but several sophrologies" (*des sophrologies*). Expect a one-sided view of rival schools.
  - **A moving target.** Six editions over 24 years; page numbers and concepts don't carry across editions.
  - **Reception:** no review found. Goodreads has one rating and no text.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **A paragraph naming sophrology** in your history or comparison chapter, for example: "The neuropsychiatrist Alfonso Caycedo founded sophrology in Madrid in 1960 out of clinical hypnosis, and through the 1960s added phenomenology (with Binswanger, 1963–64), Schultz's and Jacobson's relaxation, and, after travelling in Asia in the mid-1960s, exercises from yoga, Buddhism and Zen. Its guided 'sophronisation' targets the same threshold between waking and sleep as yoga nidra." Cite Chéné for the Caycedian account and Inserm 2021 for the evidence.
  - **The convergence as a point in its own right:** two methods from overlapping sources both settled on a voice-guided descent through the body to the edge of sleep. That supports `08` §1 ("ancient word, modern method") better than any claim of descent.
  - **A seated version** for students who can't lie down or always fall asleep, with sophrology as the precedent.
  - **A simpler body tour for beginners:** five broad regions, each released and then felt as a whole, as an on-ramp before the full rotation.
  - **"Name the category, let them choose the content"** ("a pleasant memory of your own choosing"), a citable way to explain invitational guidance (`08` §5).
  - **An optional phenodescription:** a one-line note of sensations after practice, never compulsory in groups.
  - **Voice guidance:** calm, slow, even; not lulling to sleep, not stirring emotion, not lecturing.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Don't write that yoga nidra inspired sophrology, or the reverse.** French yoga authors claim the first [@bonnasse2015fr], but every account I saw names Raja yoga, Tibetan Buddhism and Zen, never yoga nidra; and Caycedo's journey came a decade before Satyananda's book (1976). Write "parallel methods from shared sources".
  - **Don't present the sophroliminal "benefits" as findings.**
  - **Don't repeat the book's claim that EEG and clinical studies have "confirmed" the Fan of Consciousness or that pain relief is established** (Chéné & Caycedo 2014, via Inserm p. 85). Inserm calls this misleading; cite Inserm 2021 instead.
  - **Don't borrow the not-suggestion argument uncritically;** give Chertok's objection alongside it (`08` §10).
  - **Don't use Caycedian trade names as generic terms** ("Sophrologie Caycédienne®", "Méthode Alfonso Caycedo®"). Be careful with French "sophro-nidra" hybrids [@yogarina].
  - **Cite carefully:** Chéné as author, Caycedo for the preface and code of ethics; give the edition you use.
