## In one paragraph
This is the standard French textbook of Caycedian sophrology, written by one of Caycedo's senior physician-teachers. Caycedo prefaced it and added the method's code of ethics. It has been revised five times since 1994 as Caycedo kept rebuilding his method. The publisher's description of the 6th edition calls sophrology a "science of consciousness":
- it harmonises body and mind and brings an "ontological evolution" of the personality;
- precise protocols activate "a new, wider consciousness" and better adaptation to life's situations;
- it is applied in medicine, sport, education, gerontology and existential difficulties.

The book promises origins and foundations, "all the fundamental techniques", and an introduction to the fields of application: a "real précis" that initiates the reader into sophrology "as an art". It is written for trainee and practising sophrologists, many of them health professionals. For a yoga nidra author, four things in it matter, as reported by the sources that quote it:
- its theory of a **"sophroliminal" level** at the edge of sleep;
- the **basic sophronisation** (*sophronisation de base*), a guided body-region relaxation;
- the doctrine of the guide's voice, the **terpnos logos**;
- its account of what Caycedo **took from yoga, Tibetan Buddhism and Zen** after his journey to Asia in the mid-1960s.

- **Content reported by secondary sources** (edition given in brackets):
  - the history and sources of sophrology, including Caycedo's journey, the books he published in India, and the move from hypnosis to sophrology (Wikipedia cites pp. 34, 37, 43, 44 and 62 of the 5th/6th edition);
  - the theory of **states and levels of consciousness**, drawn as a fan diagram (*éventail*) (2001 edition, per Klipfel; 2014 printing, per Inserm);
  - the **principles** of sophrology;
  - the method: the degrees of **Caycedo's Dynamic Relaxation** (RDC), the five-plus-one **"Isocay systems"** of the body, and a table of the "six times" of the method (2001 edition, per Klipfel);
  - the sophronisation and the activation techniques (2014 printing, per Inserm).
- **Edition warning:** the 1998 printing **predates** Caycedo's "Isocay systems" (1999) and "Isocay consciousness" (2001), and his 12-degree method was finalised between 1995 and 2001. The 1998 text will therefore differ from the 2001–2018 material that the secondary sources describe.

## Core ideas
As reported by Inserm (quoting the 2014 printing), Klipfel (citing 2001 and 2008) and Wikipedia (citing the 5th/6th edition). Not checked against the 1998 text.
- **Sophrology is a discipline of consciousness, not just a relaxation.** Relaxation is the means, not the end. Its aim is to make the passing "sophronic" state (serene, harmonious, positively oriented) a lasting one. That state is contrasted with "ordinary" and "pathological" consciousness.
- **Levels are not states.** "Levels" measure vigilance: waking, sophroliminal, sleep and coma. "States" describe quality: pathological, ordinary or sophronic. The two can vary independently.
- **The sophroliminal level** is the zone between waking and sleep, divided into three layers:
  - an upper layer near vigilance;
  - a broad middle layer;
  - a lower layer at the edge of sleep.

  It is the privileged working level at the start of training. The book claims that memory is activated, imagination stimulated and inner and outer perception heightened there, and that a person's capacities and values can be perceived "at the deepest levels of consciousness".
- **The sophronisation is the way in.** It is a guided process that lowers the level of consciousness to the sophroliminal zone. There the "activation" exercises are done: body awareness, projecting into a positive future, recalling a positive past, mobilising a value.
- **Four principles:**
  - **positive action:** any positive action on consciousness echoes through the whole person;
  - **the body schema as lived reality:** everything passes through the body;
  - **objective reality:** the guide knows their own state and the limits of their competence;
  - **adaptability:** techniques adapt to the person, not the reverse.
- **Phenomenology is the method.** Caycedo trained with Ludwig Binswanger in Switzerland and borrowed Husserl's "return to the thing itself", suspension of judgement and bracketing. In practice this gives the **phenodescription**: after each session the person describes what they felt, without interpretation.
- **"Sources, but not confused with them."** Inserm, quoting the book, says sophrology's methodology draws on hypnosis and relaxation techniques and on the "mental procedures" of yoga, Buddhism and Zen, without being confused with them. Chéné also presents sophrology as born from doctors who wanted to renew and go beyond hypnosis (Wikipedia, p. 62).

## The practice as this book teaches it
**What Caycedo took from yoga (the brief's fourth question).** Here is the account given by the book and by the sources around it:
- **The journey:** a two-year trip to Asia: India among yogis, northern India among Tibetan lamas, then Japanese Zen (Rinzai and Sōtō) (Wikipedia, citing Esposito). Binswanger encouraged it, and Caycedo's French wife was a yoga practitioner.
- **The dates differ by source:**
  - 1964–66 (Sofrocay);
  - 1965–67 (Esposito, via Wikipedia; Maruéjouls);
  - 1965–68 (Klipfel; the compendium also says 1965–68).
- **His own books from India:** Wikipedia says he published two books there, *India of yogi* (1966) and *Letters of silence*.
- **What each tradition gave**, according to Chéné as quoted by Wikipedia (p. 44): Raja yoga as the origin of *dhyāna* meditation; traditional Mahāyāna Buddhism of Tibet; and Japanese Zen.
- **How this fed the method,** according to secondary accounts: yoga supplied exercises for the **first degree** of Dynamic Relaxation, which is about discovering the body. Buddhism supplied the meditation method behind the **second** degree. Zen inspired the **third** degree and a seated posture.
- **Kept secular.** Sophrology's own spokespeople insist that Caycedo took exercises, not beliefs (Inserm, pp. 11–12).
- **Later distance from Eastern practice.** In a Sofrocay text quoted by Inserm (not from this book), Caycedo says he studied Zen and yoga postures in order to design a posture "adapted to Western man". He says it is meant to *avoid* alterations of consciousness, "unlike hypnosis or certain Oriental procedures".
- **No yoga nidra in any of this.** Nothing I saw mentions yoga nidra, Satyananda or the Bihar School.

**Basic sophronisation (the "body scan").** This is reconstructed from Klipfel 2011 (which cites the 2001 edition for the technique and models a script on it), Garnier-Petit 2017 and Inserm 2021. **The wording is theirs, not Chéné's.**
1. **Pre-sophronic dialogue.** The guide sets the aim of the session and builds the "sophronic alliance" with the person.
2. **Settling.** Usually seated, back against the chair. The person becomes aware of the room and the points of contact, then closes the eyes (open eyes are allowed, per Inserm) and sets worries aside "until the end of the session".
3. **Descent, region by region, from head to feet.** In later editions the regions are the five "Isocay systems", each with a point of focus:
   - head and face (the forehead);
   - neck, shoulders and the outer arms (the thyroid area);
   - chest and the inner arms (the sternum);
   - abdomen (a point about 4 cm above the navel);
   - lower belly, pelvis and legs (a point about 4 cm below the navel).

   In each region the guide asks the person to **release** the muscles and then sense the region's **form** "without tension". The whole body is then gathered at the navel. The breath is allowed to settle low in the belly, with a longer out-breath, "as if at the very edge of sleep".
4. **A pleasant image or memory,** chosen by the person, lived for a few moments.
5. **Integration pause** (*pause de totalisation*): about two minutes of silence to welcome sensations without judgement.
6. **De-sophronisation.** A return in **reverse order**, from the legs up to the head, with movement, deep breaths, stretching and yawning, then opening the eyes.
7. **Phenodescription.** The person describes their experience aloud or in writing. In groups, sharing is never compulsory (Sofrocay, via Inserm).

**Session length and home practice:** a session lasts 20–60 minutes one to one, longer in groups. Home practice is a daily 10–15 minutes, often with a recording the sophrologist gives the client (Inserm). **Posture:** sophrology is mostly practised **seated or standing**. The lying position is avoided because it invites sleep (Klipfel; Wikipedia), though some schools use it (Inserm). The Caycedian "Isocay" posture keeps a light tone in the lower belly so as not to drop into sleep.

**Terpnos logos (the guide's voice).** The term comes from Plato's *Charmides*, where "beautiful words" bring *sophrosyne*. Caycedo explains the borrowing in *L'aventure de la sophrologie*, 1979, p. 94, according to Wikipedia. As described in the sources, the voice is:
- calm, slow, monotone and soothing; Fiorletta's word, quoted by Inserm, is "persuasive";
- meant neither to put the person to sleep, nor to stir emotion, nor to direct the intellect, so that they stay at the sophroliminal level;
- adapted to what the person said in the opening dialogue, with words that change from session to session.

The guide names the *kind* of representation ("a pleasant image"), but its content is left to the person. Sophrologists say this is what distinguishes the terpnos logos from **suggestion**. The Chéné-specific statements come from his *Initiation à la sophrologie* (cited by Garnier-Petit), not from tome 1. That tome 1 states the doctrine in the same terms is likely but unverified.

**Comparison with Satyananda's yoga nidra** (my synthesis for your comparison chapter):

| Element | Basic sophronisation (as reported) | Satyananda yoga nidra (`02`, `04`) |
|---|---|---|
| Target level | The "sophroliminal" level, between waking and sleep, in three layers | The hypnagogic threshold; "stay awake" |
| Posture | Mostly seated or standing; lying is avoided | Lying in *śavāsana* |
| Body work | 5–6 broad regions, head to feet, each **released** and then felt as a form | Rapid **naming** of many points in a fixed order, right thumb first |
| Intention | "Positive action"; later, a positive future project and three "fundamental capacities" | Sankalpa, repeated at the start and the end |
| Imagery | One pleasant image of the person's own choosing | Rapid images given by the teacher; *chidākāśa* |
| Ending | Reverse-order return, then the person describes their experience | Externalisation; no sharing |
| Voice | Terpnos logos: monotone; content left to the person | Directive and formulaic |

## What's distinctive
- **The only text in the project from yoga nidra's French twin.** It is the reference textbook of a guided, voice-led, body-region practice that targets the edge of sleep. It was built in the same decade as Satyananda's method, from Schultz, Jacobson, hypnosis and phenomenology, plus yoga, Tibetan Buddhism and Zen.
- **A named theory of the threshold.** The "sophroliminal" level, with its three layers, is a more explicit model of the edge of sleep than any yoga nidra book offers, though it is a model, not a finding.
- **A named doctrine of the guide's voice.** No yoga nidra book in the set gives the teacher's voice a theory of its own the way the terpnos logos does.
- **A built-in post-practice step.** The phenodescription asks for a short, non-interpretive description of what was felt. Yoga nidra has no equivalent in Satyananda's format.
- **A method that moved away from the sleep threshold.** Caycedo's later "Isocay" theory (2001) shifted the working level up into a particular kind of wakefulness, with a posture built to prevent drowsiness. That is the opposite of yoga nidra's lying-down approach to the threshold, and a useful contrast for debate 3 in `08` ("Is falling asleep failure?").
- **A Western clinician's account of borrowing from yoga.** It names Raja yoga, Tibetan Buddhism and Zen, and does not mention yoga nidra.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Authoritative for its school: written by a senior Caycedian physician, prefaced by the founder, revised over 25 years, and the work Inserm quotes when it describes sophrology's theory.
  - Precise vocabulary and protocols. It is the place to check what sophrologists mean by "sophroliminal", "sophronisation", "vivance" and "terpnos logos".
  - Openly declares its sources, Eastern and Western, rather than claiming antiquity.
- **Limitations and critiques:**
  - **Evidence.** The Inserm report (2020/21) found the theory "largely speculative". It found too few methodologically convincing studies, with results too mixed to affirm *or* refute any efficacy, and no data on adverse effects. Wikipedia also cites a 2004 French Ministry of Health answer that sophrology is not a recognised therapeutic method (I did not open that source).
  - **Is it just hypnosis?** The psychiatrist Léon Chertok argued that sophrology is hypnosis renamed (via Wikipedia). The book's claim that the terpnos logos is not suggestion is the same claim yoga nidra lineages make, and just as contested (`08` §10).
  - **Partisan.** It presents Caycedian sophrology as the "authentic" sophrology at a time when Caycedo was trademarking his method against rival schools. Inserm notes that there are now "several sophrologies". Expect the book to be one-sided about the rivals.
  - **Broad claims.** In an interview with Inserm (not in the book), Chéné said there are few areas of human activity where sophrology cannot help. The sophroliminal "enhancements" (memory, imagination, perception of values) are asserted, not tested.
  - **A moving target.** Six editions over 24 years, with the method itself redefined between 1995 and 2001. Page numbers and concepts don't carry across editions.
  - **Reception:** no review found. Goodreads has one rating (5/5) and no text.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **A paragraph naming sophrology in your history or comparison chapter.** This matters most for French readers. A defensible wording: "In 1960 the Madrid psychiatrist Alfonso Caycedo founded sophrology, from hypnosis, autogenic training, Jacobson's relaxation and phenomenology. After travelling in India, Tibetan Buddhist communities and Japan in the mid-1960s, he added exercises from yoga, Buddhism and Zen. Its guided 'sophronisation' targets the same threshold between waking and sleep that yoga nidra does." Cite Chéné for the Caycedian account and Inserm 2021 for the evidence.
  - **The convergence as a point in its own right.** Two methods, built in the same decade from overlapping sources, both settled on a voice-guided descent through the body to the edge of sleep. That supports the compendium's "ancient word, modern method" framing (`08` §1) better than any claim of descent from one to the other.
  - **A seated version.** Sophrology has a long, tested practice of doing this work seated or standing so as *not* to fall asleep. Offer a seated yoga nidra for students who can't lie down or who always fall asleep, and point to sophrology as the precedent.
  - **A simpler body tour for beginners.** Five broad regions, head to feet, each released and then felt as a whole, is gentler than a 60-point rotation. It may suit trauma-sensitive or first-time groups as an on-ramp before the full rotation.
  - **The "name the category, let them choose the content" rule.** For example, "a pleasant memory of your own choosing" instead of a prescribed image. It is a clear, citable way to explain invitational guidance (`08` §5).
  - **An optional phenodescription.** A one-line written note of sensations after practice, with no interpretation and never compulsory in groups. It is a cheap, safe way to help students notice their experience.
  - **Voice guidance.** The terpnos logos criteria (calm, slow, even; not lulling to sleep, not stirring emotion, not lecturing) give you a ready checklist for your chapter on the teacher's voice. To hear it done, listen to Chéné's own CDs rather than relying on descriptions.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Don't write that yoga nidra inspired sophrology, or the reverse.** French yoga authors claim the first [@bonnasse2015fr]. But every account I saw names Raja yoga, Tibetan Buddhism and Zen, never yoga nidra or Satyananda. Caycedo's journey (mid-1960s) also came a decade before Satyananda's book (1976). Write "parallel methods from shared sources". This confirms the ⚠️ in `01` and `11`.
  - **Don't present the sophroliminal "benefits" as findings.** Heightened memory, imagination and access to values are theory. The evidence for sophrology is as thin as for yoga nidra (`06`), and Inserm says so plainly.
  - **Don't borrow the terpnos-logos-is-not-suggestion argument uncritically.** If you use it, give Chertok's objection alongside it, as you would for yoga nidra and hypnosis (`08` §10).
  - **Don't use Caycedian trade names as generic terms.** "Sophrologie Caycédienne®" and "Méthode Alfonso Caycedo®" are registered names (Inserm; Wikipedia), and the school guards its core teaching closely. Describe the techniques in plain words. Be careful with French "sophro-nidra" hybrids [@yogarina].
  - **Cite carefully:** Chéné as author, Caycedo as author of the preface and the code of ethics. Give the edition you actually use: the 1998 printing is the 3rd edition (first printed 1996) and predates the Isocay material. The page numbers quoted on Wikipedia belong to the 5th/6th edition.
  - **Skip the rest for your purposes:** the 12-degree method, the Isocay theories, the institutional history and the fields of application add nothing to a yoga nidra book.
