## In one paragraph
*Nidrā Shakti* is a large, illustrated reference book that tries to hold the whole field of yoga nidrā in one place. It covers history and "prehistory", a research review, an A–Z of the practice's components, interviews with dozens of facilitators from rival schools, and a 108-piece anthology of transcribed practices. Its central claim is theological as much as practical. The "active ingredient" of every method, whether Satyananda's, iRest's or a research protocol, is said to be the power of sleep itself, personified as the goddess Yoganidrā / Nidrā Shakti. Yoga nidrā is therefore a naturally arising state that one allows and is welcomed into, not a technique one performs. The book is written for practitioners and facilitators. Its voice is devotional, poetic and polemical: it is anti-trademark, "decolonial", post-lineage and feminist. Its promise is that rest is both healing and a radical, liberating act, and that every trademarked method is one more doorway into the same state.

## Structure
**I did not see a table of contents.** The publisher and its pages describe the book as follows. Section names in quotation marks are the publisher's own; everything else is description.
- **Histories.** A chapter the publisher calls "Histories of Yoga Nidrā", described as "untold histories" of how the practice developed worldwide, plus "prehistory" and "Indigenous roots". The 2022 excerpt shows the range. It moves from the Devī Māhātmya goddess and the Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra, through the poet Lal Ded (Lalleśvarī), to the 19th-century "Relaxationists" (Annie Payson Call), Yeats, Edison and Dalí. It ends with the gurus Satyananda, Swami Rama and Amrit Desai, and the US stage hypnotist Ormond McGill.
- **Research.** A chapter the publisher calls "Yoga Nidrā (Shakti) as Medicine", described as a review of scientific research. The *Yoga Magazine* reviewer says it covers about 50 years of studies.
- **A–Z.** An illustrated, cross-referenced A–Z guide to aspects and components of the practice.
- **"Worlds of Yoga Nidrā".** Interviews with and contributions from 43 facilitators. The Network names Kamini Desai, Julie Lusk, Richard Miller, Rod Stryker, Pandit Rajmani Tigunait, Nirlipta Tuli and Theodora Wildcroft. The shop page also mentions testimonies from more than 50 practitioners.
- **Threshold practices.** Sleep and dream-threshold practices from other traditions. The reviewer mentions Xhosa dream practices, the Dreamtime of Aboriginal Australia and Celtic dream spaces. The excerpt mentions the Bön goddess of lucid sleep and Svapneśvarī.
- **Philosophy and the goddess.** Philosophy, and the goddess Yoganidrā Shakti Devī, whom the book "restitutes" as presiding deity. It is illustrated with paintings, including Vishnu asleep under her influence.
- **For facilitators.** Practical guidance, comparative studies of methods, and an "in-depth" section on ethics and guidelines for teaching (per the reviewer).
- **"Treasury of Transcripts".** 108 practices from over 30 facilitators. Going by the publisher's online notes, it is roughly arranged as follows:
  - Nos. 1–65: community, co-creative and Covid-lockdown live practices, guest facilitators, children's practices (38–42), the ten Mahāvidyā goddess practices (43–51), and therapeutic practices (chemotherapy, breast cancer, pregnancy, problem-solving).
  - Nos. 66–77: teaching practices from a 2018 Kerala teacher training, all "structured according to the same set of nine components".
  - Roughly nos. 77–97: trainees' "student" practices from Kerala and Cumbria.
  - Nos. 98–102: practices for the "seasons" of the menstrual cycle.
  - Nos. 103–108: practices from the Well Woman Yoga Therapy trainings (menopause, closing and integration).
  - 105 of the 108 numbers are labelled online as Treasury practices. Nos. 18 and 24 appear in the library without the label, and I did not see no. 100.
- **Back matter.** Sanskrit glossary, chronology, full index, bibliography.

## Core ideas
- **Yoga nidrā is a natural state, not a method.** Every school's method (the author counted 13 named methods by 2022) is just a different route to the same experience of the sleep threshold. That experience is the "active ingredient", and the branded methods are packaging around it. The *Yoga Therapy Today* interview uses exactly this box-and-contents image.
- **The goddess restored.** Yoganidrā / Nidrā Shakti, the sleep goddess of the Devī Māhātmya, presented as a form of Kālī, is put back at the centre. The book argues that commercial and traditional schools have stripped her out and trademarked what remained.
- **Sleep is welcomed, not policed.** The excerpt mocks North Indian schools that tell students not to fall asleep and so make them rigid. Some Treasury practices are designed to end in sleep (no. 25 "Sleep nidrā"; children's bedtime practices).
- **Rest as liberation.** Rest is framed as a "radical act" against capitalism's theft of sleep (the "Decolonising Sleep" essays). It also restores rhythmic cycles: breath, sleep, digestion, menstruation, creativity.
- **Post-lineage practice.** The Network borrows "post-lineage" from Theodora Wildcroft's research and presents Total Yoga Nidrā as its answer to harmful and exclusionary experiences in traditional yoga nidrā lineages (Operating Ethics). The excerpt lists Satyananda, Swami Rama and Amrit Desai among the gurus whose reputations are compromised. Facilitators should improvise for the people in front of them, the place and the season, not follow guru-dictated scripts or research protocols.
- **Openly hypnotic.** The book treats hypnosis as kin, not a threat. Nirlipta Tuli's Treasury practices count down from 54, 27 or 9 (fractions of 108) in no. 34, and use "conscious overloading", the illusion of choice and humour to bring on sleep in no. 25. The training practices use storytelling as a hypnotic device (no. 68). The author's own summary is that people find the practice therapeutic, meditative and "mostly hypnotic" (see quotes).
- **Permission, consent and accessibility.** Invitations and questions replace commands. The interview has the author offering more props and permission to stay lying down at the end. There is a rotation designed for trans and queer practitioners with dysphoria or body dysmorphia (no. 10, by Simran Uppal), and a trauma-informed frame.
- **The life cycle as initiation.** Practices for menarche, the menstrual "seasons", pregnancy, birth, the postnatal period and menopause, carried over from *Yoni Shakti* (2014).
- **Creativity and intuition** are named as gifts of the state, alongside healing.

## The practice as this book teaches it
The book does not prescribe a single script. What I could see is a **framework of nine components** that is delivered flexibly, improvised and often co-created with the listeners. The Treasury exists to show how many ways each component can be done. Below are the stages as they appear in the 2022 excerpt, which is itself structured like a nidrā (its numbered section headings), and in the excerpt's hand-drawn **labyrinth** diagram. In the diagram, numbered points 1–9 run through rings labelled Externalising, Settling, Inviting Intent, Savouring Intent, Intuition and Body Parts, with Paradox at the centre.

| Component (numbered as in the 2022 excerpt) | Satyananda baseline (see `02`) | *Nidrā Shakti* / Total Yoga Nidrā (as seen) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Preparation | Lie in śavāsana, stay still, resolve to stay awake (`08` §3) | Arrival and permission: be as comfortable as possible, use props and blankets, you have arrived and there is nothing to do. Can open with a Sanskrit prayer to the goddess (no. 11), humming (no. 82), or an imagined walk on a beach (no. 84). |
| 2 Relaxation / settling | Body and sound awareness | "Settling in": spacious, unhurried. Sometimes a breath practice, e.g. four-part prāṇāyāma (no. 58) or coherent breathing (no. 72). |
| 3 Sankalpa | Short positive resolve, repeated 3× | **Intent**, "invited" early and "savoured" later. It is reframed: the excerpt's resolve is to return, repair and restore. Lusk's script (no. 9) uses a "heartfelt pledge" at the start and end. Two practices (no. 73) explore *saṃkalpa / nirvikalpa* as a cycle and as embodied enquiry. I could not confirm whether the book calls intent optional. |
| 4 Rotation of consciousness | Fixed order, right thumb first | **Many rotations, taught side by side**: Satyananda (nos. 76, 82, 97), the Himalayan "61 points" and marma itinerary (nos. 58–60, 79, 80, 84), the iRest itinerary (nos. 85, 94), a head-to-toe route taken from the Devī Kavacam nyāsa (no. 1), labyrinth (no. 15), the Mahāvidyā "medicine wheel" (no. 103), triangles and diamonds (nos. 23, 31), a two-fold accessible rotation (no. 10), and a "multiple rotations" teaching practice (no. 70). Rotations are often sung or carry an image to each point: a smile, flowers, moon nectar, snowflakes, light, holy oil. |
| 5 Opposites | Pairs of sensations/feelings | **Paradox at the centre of the labyrinth**: pairs of opposites, e.g. the body as a house filling and emptying with the breath (no. 64). Also a four-cornered *catuṣkoṭi* (tetralemma) rotation for problem-solving (no. 62) and a practice for transcending polarities (no. 14). |
| 6 (not identified) | Breath awareness. In Satyananda it comes *before* opposites. | The 2022 excerpt does not show a sixth heading, so I could not identify this component. |
| 7 Imagery / return to intent | Rapid images, guided visualization; sankalpa again | "Return to resolve, to connect with intuition": a story or inner journey, open space for insight and creativity, dream recall (no. 52), poems sung or read (Hafiz, Lalla, John O'Donohue). |
| 8 Externalising | Return to body and room | "Externalise, contextualise and bring us home": a gradual return. Permission not to get up. |
| 9 Close | Formal ending | Completion and thanks. Sometimes a mantra or a Shanti Path. |

- **Length:** the Treasury practices run from about 9 to 42 minutes, mostly 15–30. A "bare bones" complete practice is 15 minutes (no. 71), and that is the brief trainees are given. One teaching practice uses only about 800 words in roughly 20 minutes (no. 30), and another is deliberately minimal (no. 29).
- **Posture:** lying down and heavily propped. Many practices are recorded outdoors ("Wild Nidrā" at dawn and dusk).
- **Voice:** invitational and permissive by default. A Satyananda-format practice by Nirlipta Tuli is rewritten with questions instead of commands (no. 33), and there is a model invitational script (no. 74). There are also two-voice and multi-voice practices, singing, harmonium and singing bowls, children's stories, and humour.
- **Hypnotic elements:** used openly (nos. 25, 28, 32, 34, 68, 75).
- **Example, paraphrased (no. 11):** after a Sanskrit invocation, the listener is invited to bring a smile of love and acceptance to each place in the body in turn, and a sung poem follows near the end.

## What's distinctive
- **The only cross-school anthology of its kind.** Practices from Satyananda-, Himalayan- and iRest-style teachers sit next to tantric, children's, clinical, hypnotic and community-improvised ones, each with dated context notes: who, where, for whom, and why. No other book in `02` lets you compare rotations and openings across schools on the page.
- **Interviews across rival schools.** Founders and senior teachers of competing schools (Miller, Desai, Stryker, Tigunait) appear in one book, alongside a scholar of post-lineage yoga (Wildcroft).
- **The fullest case for the goddess.** It argues that yoga nidrā "belongs" to the sleep goddess Yoganidrā, and connects the nyāsa armour of the Devī Kavacam to modern rotation in a concrete, checkable way (no. 1).
- **Hypnosis embraced.** On debate 10 (`08`) it takes the "close cousin" position openly, where the lineages say "no".
- **Clear positions on the debates.** On misconduct it goes beyond "acknowledge briefly": its whole method is built as a response to the lineages (debate 6, third option). On falling asleep it welcomes sleep (debate 3). On scripts it goes as far as possible toward free and improvised (debate 7).
- **Breadth of life-cycle material.** Menstrual, menopausal, pregnancy and children's material and practices for marginalised groups, wider than any other yoga nidrā book.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Huge comparative breadth and generous attribution of other teachers' work.
  - Transcripts with audio, which you can actually listen to and compare.
  - A clear ethical stance (consent, trauma-informed, inclusive).
  - Practical facilitator guidance.
  - Scholars of yoga gave the publisher endorsement blurbs: Mark Singleton praises the "new scholarship" and the paintings, and Jason Birch its "contribution" to yoga studies. These are blurbs, not reviews.
  - A good bibliography and chronology, judging by the publisher's description.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **Few independent reviews.** The only substantive review I found is Caroline Shola Arewa's in *Yoga Magazine* (Jan 2025). It is enthusiastic ("a Mother-piece") and praises the book as easy to read despite its 700 pages. But Arewa says she was interviewed for the book, so she is not independent. I found no scholarly review.
  - **The research is told as celebration, not critically appraised.** The 2022 excerpt retells trials as success stories, contradicts itself on its count (84 studies since 1970 on one page, more than 84 since 2016 on the next), and mixes healing testimonies (pain lessened, sight returned) with study results. The Network's academic-resources page repeats the single-study "65% dopamine" figure without caveats. The book appeared before the 2025–26 meta-analyses (`06` §6.0), which find effects shrink against active controls.
  - **Shaky dating in places.** The excerpt dates the Devī Māhātmya to "c600 BC". Scholars usually place it around the 5th–6th century CE, and the Network's own note on practice no. 1 calls the related Kavacam "sixth century". Check the book's chronology before borrowing any date.
  - **Interpretation presented as history.** "Indigenous roots" and the goddess as "active ingredient" are devotional and comparative readings. They do not trace historical transmission. Birch & Hargreaves (2015) show that the word *yoganidrā* means different things in different texts (`08` §1).
  - **A partisan voice.** It is written by founders of a rival post-lineage school with a stated grievance against the lineages. Its history of Satyananda should be checked against primary documents such as the Royal Commission report (`08` §6).
  - **Practical barriers.** The book is expensive and heavy (£45, 704 pp., shipped from the UK), is listed only in hardback, and its online transcripts are locked to buyers. The devotional, punning prose suits some readers and not others.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **A rotation comparison table.** Use the Treasury as a pattern library: Satyananda, Himalayan 61-point, iRest, the Kavacam nyāsa route, labyrinth, triangles, the Mahāvidyā wheel, the tetralemma. Present the rotations side by side and credit each school. Nothing else in your sources lets you do this so concretely.
  - **The nyāsa → rotation link via the Devī Kavacam (no. 1).** It is the most specific, checkable bridge between traditional *nyāsa* and the modern body rotation. Verify the verses and the dating in an Indological source (Mallinson & Singleton; Birch & Hargreaves) before you rely on it.
  - **Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra v. 75.** The verse on the moment between waking and sleep makes a strong primary-text epigraph. Use a scholarly translation, not the excerpt's verse rendering.
  - **Invitational language, with models.** No. 33 (Satyananda structure, permissive wording) and no. 74 (fully invitational) are ready-made before-and-after examples for a chapter on voice (`08` §5).
  - **Concrete length benchmarks.** A complete practice in 15 minutes; about 800 words for 20 minutes. Use these when you size your own scripts.
  - **Accessibility techniques.** The two-fold rotation for dysphoria and dysmorphia (no. 10), permission-giving, props, and permission to stay lying down. Credit them.
  - **A citable source for interviews.** The "Worlds of Yoga Nidrā" interviews are good for first-hand statements from Miller, Desai, Stryker and Tigunait, with page numbers once you have the book.
  - **"Post-lineage" as a frame.** If you want a non-guru framing, take the term from Wildcroft's research and credit her, rather than from this book.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Health claims and the research narrative.** Don't import them. Use `06` and the meta-analyses instead, and cite this book's research chapter only as a bibliography lead.
  - **The goddess as historical fact.** You can present "Nidrā Shakti as the active ingredient" as one teacher's devotional reading. Do not present it as history.
  - **"Indigenous roots" claims.** Avoid suggesting that the world's dream practices descend from, or belong to, yoga nidrā.
  - **Dates.** Don't take dates from it, e.g. the Devī Māhātmya, without checking.
  - **Hypnotic techniques.** If you use them (counting down, conscious overloading, illusion of choice), tell the reader what they are. That honesty is part of consent. It also bears on debate 10.
  - **Its framing and vocabulary.** Total Yoga Nidrā, Wild Nidrā, Yoni Nidrā and Nidrā Shakti are the Network's own names. Since your book will be compared with this one, set your own framing and positions in `workbench/voice-and-positions.md` first. One way to stand apart: evidence-calibrated, more structured, shorter.
  - **Its account of Satyananda.** Cite it as one informed but partisan account, alongside the primary sources.
  - **Credit line.** Cite it as Dinsmore-Tuli, U. (compiler and author), with contributions by N. Tuli, 2024. Don't list Nirlipta Tuli as co-author.
