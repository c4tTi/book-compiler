## In one paragraph
*Nidrā Shakti* is a large, illustrated reference book that tries to hold the whole field of yoga nidrā in one place. It covers history and "prehistory", a research review, an A–Z of the practice's components, interviews with dozens of facilitators from rival schools, and a 108-piece anthology of transcribed practices. Its central claim is theological as much as practical. The "active ingredient" of every method, whether Satyananda's, iRest's or a research protocol, is said to be the power of sleep itself, personified as the goddess Yoganidrā / Nidrā Shakti. Yoga nidrā is therefore a naturally arising state that one allows and is welcomed into, not a technique one performs. The book is written for practitioners and facilitators. Its voice, as far as the excerpt shows, is devotional, poetic and polemical: it is anti-trademark, "decolonial", post-lineage and feminist. Its promise is that rest is both healing and a radical, liberating act, and that every trademarked method is one more doorway into the same state.

## Structure
**Table of contents not available.** I did not see one. The publisher and its pages describe the book as follows. Names in single quotation marks are spelled as on the publisher's home page, which uses no macrons; everything else is description.
- **Histories.** A chapter the home page calls 'Histories of Yoga Nidra'; the shop page speaks of "untold histories" of how the practice developed worldwide, and the book page of "prehistory" and "Indigenous roots". The 2022 excerpt shows the range. It moves from the Devī Māhātmya goddess and the Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra, through the poet Lal Ded (Lalleśvarī), to the 19th-century "Relaxationists" (Annie Payson Call), Yeats, Edison and Dalí. It also names the gurus Satyananda, Swami Rama and Amrit Desai and the US stage hypnotist Ormond McGill (pp. 23–24).
- **Research.** A chapter the publisher's home page calls 'Yoga Nidra as Medicine'; the shop page describes a "review of scientific research into the medicine of yoga nidrā". The *Yoga Magazine* reviewer says it covers about 50 years of studies.
- **A–Z.** The home page's 'A-Z Guide': an illustrated, cross-referenced guide to aspects and components of the practice.
- **'Worlds of Yoga Nidra'.** Interviews with and contributions from 43 facilitators. The home page names Kamini Desai, Julie Lusk, Richard Miller, Rod Stryker, Pandit Rajmani Tigunait, Nirlipta Tuli and Theodora Wildcroft. The shop page also mentions testimonies and contributions from more than 50 practitioners.
- **Threshold practices.** Sleep and dream-threshold practices from other traditions. The reviewer mentions Xhosa dream practices, the Dreamtime of Aboriginal Australia and Celtic dream spaces. The excerpt mentions the Bön goddess of lucid sleep and Svapneśvarī.
- **Philosophy and the goddess.** Philosophy, and the goddess Yoganidrā Shakti Devī, whom the book "restitutes" as presiding deity (shop page). Arewa reports colour plates, some showing Vishnu asleep under her influence.
- **For facilitators.** Practical guidance and comparative studies of methods (shop page). The publisher's book page lists "Ethical Guidance for facilitators", and Arewa calls the section on ethics and guidelines for teaching "in-depth" (not seen).
- **'Treasury of Transcripts'.** 108 practices from over 30 facilitators. Going by the publisher's online notes, it is roughly arranged as follows:
  - Nos. 1–65: community, co-creative and Covid-lockdown live practices, guest facilitators, children's practices (38–42), nine Mahāvidyā practices covering all ten goddesses (43–51; no. 47 combines Mātangi and Kamalātmika), taken from the card deck's set of ten nidrās, and therapeutic practices (chemotherapy 54, breast cancer 55, pregnancy 56–57, problem-solving 62).
  - Nos. 66–76: teaching practices from a December 2018 Kerala teacher training, each "structured according to the same set of nine components" (note on no. 66, which numbers the set "#66 – 77").
  - Nos. 77–97: trainees' "student" practices, from Kerala (77–88) and Cumbria (89–97) (note on no. 71).
  - Nos. 98, 99 and 101 (no. 100 not seen): practices for the "seasons" of the menstrual cycle.
  - Nos. 102–108: practices tied to the Yoni Shakti Well Woman course and trainings, including menopause (104, 107) and a closing integration practice (108).

## Core ideas
- **Yoga nidrā is a natural state, not a method.** Every school's method (the author counted 13 named methods by 2022) is just a different route to the same experience of the sleep threshold. That experience is the "active ingredient", and the branded methods are packaging around it. The *Yoga Therapy Today* interview uses exactly this box-and-contents image.
- **The goddess restored.** Yoganidrā / Nidrā Shakti, the sleep goddess of the Devī Māhātmya, presented as a form of Kālī, is put back at the centre. The excerpt's postscript argues that commercial and traditional schools have stripped her out and trademarked what remained.
- **Sleep is welcomed, not policed.** The excerpt mocks North Indian schools that tell students not to fall asleep and so make them rigid (p. 26). Some Treasury practices are designed to end in sleep (no. 25 "Sleep nidrā"; no. 42, a children's bedtime story). I could not tell whether the book also draws the line between yoga nidrā and sleep that *Yoga Nidra Made Easy* draws, with its stay-awake tips (see `dinsmoretuli2020.md`).
- **Rest as liberation.** Rest is framed as a "radical act" against capitalism's theft of sleep (the excerpt's coda, p. 34, and the "Decolonising Sleep" essays). It is also framed as restoring "rhythmic cycles" (the Decolonising Sleep essay title; *Kith Review* pp. 27, 35); I found no source that lists which cycles.
- **Post-lineage practice.** Uma takes the term "post-lineage" from Theodora Wildcroft's research (*Yoga Therapy Today*, 2019). The Network's Operating Ethics page presents Total Yoga Nidrā as its response to "exclusionary, harmful and limiting" experiences in traditional yoga nidrā lineages. The excerpt lists Satyananda, Swami Rama and Amrit Desai among "a crew of yogic gurus with ambiguous reputations" (*Kith Review*, p. 24). Facilitators should improvise for the people in front of them, the place and the season, not follow guru-dictated scripts or research protocols (postscript, p. 36).
- **Openly hypnotic.** Hypnosis is treated as kin, not a threat. Per the publisher's notes, Nirlipta Tuli's no. 34 counts down from 54, 27 or 9 (fractions of 108), and no. 25 uses "conscious overloading", the illusion of choice and humour to bring on sleep. A training practice uses storytelling as a hypnotic device (no. 68). The author's own summary is that people find the practice therapeutic, meditative and "mostly hypnotic" (see quotes).
- **Permission and accessibility.** Invitations and questions are meant to replace commands (notes on nos. 33 and 74), though the no. 33 recording is still mostly plain instructions. The earlier reading of the yogatherapy.health interview noted the author offering more props and permission to stay lying down at the end; the page did not load when I rechecked it, so treat that as unverified. No. 10 (Simran Uppal), made with LGBTQ young people, uses what its note calls a "two-fold" rotation, aimed at practitioners with gender dysphoria or body dysmorphia. The Network's Operating Ethics page commits to trauma-informed practice.
- **The life cycle.** Per the Treasury notes: menarche (45), the menstrual "seasons" (98, 99, 101), pregnancy (56–57) and menopause (104, 107). Birth appears only as the use of no. 33 in birth rehearsals for expectant fathers. The excerpt also mentions childbirth and postnatal depression in readers' testimonies and a Thai postnatal study (pp. 28–31), but I found no postnatal practice. Several of these practices were recorded on the Yoni Shakti Well Woman trainings or sit in that course (notes on 98, 99, 101–103 and 107); `02` says the life-cycle themes come from the book *Yoni Shakti* (2014), but I did not see that stated in a source.
- **Creativity and intuition** are named as gifts of the state, alongside healing (excerpt pp. 31–34).

## The practice as this book teaches it
The book does not prescribe a single script. What I could see is a **framework of nine components** that is delivered flexibly, improvised and often co-created with the listeners. The Treasury exists to show how many ways each component can be done. The table below takes the components from the 2022 excerpt, which is itself structured like a nidrā (its numbered section headings). The excerpt also has a hand-drawn **labyrinth** diagram (p. 30). Its rings are labelled, from the outside in, Externalising, Settling, Inviting Intent, Savouring Intent, Intuition and Body Parts, with Paradox at the centre, and numbered points 1–9 lie along the path; I could not read a one-to-one mapping between the points and the rings from the scan.

**The same framework in *Yoga Nidra Made Easy*.** The authors' 2022 Hay House book sets out the same nine-part labyrinth (see `dinsmoretuli2020.md`): a **fixed order** with variable content, ingredient 6 as optional "imaginative capacity", and a resolve that is "not necessary" (p. 75). The Treasury notes point the same way: the Kerala teaching practices share "the same set of nine components", and the Kerala beach practices show "all components" in "the usual cyclical progression" (note on no. 67). So the order is probably fixed here too, but check component 6 and the sankalpa position in the book.

- **Length:** the Treasury practices run from about 9 to 42 minutes, mostly 15–30. A "bare bones" complete practice is 15 minutes (no. 71), and that is the brief trainees are given. One teaching practice uses only about 800 words in roughly 20 minutes (no. 30), and another is deliberately minimal (no. 29).
- **Posture:** lying down; no. 33 gives the classic flat śavāsana instructions. Several practices were recorded outdoors ("Wild Nidrā" at dawn and dusk, nos. 35–37, 67).
- **Voice:** meant to be invitational and permissive. The publisher's note says a Satyananda-format practice by Nirlipta Tuli (no. 33) uses permissive instructions framed as questions or invitations; its recording is in fact mostly plain instructions with some permission phrases (see below). There is also a model invitational script (no. 74), which I have not heard or read. There are also two-voice and multi-voice practices, singing, harmonium and singing bowls, children's stories, and humour.
- **Hypnotic elements:** used openly (nos. 25, 28, 32, 34, 68, 75).
- **Example from the publisher's note (no. 11):** per the note, no. 11 opens with a Sanskrit prayer, uses a smile as the image carried round the body, and ends with a sung Hafiz poem. I have not heard it.
- **Example from a recording (no. 33, Nirlipta Tuli, about 19 minutes; from a machine transcript of the free audio, which I did not listen to).** The order is preparation (posture and stillness), an explanation of the resolve, a Satyananda rotation starting at the right thumb, opposites, breath, the space behind the closed eyes, and a slow return. Here is the opposites-and-breath section, paraphrased. After the rotation ends on the whole body, the guide invites heaviness, as if the body might leave an imprint where it lies and the ground were cradling it; then lightness, then coolness, then warmth, each let go before the next. Breath awareness follows (Satyananda puts it before the opposites): the listener notices the body rising and falling, then on each out-breath sweeps awareness from the crown down to the fingertips and toes, letting tension drain into the floor. The sweep is then pictured as a wave of golden light and repeated for a few rounds, before attention moves to whatever shapes, colours or light appear behind the eyelids. The resolve is explained at the start as a short positive sentence said three times, with the option of simply thinking about a direction instead; I found no point near the end where it is repeated.
- **Example from the excerpt (the paradox stage, p. 29).** The excerpt is a literary piece shaped like a nidrā, not a practice transcript. Its paradox section begins after awareness has been carried round the resting body. It names the paradox as being aware while resting in lucid sleep, then turns to the reader with direct questions, asking whether they can sense it. The same habit of addressing the listener with questions runs through the excerpt: whether they can let themselves be nowhere and everywhere, whether they can notice the in-between just before dropping off, and, before the journey starts, whether they are resting comfortably.
- **Typical phrasing patterns.** In the excerpt: questions in place of commands ("can you…?"), "I wonder…" openers, a comfort check before the journey begins, and warm address ("dear one", "dear pilgrim"). In the no. 33 transcript: mostly plain present-tense instructions ("feel…", "become aware of…") and rapid naming of body parts, softened by a few permission phrases ("you can…", "you may find it useful…", "when you feel ready…"). I found no question forms in no. 33, so the publisher's note overstates how permissive that recording is.

## What's distinctive
- **The largest cross-school anthology I found.** Practices from Satyananda-, Himalayan- and iRest-style teachers sit next to tantric, children's, clinical, hypnotic and community-improvised ones, each with a dated context note: who, where, for whom, and why. None of the other books described in `02` offers this, going by their entries there. The publisher's page quotes Tracee Stanley's blurb that "never before" has so much about yoga nidrā been shared in one book.
- **Interviews across rival schools.** According to the publisher, founders and senior teachers of competing schools (Miller, Desai, Stryker, Tigunait) appear in one book, alongside a scholar of post-lineage yoga (Wildcroft).
- **A sustained case for the goddess.** It argues that yoga nidrā "belongs" to the sleep goddess Yoganidrā, and the publisher's note on no. 1 connects the nyāsa armour of the Devī Kavacam to modern rotation in a concrete, checkable way.
- **Hypnosis embraced.** On debate 10 (`08`) it takes the "close cousin" position openly, where the lineages say "no".
- **Positions on the debates.** On misconduct, the Network's Operating Ethics page presents its whole method as a response to harms in the lineages (debate 6, third option); the excerpt itself only names gurus "with ambiguous reputations". On falling asleep, it welcomes sleep in some practices and mocks schools that forbid it (debate 3); check whether it also keeps the 2020 book's line between yoga nidrā and sleep. On scripts it leans hard toward free and improvised content (debate 7), though within a set nine-part frame.
- **Breadth of life-cycle material.** Menarche, menstrual seasons, pregnancy and menopause practices, children's practices, and practices made for marginalised groups (nos. 10, 14). That is wider than the other yoga nidrā books in `02`, going by their entries there.
- **What it adds over *Yoga Nidra Made Easy*.** For the nine-part method itself, *Made Easy* is enough: it is cheaper, has a previewable table of contents, and its 22 companion recordings stream free (see `dinsmoretuli2020.md`). *Nidrā Shakti* adds the 108-practice Treasury with transcripts, the cross-school interviews, the histories and goddess material, the research chapter and the A–Z.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Huge comparative breadth and generous attribution of other teachers' work (going by the Treasury notes).
  - Transcripts with audio, which you can listen to and compare. Many recordings are free on public pages.
  - An ethical stance stated in public: inclusive and trauma-informed (per the Network's Operating Ethics page), plus an "in-depth" ethics and guidelines section (per Arewa). Not verified in the book.
  - Practical facilitator guidance and comparative studies of methods (per the publisher's description; not verified in the book).
  - Scholars of yoga gave the publisher endorsement blurbs: Mark Singleton praises the "new scholarship" and the paintings, and Jason Birch its "contribution" to yoga studies. These are blurbs, not reviews.
  - It has a Sanskrit glossary, chronology, full index and bibliography (per the publisher); I have not seen how good they are.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **Few independent reviews.** The only substantive review I found is Caroline Shola Arewa's in *Yoga Magazine* (Jan 2025). It is enthusiastic ("a Mother-piece") and praises the book as easy to read despite its 700 pages. But Arewa says she was interviewed for the book, so she is not independent. I found no scholarly review, and a web search found no Goodreads or Amazon listing (direct searches on those sites were blocked).
  - **The research is told as celebration, not critically appraised.** The 2022 excerpt retells trials as success stories, contradicts itself on its count (84 studies since 1970 on p. 29, more than 84 since 2016 on p. 30), and mixes healing testimonies (pain lessened, sight returned) with study results. It also says early brain studies (Topeka, Copenhagen, London) found that brainwave states and dopamine levels could be consciously controlled through the practice (p. 30). The Network's academic-resources page repeats the single-study "65% dopamine" figure without caveats. The book appeared before the 2025–26 meta-analyses (`06` §6.0), which find effects shrink against active controls.
  - **Sleep-equivalence and brainwave stages: not found.** I found no "1 hour = 4 hours of sleep" claim and no delta, theta or gamma language in the excerpt, the publisher's pages or the Treasury notes. Whether the research chapter repeats such claims is unknown. *Made Easy* debunks "20 minutes = 2 hours" but promotes delta and gamma "recipes" (pp. 200–204), so expect a similar mix.
  - **Safety and contraindications: not seen.** I did not see the ethics and guidelines section. Nothing I read mentions contraindications such as dissociation, psychosis or severe depression. The Treasury includes practices for chemotherapy (54), breast cancer (55), chronic disease (17), pregnancy (56–57) and dysphoria (10), and one hypnotic practice deliberately uses "dissociative multiple bodies" (note on no. 28), which `07` §7.1 flags as a risk for people prone to dissociation.
  - **Shaky dating in places.** The excerpt dates the Devī Māhātmya to "c600 BC" (p. 23). Scholars usually place it around the 5th–6th century CE, and the Network's own note on practice no. 1 calls the related Kavacam "sixth century". Check the book's chronology before borrowing any date.
  - **Interpretation presented as history.** "Indigenous roots" and the goddess as "active ingredient" are devotional and comparative readings. They do not trace historical transmission. Birch & Hargreaves (2015) show that the word *yoganidrā* means different things in different texts (`08` §1).
  - **A partisan voice.** It is written by founders of a rival post-lineage school with a stated grievance against the lineages. Its history of Satyananda should be checked against primary documents such as the Royal Commission report (`08` §6).
  - **Practical barriers.** The book is expensive and heavy (£45, 704 pp., shipped from the UK), is listed only in hardback, is not in the British Library catalogue, and its online transcripts are locked to buyers. The devotional, punning prose of the excerpt suits some readers and not others.

## For your own book
- **Verdict:** buy (or borrow, if you can find a copy) only if you will use the Treasury transcripts or the interviews. For the method itself, use *Yoga Nidra Made Easy* plus the free recordings listed below.
- **Take:**
  - **A rotation comparison table.** Use the Treasury as a pattern library: Satyananda, the Himalayan itinerary and its 61-point form, the marma rotation, iRest, the Kavacam nyāsa route, labyrinth, triangles, the Mahāvidyā wheel, the tetralemma. Present the rotations side by side and credit each school. Nothing else in your sources lets you do this so concretely.
  - **The nyāsa → rotation link via the Devī Kavacam (no. 1).** It is the most specific, checkable bridge between traditional *nyāsa* and the modern body rotation. Verify the verses and the dating in an Indological source (Mallinson & Singleton; Birch & Hargreaves) before you rely on it.
  - **Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra v. 75.** The verse on the moment between waking and sleep (excerpt p. 22) makes a strong primary-text epigraph. Use a scholarly translation, not the excerpt's verse rendering.
  - **Invitational language, with models.** No. 33 (Satyananda structure, mostly plain instructions with a few permission phrases) and no. 74 (described by its note as fully invitational; not yet heard) could serve as before-and-after examples for a chapter on voice (`08` §5).
