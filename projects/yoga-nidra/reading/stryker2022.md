# Enlightened Sleep: Restore Body, Mind, and Soul with the Power of Yoga Nidra — Rod Stryker (announced for 2022, then May 2023; no published edition found)

> **Verdict in three lines**
> 1. **Unpublished.** Announced by Sounds True (2022, then May 2023) but, on all the evidence I found, never released. Do not cite it or list it as a published book.
> 2. **Read instead:** *The Four Desires* (2011, the citable book on his sankalpa method); Kelly McGonigal's "How to Create a Sankalpa" (Yoga International, quotes Stryker at length); his free yoga nidra on rodstryker.com (email sign-up) or a Yoga International practice; Stanley, *Radiant Rest* (2021), whose author calls him one of her main teachers (see `stanley2021.md`); and the Stryker interview in Dinsmore-Tuli & Tuli, *Nidra Shakti* (2022), if your copy has it (see `dinsmoretuli2022.md`).

## In one paragraph
*Enlightened Sleep* was meant to be the book-length statement of **ParaYoga Nidra** by one of the first Western teachers to introduce and popularise yoga nidra (from about 1990, by his own account on his About page). The publisher's blurb promises three levels of benefit: rest, health and sleep; then changing subconscious patterns, false self-perceptions and even trauma; and finally spiritual insight and purpose. It opens with a sleep-and-power-nap hook, pitched as something that sounds too good to be true but isn't. The only sentence of the book in circulation is a blurb line saying yoga nidra is far more than deep relaxation. Announced for 2022 and then May 2023, the book apparently never appeared. Stryker's March 2026 essay describes a personal crisis lasting about five years and a rethinking of what meditation can and cannot do, which led to a new method, "Luminous Coherence". No source links this crisis to the book's delay, but the timing overlaps. For your purposes, the book is an **absence**. What you can study is the ParaYoga Nidra teaching documented in *The Four Desires*, in articles and in practice descriptions. That teaching is centred on **sankalpa as the bridge between deep rest and life purpose (dharma)**.

## Structure
Table of contents not available. None was published, and there is no Google Books preview or look-inside. As reported, the blurb describes an arc from relaxation and health, through reshaping the unconscious, to spiritual awakening, combining traditional teaching, recent research and many guided practices. The best clue to the planned architecture is the 2019 ParaYoga Nidra five-day training (12–14 nidra practices). Its listing says the method has **five aims** but describes only three levels: maximising health and well-being, reshaping the unconscious, and spiritual practice; the approach is adapted to whichever aim you pursue. It teaches the theory of **sankalpa** (how thought becomes form) in relation to **dharma**, and reads the practice through the **Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad** (waking, dreaming, sleeping and the "4th state") and the **koshas**. This is an inference about the book, not a report of it.

## Core ideas
These come from Stryker's documented teaching. The book may or may not have presented them this way.
- **Practice and state.** In his website essays (c. 2024), yoga nidra at its truest is a state of consciousness, not a technique: total relaxation together with heightened awareness, which he identifies with *turīya*, the fourth state. He criticises teachers who reduce it to a relaxation script or a sleep aid.
- **Peeling off identifications.** The way in is subtraction rather than elaborate visualisation. You release physical tension, stop identifying as "the breather", then let go of being "the doer", and rest as the awareness in which everything arises. He frames this in Advaita Vedānta terms and through the AUM teaching of the Māṇḍūkya.
- **The aim decides the method.** The 2019 training listing names five aims but describes only three levels: maximising health and well-being, reshaping the unconscious, and spiritual practice. The sequence is adapted to the aim.
- **Sankalpa is the centre.** A sankalpa is a vow that serves your dharma, your overall purpose. Stryker glosses *kalpa* as a vow or rule that overrides all other rules, and *san* as connection with the highest truth (as quoted by McGonigal). It differs from a New Year's resolution because it speaks to the whole arc of a life.
- **Plant it from wholeness, not lack.** Repeating "I want X" reinforces the belief that you lack X. So recall the sankalpa after the mind has settled into oneness (McGonigal). McGonigal herself names yoga nidra as one of the most powerful settings for this, and the *Four Desires* excerpt describes it as a very valuable tool to pair with a resolution. He cites the *Tripura Rahasya*: the quality of the mind that recites a resolve decides its effect (McGonigal).
- **Seed and soil.** In the *Four Desires* excerpt, accumulated stress compacts the "soil" of the unconscious, so even a perfect seed (resolve) will not grow. Deep relaxation loosens the soil and lines the unconscious up behind your conscious goals. He presents this as one reason most resolutions fail.
- **Being and becoming.** We are both *para ātman* (whole, needing nothing) and *jīva ātman* (born with a purpose and always becoming). Stryker says happiness requires walking both paths at once.
- **Physiological claims.** In the *Four Desires* excerpt, yoga nidra offers a systematic route straight into delta, where ordinary sleep gives only 20–30 minutes of it. A 2020 podcast host summarises him the same way: the body asleep, the mind in delta, with a thread of consciousness kept (see Strengths and limitations).
- **His later revision (2026).** Stryker writes that decades of practice and elevated meditative states did not free him from conditioning, rumination or depression. He concludes that emotions have to be met and integrated, not transcended, and that traditional yoga sidelines emotion and trauma. His new method combines neuroscience, CBT and tantric kriya. Any future *Enlightened Sleep* may differ a good deal from the 2021–23 plan.

- **Sankalpa method, as far as it is documented:**
  - **Finding it:** the detailed method is in *The Four Desires*. Judging by its part titles ("Live your mighty purpose", "An intention formed in the heart", "Overcoming resistance"), that book moves from purpose, to a sankalpa, to obstacles. ⚠️ An earlier draft of this summary said he teaches that the sankalpa is discovered rather than invented, because dharma is already inherent in the soul, citing a "2012 Himalayan Institute talk". I could not locate or open that talk, so treat that wording as unverified until you see it in *The Four Desires*.
  - **Wording it:** I found no documented rule from Stryker himself. The present-tense rule in McGonigal's article is Richard Miller's, not Stryker's.
  - **Planting it:** from a settled, whole state rather than from lack (McGonigal).
  - **Nourishing it:** the ParaYoga **"departure point"**. Give up one habitual, unhelpful behaviour for **40 days**. Each time the impulse comes, pause for a few breaths, settle into wholeness, then recall the sankalpa. This turns the craving into a cue (McGonigal).
- **Falling asleep:** not addressed in anything I could read. His framing (the body asleep while a thread of awareness remains) puts him on the "stay aware" side of debate 3, but there is no documented guidance for students who do fall asleep.

## What's distinctive
Based on the teaching, since the book is unavailable:
- **Sankalpa tied to dharma and the four aims of life (puruṣārthas).** Among the yoga nidra authors summarised in this project, I found none who ties the resolve so firmly to a life-purpose framework (a judgement from the project's reading notes, not from an outside source). Here yoga nidra is the engine of *The Four Desires*. Stanley was his student for over ten years (Stanley 2021, p. 19, per `stanley2021.md`), so her sankalpa teaching plausibly draws on his. But her "heart-level / discovered truth" framing is still to be checked against her pp. 6, 46 and 196 (`stanley2021.md`), so don't state the line of descent as fact.
- **Time-boxed, concrete resolves** (6–18 months), combined with a non-dual insistence that you plant them from completeness. Miller (iRest) separates the heartfelt desire from the intention (`miller2005.md`); Stryker holds them together as "being and becoming".
- **The off-the-mat "departure point" practice**, which links the sankalpa to habit change.
- **"The aim changes the method":** a teaching taxonomy (five named aims, three described levels) instead of one fixed sequence.
- **Two registers by one author.** One is the early, achievement-minded "Relax into Greatness" (resolutions, delta waves, success). The other is the later non-dual "beyond relaxation" essays. Then comes a public 2026 self-critique of transcendence-focused practice. That arc is itself a case study for your debates chapter.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:** (of the documented teaching, not the book)
  - One of the clearest accounts I found of *why* a sankalpa planted from lack backfires, with a practical remedy (pause, settle, then recall).
  - It integrates rest with purpose and daily choices.
  - It draws on deep tantric and Vedāntic sources (Māṇḍūkya, koshas, *Tripura Rahasya*, *Yoga Tāravalī*).
  - Over four decades of teaching (first class 1980 per the About page and Wikipedia's body text), widely used recordings, and a large trained-teacher network.
- **Limitations and critiques:** the research points below are cross-references to this project's own files; full citations are there, not in this file.
  - **No reviews exist**, because the book has no readers: 0 ratings on Amazon and Goodreads. Nothing can be cited from it.
  - **Delta claim:** the idea of systematic access to delta is not supported (see `06` §6.1, "EEG", for citations). The EEG literature mostly shows alpha/theta. A 2025 systematic review found only more theta in experienced practitioners (preprint; only 2 studies met all quality criteria). An Italian retreat study found no sleep spindles or K-complexes (n = 6). "Delta with awareness" rests on the single-subject Swami Rama anecdote (`06` §6.1, §6.3).
  - **Unsourced statistic:** the claim that about 80% of people fail to achieve their intentions is given without a source.
  - **History:** the excerpt says ancient texts described systematic relaxation and called it yoga nidra. That contradicts Birch & Hargreaves (2015), for whom medieval *yoganidrā* is a samādhi-like absorption, not body-scan relaxation (cited via `08` §1 and `satyananda1976.md`). His later "state" essays sit closer to the textual meaning.
  - **PTSD research:** the excerpt treats early PTSD studies at Walter Reed and the VA as validating tradition. These were most likely the early iRest pilots (inference; cf. the small, uncontrolled iRest veteran studies in `06` §6.2). The research cited in `06` is small and studies yoga nidra as a complement to treatment, not a replacement.
  - **Etymology:** *san* = connection with the highest truth is a devotional gloss. Monier-Williams gives *saṃkalpa* as a conception or intention formed in the mind or heart, will or purpose, and also a solemn vow before a ritual.
  - **Marketing register:** the blurb's sleep, power-nap and too-good-to-be-true pitch overpromises (`08` §11). Any implication that yoga nidra substitutes for sleep has no evidence (`06` §6.3, row 1, "1 hour of yoga nidra = 4 hours of sleep"). The sleep evidence in `06` §6.2 is thin: a small RCT (Datta et al. 2021, n = 41, vs. CBT-I) and an online study with very small to small effects against a waitlist (Moszeik et al. 2020).
  - **The author's own caveat:** by 2026 Stryker himself writes that deep meditative states did not heal his depression or conditioning. That undercuts the blurb's trauma promise more sharply than any outside reviewer could.
  - **Biography:** much of the biographical detail is self-published (his site; Wikipedia flags his article as autobiographical), and Wikipedia is internally inconsistent on when he began teaching (body text 1980, matching the About page; lead "since 1982"). The sources also differ on when he began yoga (at 19 per the About page; early twenties per the 2007 Yogi Times interview).

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The "lack" principle for sankalpa.** A resolve stated as a want can reinforce a sense of deficiency, so let the practitioner settle first and recall it from a felt sense of enough. It is psychologically sensible, it agrees with Miller, and it gives your sankalpa section a clear *why*. Credit Stryker through McGonigal's Yoga International article or *The Four Desires*.
  - **Two kinds of sankalpa, both optional.** Offer a "being" statement and, separately, a concrete, time-limited intention (his 6–18 months). That gives readers a choice instead of one model, and keeps you honest on debate 8.
  - **The departure-point idea, softened.** Use a recurring habit or impulse as a cue to pause and remember the intention. Drop or soften the 40-day abstinence framing if your book is rest-oriented.
  - **"The aim shapes the practice."** Organise your scripts by purpose (sleep, recovery, emotional work, insight). Don't present one master sequence as the method.
  - **Gratitude before the resolve.** A small, adoptable sequencing idea; test it in your own classes.
  - **His 2026 self-critique for your safety chapter.** A senior lineage-holder says publicly that elevated states did not resolve his trauma and depression, and that emotions have to be met rather than transcended. That supports modest claims and referral language (`07`).
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Don't cite *Enlightened Sleep*** as a source or list it as a published book. Cite *The Four Desires* (2011) and the articles. If you mention it, describe it as announced but unpublished.
  - **Drop the delta-wave language** and the 80%-of-resolutions-fail statistic.
  - **Don't repeat "ancient texts taught systematic relaxation called yoga nidra".** Use the "ancient roots, modern form" line from `08` §1.
  - **Use dictionary meanings for *saṃkalpa***, and label devotional etymologies as such.
  - **Treat "reshaping the unconscious" and "clearing stored stress" as metaphors** (`06` §6.3), and do not echo the blurb's claim about changing trauma.
  - **The brief's "sleeping/waking bodies" distinction:** don't use it until you have seen it in a Stryker source.
- **To do before citing his guiding voice:**
  - Listen to the free yoga nidra on rodstryker.com (email sign-up; length not stated on the pages I saw) or the 28-minute Yoga International "Yoga Nidra Practice" (members). Note: how he guides the gratitude-to-sankalpa transition, his pacing and silences, how he closes and brings people back, and whether he says anything to those who fall asleep. That will settle his place on debate 5 (directive vs. invitational) and fill the blank Return row.
  - Check *Nidra Shakti*: the publisher lists Stryker among the interviewees in "Worlds of Yoga Nidra" (`dinsmoretuli2022.md`), so check the page numbers in your copy. It may be the only Stryker yoga nidra text you can read in print.
- **For the compendium** (none of `01`, `02`, `06` or `08` currently mentions Stryker or ParaYoga; `10` and `99` list only his app and e-course):
  - `02`: list *Enlightened Sleep* as "announced 2022/2023, unpublished", with *The Four Desires* (2011) as the citable text.
  - `01`: add ParaYoga / Tantra Yoga Alchemy (Mani and Alan Finger, then Tigunait / Sri Vidya from 1999) as a US lineage and as a root of Stanley's approach (Stanley 2021, p. 19).
  - `08` debate 8: cite Stryker (via McGonigal / *The Four Desires*) for "plant from wholeness" and the dharma-linked sankalpa. Debate 3: place him on the "stay aware" side, with the caveat that his guidance on falling asleep is undocumented.
