## In one paragraph
*Mon yoga nidra* is a bright, illustrated self-help manual from a major French publisher. It is written for lay readers who want to unwind at home, not for teachers or scholars. It presents yoga nidra as an "ancestral" relaxation practice, glossed in French as "yoga du sommeil éveillé", in which you lie down and are guided into a reverie between waking and sleep. The practice is said to release tension on three levels: physical, emotional and mental. The book's selling point is a menu of fifteen ready-to-use themed sessions, grouped under three verbs: **soothe** (stress, anger, fears, sleep, a heavy burden), **activate** (letting go, self-confidence, acceptance, perspective, balance) and **change** (leaving a comfort zone, adapting, setting limits, starting to forgive, dropping the mask). Each session runs on the same 8-stage skeleton and comes with a mudra to do before or after. A final chapter teaches you to write your own sessions as "Nidra stories". The promise is large and stated without hedging: instant relief from tension, sleep-equivalent rest, emotional mastery, and freedom from limiting beliefs.

## Core ideas
- **What it is.** Yoga nidra is described as a "yoga of waking sleep": a state between waking and sleep, a kind of reverie, also called a "twilight state" (*état crépusculaire*). It releases tension on three levels at once: physical, emotional and mental.
- **Ancient and universal roots.** Every culture has explored the thresholds of sleep, the authors say: vision quests, lucid dreaming in ancient Mexico, African shamans, Aboriginal Australians, Tibet, Indian caves. The practice's own roots are placed in the Upanishads and the tantras, and it is called a sacred, millennia-old practice. Satyananda is credited, among others, with adapting it to modern life, drawing on tantra and on scientific research into stress. In the sampled pages, Western relaxation methods and Boyes (1973) are not mentioned.
- **Relaxation is not yoga nidra.** An epigraph from yoga teacher and author Christian Tikhomiroff says relaxation only *prepares* for yoga nidra, which is a journey into one's inner night.
- **Pair it with mudras.** The authors add mudras, as they do in their own online and in-person sessions. Mudras are done before or after a session, never during it, and are said to direct energy and induce specific emotional and mental states.
- **"Ten good reasons" (pp. 13–14).** The list escalates fast:
  - tension falls "instantly";
  - a session is "deeper than sleep" and equals 3–4 hours of it, though it does not replace sleep;
  - deep introspection into the ego;
  - control of emotions;
  - better memory and concentration;
  - freedom from limiting beliefs, "reaching your goals" and full self-realisation.

  The list closes by admitting that change needs regular practice and a will to change.
- **"Six explanations" of why it works (pp. 15–20):**
  1. Yoga nidra is said to be the *origin* of meditation, self-hypnosis, EMDR and sophrology, so their benefits are all felt in a session.
  2. It acts on "invisible levels": the senses, intellect, ego and subconscious go quiet, and it works on the five koshas (rendered as physical, energetic, emotional, mental and spiritual bodies) and the chakras.
  3. It acts on brainwaves. Four pages describe gamma, beta, alpha, theta and delta, and yoga nidra is said to favour alpha.
  4. It slows and deepens the breath.
  5. It works on emotions, thoughts and beliefs. Images, scenarios and stories keep the mind on the guidance and bring stuck emotions and false beliefs up and out.
  6. It moves you through levels of consciousness, up to becoming "the witness of oneself".
- **A session has a goal.** Each one is built around an objective, such as calming stress or adapting to a new situation (p. 21).
- **Stay awake.** The "only constraint" is to stay awake and alert and not fall asleep, however tempting (p. 21).
- **Write your own.** Readers are guided step by step to write their own sessions. The pocket edition calls these "Nidra stories".

## What's distinctive
- **The only book in the set that pairs every session with a mudra.** This is the authors' signature (their *Mudra* book and the 2023 card set), and the gesture is a ritual bookend outside the nidra itself.
- **An emotional-themes menu organised by verbs:** soothe, activate, change. Lusk and Verma offer themed script collections in English, but none uses this three-way split. It works like a pharmacy shelf: pick the session for the problem.
- **A two-tier model:** a 12-stage "long version", for orientation, next to the 8-stage form used for practice. This is a neat way to show readers that the short form is a condensation.
- **A hybrid print/audio set:** 13 printed scripts and 2 sessions available only as audio.
- **"Write your own" for lay readers, built around stories.** Bonnasse (2015) also teaches you to build sessions, but from contemplative micro-practices from the *Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra*. Here the unit appears to be a narrative.
- **The French market's mainstream register.** Boyes (1973) was the classroom manual and Bonnasse (2015) the contemplative one. This is the glossy self-help one, from Flammarion, now in a mass-market pocket edition. It also carries a Québec Raja-yoga voice into the French market.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Very easy to use. Each session is self-contained, with the same three-part layout every time, and you can find a session by its problem.
  - The 12-stage list, the 8-stage practice form and the per-session mudra give a clear, repeatable architecture.
  - A real closing chapter (at most about 35 pp.) on writing your own sessions.
  - Warm, practical tone. The publisher page quotes *Les Échos*: « Pas de prêchi-prêcha mais du concret, du pratique. » ("No preachiness, just concrete, practical material.") I did not open the article itself.
  - Attractive layout and illustrations, a cheap pocket edition, and a co-author who has taught Raja yoga since 1976.
  - Reception is positive but thin. Amazon.fr: 4.3/5 from 13 ratings (72% five-star, 9% four-star, 19% two-star). The visible reviews call it practical and complete, mention the audio, and say it is good for leading guided relaxation. The one visible two-star review ("Pas accroché", i.e. it didn't click) says only that the reviewer was disappointed after a training with "yogabathi"; it gives no specific reason, so it says nothing definite about depth or lineage fidelity. Goodreads: one rating.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **No independent review found.** A web search and the earlier catalogue searches found no review in the yoga press, no academic notice and no author interview about the book; the publisher quotes only two magazine lines. The EUY post is an announcement.
  - **Promise level is high.** Instant relief, sleep-equivalent rest, emotional mastery, "reaching your goals" and self-realisation put it at the "big claims" end of `08` debate 11 ("What should you promise?").
  - **Unsupported and wrong claims, kept in the 2026 reissue:**
    - "3–4 hours of sleep" per session. There is no evidence for this (`06` §6.3).
    - Tension falls "instantly". Pooled effects are moderate and shrink against active controls (`06` §6.0).
    - Yoga nidra is the "origin" of meditation, self-hypnosis, EMDR and sophrology. Meditation is older. The hypnosis and autogenic influences ran *into* modern yoga nidra (`08` debate 10). EMDR has its own unrelated origin. The sophrology link is unverified (`11` §11.2).
    - Delta waves as "healing energy" located "in the unconscious". That is pop neuroscience, not EEG findings; the compendium rates "reaches delta while aware" as resting only on anecdote and claims (`06` §6.3). The safe part, more alpha and theta, matches `06` §6.1.
    - Mudras directing energy and inducing precise emotional states. The compendium has no evidence entry on mudras, so this claim is **unassessed** here, not refuted.
  - **Historical framing is one-sided:** "millennia-old" and Upanishadic, with Satyananda among the modern adapters. There is nothing on the Western relaxation ancestry, on Boyes, or on the scholarly view of the word's history (debate 1).
  - **No sources.** The contents list no bibliography or notes.
  - **Goal-driven and self-improvement framed:** goals, limiting beliefs, "reaching your objectives". It sits at the "necessary/goal-oriented" end of debate 8, with no visible discussion of when *not* to aim at change.
  - **Safety not visible.** Nothing in the sample about trauma, psychiatric conditions or when to stop. Check the full book before assuming it is absent.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The three-part session card:** what this theme means → a short preparatory gesture → the script on a fixed skeleton. Readers can find and run a session in seconds. If you write a script section, this card format beats long continuous scripts.
  - **The "long version vs. practice version" device.** Show the full classical sequence once, then teach a condensed form and say explicitly what you dropped and why. It answers the "why does your structure differ from Satyananda's?" question before readers ask it.
  - **A verb-based grouping of themes** (soothe / activate / change) as a way to organise a script library. It is clearer than grouping by body system or by lineage.
  - **A closing chapter that teaches readers to write their own practices.** It suits a teacher's book well; here it fills the last ~35 of 224 pages (about a sixth) of a mainstream trade title.
  - **Consider offering a few practices as audio only.** Check in the book why Dumas & Sansregret chose these two and how readers reach them (QR code, download, app).
  - **If you write in French,** the vocabulary here is the mainstream one: "yoga du sommeil éveillé" (Boyes's 1973 title phrase, still current), "état crépusculaire", and "affirmation positive" for sankalpa. Know it even if you choose other terms.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Do not repeat** the "3–4 hours of sleep" equivalence, the "instant" effects, or the claim that yoga nidra is the origin of meditation, hypnosis, EMDR and sophrology. Each is either untested or wrong (`06` §6.3, `08` debates 10–11). If you mention them, present them as popular claims and correct them.
  - **Replace the brainwave pages** with two sourced sentences: alpha/theta shifts and "local sleep", with the caveats in `06` §6.1. Four pages of frequency bands imply a precision the evidence does not have.
  - **Rework the history.** Use "ancient roots, modern form" (Birch & Hargreaves; Singleton 2005; Boyes 1973) rather than "millennia-old sacred practice".
  - **Soften the stay-awake rule** if your readers include tired beginners or you offer a sleep practice. A book that says staying awake is the only rule and also sells a sleep session leaves readers unsure (debate 3).
  - **Watch the self-improvement pull.** Goal-led themes are marketable, but if your book leans toward rest rather than fixing, say so (debate 8). If you use themes, frame them as invitations, not prescriptions.
  - **Keep mudra claims modest** if you borrow the mudra pairing. "A gesture to mark the beginning and end" is defensible; "directs energy and induces precise emotional states" is a claim the compendium has not assessed, so source it before you make it.
  - **On tone:** the brief asked for a comparison with Dinsmore-Tuli 2022 as a model of accessible writing. They are accessible in different ways. Dumas & Sansregret are accessible through **simplicity and packaging**: short pages, lists, quotes, pictures, ready-made answers, but at the cost of accuracy and nuance. Dinsmore-Tuli is accessible through **permission and honesty**: choice of posture, sleep allowed, intent optional, critical history, but at great length. Her short *Yoga Nidra Made Easy* (2020) is the closer like-for-like model. Take the layout discipline from this book and the voice and candour from Dinsmore-Tuli.
