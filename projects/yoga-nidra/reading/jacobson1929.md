## In one paragraph
Jacobson was a Chicago physician and physiologist who trained at Harvard. In this book he reports about twenty years of work (Wikipedia). His claim is that tension in the skeletal muscles is part of nervousness, anxiety and many "functional" illnesses, and that a patient can be *taught*, as a skill, to remove even residual tension. Removing it calms emotion and mental activity too. Nathoo describes it as a technical book for doctors and scientists, followed in 1934 by the popular *You Must Relax*. For a yoga nidra writer it matters for three reasons:
1. It is the first book-length, scientifically argued account of a systematic, part-by-part relaxation method. Jacobson had begun using relaxation with patients in 1915 and had already outlined the technique in 1924 ("The Technic of Progressive Relaxation", per Gessel).
2. Historians of modern yoga name Jacobson as a source of yoga's "relaxationism" (Singleton 2005, as summarised by Nathoo 2016).
3. Popular PMR, which some yoga nidra books now borrow, descends from it in a simplified form.

**How much is about yoga nidra:** none of it directly. The book predates Satyananda's yoga nidra by decades. Its whole relevance is as an ancestor and as a comparison.

## Core ideas
- **Relaxation is the absence of contraction.** It is not rest and not sleep. Lying down or sleeping can leave muscles tense, and Jacobson used this to explain why the "rest cure" failed. Trained relaxation was meant to be better than rest or even sleep (Nathoo, citing *You Must Relax* p. 44). Jacobson told patients to get up an hour earlier rather than skip practice (BBC).
- **You cannot be tense where you are fully relaxed.** He built emotional calm on this (see the quote below).
- **Mind = minute muscle activity.** Imagining or recalling an action produces tiny contractions in the muscles concerned (Am. J. Physiology series, 1930–31, per Gessel and Lehrer). Visual thinking involves the eye muscles and verbal thinking the speech muscles. Relaxing those muscles reduces "all kinds of mental activity" (Lehrer, citing 1938 ch. 10). Trained subjects reported a fading or disappearance of conscious content: they "could not simultaneously relax and reflect" (Gessel, on 1927 observations).
- **Deeper than light sleep, in one sense.** In deeply relaxed trained subjects the knee-jerk reflex shrank or vanished. Jacobson concluded that waking muscle tone could fall below that of light sleep (Gessel).
- **A skill, taught slowly.** Patients had two or more hours of instruction a week and practised alone for one or two hours a day (*You Must Relax* p. 126, via Nathoo). Lehrer says Jacobson's early descriptions involved "many sessions over many months", but that he often trained patients briefly (1938 ch. 18).
- **No suggestion.** Jacobson avoided hypnotic-style suggestion. His reason was that *thinking* a muscle is relaxed is not the same as its *being* relaxed (Lehrer). He set his method apart from hypnosis, mysticism, Schultz's autogenic training and Annie Payson Call (Nathoo).
- **Measured, not felt.** Tension was checked first by palpation, later with surface EMG built with Bell Labs engineers (Lehrer; Gessel). Even so, he did not want patients relying on a machine (Lehrer).

## The practice as this book teaches it
Reconstructed from Gessel, Lehrer and Nathoo. Check against the book.
- **Position:** lying down at first. After the whole body has been covered lying down, the whole process is repeated sitting up (Gessel). "Differential relaxation" (staying relaxed while reading or talking, using only the muscles the task needs) is part of the method (Lehrer, 1938 ch. 6); that it comes after the sitting stage is my inference.
- **First lesson:** bend the hand back at the wrist and *study the sensation* in the forearm. This is observation, not relaxation. Then stop the action and notice what changes. Do this three times in all, several minutes apart. Spend the rest of the hour lying still, attention lightly on the muscle studied that day (Gessel).
- **Sequence:** one region at a time over successive sessions, ordered by anatomy: limbs, then trunk, then neck, then head (Gessel). Every third session is a "zero period" of relaxation only, with no contraction (Gessel). Special care goes to the muscles around the eyes and mouth and the speech muscles, especially for obsessive thinking and insomnia (Lehrer). I could not verify the finer order: right before left, or which limb first.
- **Course length:** none of my sources gives a number of sessions or months for the full lying–sitting course. Lehrer says only that early descriptions involved "many sessions over many months". For contrast: a single yoga nidra session usually runs well under an hour; Wolpe's shortened PMR took about 20 minutes (Wikipedia).
- **The tense–release principle, correctly stated:**
  - The contraction is a **teaching device**. Its job is to show what tension feels like. Releasing it is not supposed to produce relaxation.
  - With the "method of diminishing tensions", each contraction is made smaller, until the patient can feel tension when merely *thinking* of tensing (Lehrer, citing 1938 pp. 185–186), and then the faint sensations of resting muscle tone (Lehrer, citing 1938 ch. 13).
  - Then comes the instruction to "go negative": simply stop, down to zero muscle tension (Lehrer, who gives no page citation for this step; the phrase may come from his own training with Jacobson).
  - Squeezing hard and letting go for a "rebound" came later: Lehrer attributes the rebound rationale to Bernstein & Borkovec (1973). He argues that large contractions leave *more* residual tension afterwards and blunt sensitivity to small tensions.
- **Language:** by implication from Lehrer and Gessel, plain instruction plus the patient's own observation, with no imagery, no resolve and no soothing patter (Gessel dismisses "tapes that play soothing noises or tell him that he is getting relaxed"). Unverified in the book itself.
- **How a stage might sound (reconstructed by me from Gessel and Lehrer, not Jacobson's words):**
  > Bend your hand back at the wrist. Notice the faint sensation high in the forearm, on the back. That is tension. Now simply stop. Go negative: don't do anything to relax, just stop doing. Stay with the forearm for a few minutes.

  Compare a Satyananda rotation cue (paraphrased, see `satyananda1976`): "Right-hand thumb, second finger, third finger… palm, back of the hand, wrist…", each point named briskly, with no movement and no study of sensation.

## What's distinctive
**Against yoga nidra's rotation of consciousness** (Satyananda: about 76 named points, brisk, starting at the right thumb, no movement; see `satyananda1976`):

| | Jacobson | Satyananda's yoga nidra |
|---|---|---|
| Shared ground | Lying supine, moving attention part by part, feeling sensation in each part | The same |
| Shared belief | Bodily release calms emotion and mind | The same. Satyananda's 1978 compilation even speaks of muscular, emotional and mental "tensions" (see `satyananda1976`). |
| What attention does | Tests and releases muscle activity in one region | Touches a point and moves on |
| Pace | One region per hour-long session; the full course took many sessions (Lehrer: early descriptions spoke of many months) | The whole body in minutes |
| Muscular action | Small, deliberate contractions as a teaching aid | None |
| What the mind should do | Fall quiet. Imagery is itself treated as eye-muscle activity to be relaxed away. | Stay aware and cultivate imagery (the visualisation stage) |
| Suggestion | Excluded on principle | The sankalpa is suggestion. Birch & Hargreaves read it as autosuggestion inspired by Western relaxation therapy. |

So Jacobson is a plausible ancestor of the *body-sequence and tension* layer of yoga nidra. He is **not** an ancestor of the sankalpa, which Birch & Hargreaves (following Singleton 2005) see as inspired by Western relaxation therapies and autosuggestion, and not of the visualisation. Naming Coué or Schultz as the specific source would be my own inference; see `coue1922` and `schultz1932`.

**Contemporary and later readings in this project:**
- **Schultz (1932)** gives Jacobson's progressive relaxation a chapter of its own (Ch. XII, among the "related methods", pp. 233–273; see `schultz1932`), a contemporary physician's view. I have not seen its content.
- **Boyes (1973, pp. 3–7; see `boyes1973`)** groups Jacobson with Schultz as Western methods that mostly use suggestion, imagination and thought. Given Jacobson's explicit anti-suggestion stance, that is a mischaracterisation, at least of Jacobson's own method.
- **Sophrology** (Caycedo, from 1960) built partly on Jacobson's relaxation, alongside hypnosis and autogenic training (see `caycedo1998`): a descendant that sits closer to yoga nidra.

**Checking the compendium's attributions:**
- **Birch & Hargreaves (2015)** never name Jacobson, autogenic training or muscle tensing. They link a medieval practice to "body-scanning techniques of modern relaxation therapy": the *Yogayājñavalkya*'s eighteen-*marma* pratyāhāra. Their Western link is for the *sankalpa*.
- **Singleton (2005)** cites Jacobson 1929 and 1934 (Crossref reference list). Nathoo summarises his thesis: modern yoga's relaxation owes more to Call and Jacobson than to any ancient tradition.
- **Dinsmore-Tuli (2020)** lists Jacobson (1934) in her Western lineage (see `dinsmoretuli2020`).

`01` §1.2 and `08` debate 1 should cite Birch & Hargreaves only for the general overlap with relaxation therapy and for sankalpa as autosuggestion, not for Jacobson specifically.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - A precise, teachable idea of what "relaxed" means, with an objective test.
  - Training of interoception (sensing the inside of the body) at very fine grain.
  - Differential relaxation for daily life, the ancestor of "applied relaxation".
  - Lehrer (2025) cites reviews finding PMR reduces anxiety, depression and fatigue (cf. `06` §6.4: general relaxation mechanisms; the effect is shared, not specific to Jacobson).
  - Firmly secular and anti-suggestion. That is useful to readers wary of hypnosis (`08` debate 10).
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **The mind theory took a hit.** In 1966 Lee Birk, paralysed with curare, still felt terrified and could still control his heart rate and sweating by mental effort. Muscle tension is therefore not necessary for anxiety (BBC). Lehrer still defends the clinical value.
  - **A heavy dose.** The programme is long, clinician-led and expensive in time.
  - **Its claims are of its time.** It treats a wide range of "nervous" and gut disorders as tension disorders, and Jacobson wanted "neuro-muscular hypertension" to replace "neurasthenia" (Nathoo, citing Lanier's 1930 review).
  - **Lost in simplification.** Most "Jacobson" PMR today, in yoga nidra books too, is the shortened tense-hard-and-release version. Lehrer argues it may work *less* well, and few therapists teach the original thoroughly (BBC).
  - **Reception.** Reviewed in *NEJM* and *JAMA* (1929) and several journals in 1930 and 1938–39; I could read none of them.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **Name the ancestor accurately in your history chapter** (`01` §1.2): "Jacobson's progressive relaxation (outlined 1924, book-length 1929) systematised lying-down, part-by-part relaxation and the idea that releasing the body quiets the mind." Pair it with Call (1891) and Schultz (1932). Credit Singleton for the argument. The safe formula is "resembles / shares a lineage with", not "comes from".
  - **The "study, then go negative" principle** as an optional beginner's door into body awareness. Use a small contraction only to locate a sensation, then release with no effort, with the contraction smaller each time. It fits iRest-style "sensing" better than the loud squeeze-and-drop version (see `lusk2015`, `miller2015a`).
  - **The distinction between rest and relaxation.** Lying down is not automatically release, which is a strong reason for guided practice, and relevant to `08` debate 3 (Is falling asleep failure?). Present it as Jacobson's clinical claim, not as proof that yoga nidra beats sleep.
  - **Relaxing the eyes, jaw and tongue to quiet inner speech and imagery.** This is a concrete cue that works in any script, with a historical pedigree.
  - **The anti-suggestion stance** as a foil when you write about sankalpa (`08` debates 8 and 10). Jacobson shows that a relaxation method can deliberately leave out suggestion, so including a resolve is a choice.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **"All thought is muscle"** as fact. Birk's curare experiment undercut it, and modern neuroscience does not support it.
  - **"Better than sleep" and the knee-jerk "below light sleep" finding** as support for "1 hour of yoga nidra = 4 hours of sleep" (`06` §6.3). It is a different method and a different measure.
  - **Calling squeeze-and-release "Jacobson's method".** That belongs to the later abbreviated versions: Wolpe shortened it to about 20 minutes (Wikipedia); the tense-hard "rebound" rationale is Bernstein & Borkovec 1973 (Lehrer).
  - **Citing the attribution that Birch & Hargreaves compare yoga nidra with Jacobson.** They do not.
  - **The 1929 vs 1938 edition.** If you quote method details, cite the 1938 2nd edition, which modern authors use. Cite 1924/1929 for priority.

## Short quotes (optional, max 2, each under 25 words, with page if known)
