## In one paragraph
*Radiant Rest* is a practice handbook and a spiritual manifesto for rest. Stanley is a former Hollywood film producer who has taught yoga nidra since the early 2000s. She presents yoga nidra in three ways at once: as a technique, a state of consciousness and a goddess (Introduction, p. 3; see the quotes below). The techniques only prepare you for the state, which comes through grace. The book is written for beginners, for busy "householders" and for teachers (who are told to practise one script consistently for 40 days before teaching it). It moves from history and the subtle-body map (Part One), through the obstacles to rest (trauma, race-based stress, ancestral exhaustion, feeling undeserving) and how to set up a nest and an altar (Part Two), to six practices ordered from gross to subtle (Part Three). Each practice is matched to an element and a kośa and surrounded by rituals for falling asleep and waking. The promise is deep rest as a birthright, and beyond that, waking up to one's own radiance.

What the chapters contain, from the Introduction's own outline (pp. 5–9) and the snippets:
- **Ch. 1** is a short history. The medieval texts that name yoganidrā (the *Devī Māhātmya*, *Haṭhapradīpikā*, *Yoga Tārāvalī* and *Maṇḍala Brāhmaṇa Upaniṣad*) give no complete instructions for entering the state (p. 17). The modern teachers covered are Satyananda, Richard Miller, Rod Stryker (taught by Mani Finger; his own style is "Enlightened Sleep"), Amrit and Kamini Desai, and Dinsmore-Tuli and Tuli (pp. 18–19). Swami Rama and his teacher "Mataji" follow (pp. 20–21). Then comes the goddess Yoganidrā: the *Devī Sūktam*, and a version of the *Devī Māhātmya* story in which Brahmā praises her until she appears (she is the one who holds Viṣṇu in his meditative sleep), and the suggestion that you frame a practice as a prayer to the Divine Mother (pp. 21–23). This section also cites Birch and Hargreaves' essay on the history of *yoganidrā* (p. 23).
- **Ch. 2** sets out the states of the *Māṇḍūkya*. It lays brain-wave stages and sleep stages over them, describes the Menninger experiments with Swami Rama, the koshas, and "gateways" to turīya, and ends with the stage list (pp. 45–47).
- **Ch. 3** covers the obstacles to relaxing: trauma, race-based stress (drawing on Gail Parker), ancestral narratives, and the sense of not deserving rest. Its exercises are Body Awareness, Pratyāhāra and Energetic Protection.
- **Ch. 4** is the Householder's Flow: a 24-hour cycle of 2–3 minute "mini practice portals".
- **Ch. 5** covers the nest, props, the altar, and falling asleep.
- **Chs. 6–11** are the six practices.
- **The Conclusion** teaches you how to guide yourself without a recording.

## Core ideas
- **Beyond technique.** Yoga nidra is a technique, a state and a goddess. The state cannot be forced or demanded; arriving there takes preparation, clarity and grace (p. 3). The techniques prepare the ground. Stanley says she teaches the *methods that lead to* the state, not the state itself.
- **Laya yoga: dissolving from gross to subtle.** The practice moves awareness from the physical body inward through the five kośas. The six practices are ordered to match: earth and the physical body first, then prana, mind, wisdom and bliss, and last the "void" beyond the elements.
- **The Māṇḍūkya map.** Waking, dreaming, deep sleep and turīya, each linked to the syllables of AUM and to a centre in the body (third eye for waking, throat for dreaming, heart for deep sleep). The **junctions between states** are the gateway to turīya. Here she draws on Swami Lakshmanjoo's Kashmir Shaivist idea of a gap or junction between waking and dreaming, and between dreaming and deep sleep (p. 41). Hence her stress on the moments of falling asleep and waking.
- **Transitions as portals.** Bedtime and wake-up rituals use the hypnagogic and hypnopompic states. Every pause in the day (between breaths, at sunrise or sunset) is one of her *little nidra moments*.
- **Rest as birthright and reclamation.** Stanley writes that she never saw anyone in her family rest and had to learn how (p. 58). Self-inquiry prompts ask whether you feel you deserve rest (pp. 61, 71). Ancestors can be invited to rest with you and asked for protection. Race-based stress is not the same as PTSD (via Gail Parker, p. 55). The framing is close to "rest as resistance" without being polemical; Tricia Hersey is not cited.
- **Devotion over discipline.** Practice is reframed as a 24-hour flow for householders. She openly criticizes the way yoga is sold as a wellness product instead of a lifelong path (Yoga Journal excerpt).
- **Nature as teacher.** "Nature-amplification" practices take the visualizations (moon, stars, earth, water) outdoors to the real thing. She calls this "rewilding".
- **Self-inquiry and freewriting** after every practice. Memory (*smṛti*) of what arose is treated as part of the practice.
- **Sankalpa is used lightly.** It is often offered but optional, and it is let go as practice deepens into non-doing (p. 46; see quote 2). The glossary defines it as awareness of, and commitment to, a heartfelt resolve that leads to one's highest truth.
- **Lineage, told honestly but devotionally.** She honours the Himalayan lineage and the Divine Mother. She also notes that Swami Rama's life included documented allegations of abuse (p. 20) and points readers to Dinsmore-Tuli's *Yoni Shakti* under the heading "History of Harm in Yoga Nidra" (Recommended Resources, p. 213).

## The practice as this book teaches it

**A. Her generic sequence (ch. 2, pp. 45–47).** She stresses that this is the common order, not a rule. Teachers should respond to whatever the student needs at the time rather than follow it mechanically (p. 45). The steps are mapped onto Patañjali's limbs:
1. **Supported posture.** Śavāsana, side-lying, restorative shapes or any fully supported position, with plenty of props. This is *āsana* as a steady, effortless "seat".
2. **Diaphragmatic breathing.** Watch the navel rise and fall until the breath is effortless, smooth, even and continuous (*prāṇāyāma*).
3. **Pratyāhāra.** Withdraw from sound, sight and the other senses, like the tortoise drawing in its limbs. She calls this the most important step, the start of a gradual dissolution (p. 46).
4. **Sankalpa.** A resolve, intention or affirmation. It is optional, and released in deeper states.
5. **Systematic relaxation of the body**, part by part. Then awareness of breath: feel that the body is breathing you, and give in to the involuntary breath. (The snippet doesn't show whether breath awareness is a heading of its own.)
6. **Visualization.** An image, points of light or a mantra (*dhāraṇā*).
7. **Resting awareness.** Settle at the heart, throat, third eye, or lower at the womb (*dhyāna*).
8. **Surrender.** Dissolve into silence and spaciousness, possibly toward samādhi or turīya, which comes, if it comes, through grace.
9. **Welcoming ourselves back.** Space, time, breath, body.
10. **Sankalpa smṛti.** Remember the sankalpa in the transition back to waking.
11. **Freewriting or journaling**, usually 3–15 minutes, straight after practice.

Compared with Satyananda, there is **no rotation of consciousness** in this generic list, **no pairs of opposites**, and **no rapid images**. Pratyāhāra is a named stage before body relaxation, and the end is journaling. She credits rotation and opposites to the "Bihar Tradition" in the appendix (p. 180).

**B. How each practice chapter is built.** The template is very clear:
- an invitation naming the element and kośa;
- **Benefits**, **Practice this** (when to use it: anxiety, before an exam, decisions, low energy) and, at least in Practice Two, **Teach this** (notes for teachers);
- optional **Preparing for Deep Relaxation** (poses, prāṇāyāma, bīja mantras such as *lam* and *gam*, or *aum hrim*);
- a **ready-written sankalpa** for that practice;
- the timed script, with minutes for each segment in brackets;
- **Self-Inquiry** questions (10–15 minutes of fast writing, straight after);
- a **Nature-Amplification** practice;
- a **Bedtime Ritual** and a **Wake-Up Ritual** of 2–15 minutes.

**D. Other printed practices with no audio.**
- **Pranava Practice** (5–30 min, ch. 2): chant A–U–M, moving down third eye → throat → heart, then upward, each syllable linked to a state.
- **Mental alarm clock** (ch. 2): picture an analogue clock set to your waking time.
- **Body Awareness Exercise** (5 min).
- **Pratyāhāra Practice** (10–15 min, may be done seated with eyes open).
- **Energetic Protection** (three circles).
- **Ancestor-protection practice.**
- **Sleep Flow** (5 min, credited to Swami Rama's teaching): crocodile pose, 4-count inhale and 8-count exhale, belly awareness.
- **Sustained-awareness night practices:** the 108 countdown to sleep (a note credits Bonnasse's *Yoga Nidra Meditation*), and the "Dream Stone", a crystal held through the night. She advises limiting these to two or three nights in a row.
- **Suṣumnā meditation** (10 min).

**E. Falling asleep (ch. 5, pp. 95–99).**
- Yoga nidra and sleep are kept apart. Approach yoga nidra as a transformative practice that also rests you. If the aim is to fall asleep, call it a separate **"Sleep Flow"**.
- Don't build the nest on your bed.
- Practise yoga nidra before noon where possible, so it isn't too close to bedtime.
- She calls yoga nidra "essential" for staying rested with insomnia.
- In groups, people may fall asleep, cough or snore; set props so the head sits above the heart.
- For persistent sleep problems she suggests a sleep study and an Ayurvedic herbal tonic (p. 98).
- Her ideal is to stay aware at the edges of sleep. Even so, falling asleep is treated as normal, not as failure. I saw no scolding about it in any passage.

**F. Trauma and accessibility.**
- She lists choices for trauma survivors (p. 54): eyes can stay open, you can leave the room, you can move.
- An **"Eyes Open" nest** for PTSD: seated against a wall, facing the door (p. 89).
- Adaptations for wheelchair users, and for people in prison with no props: a rolled T-shirt or socks (p. 92).
- Pregnancy (after the second trimester) and back problems: lie on the left side with a bolster between the knees (p. 88). The appendix says crocodile pose (Makarasana) is not for pregnancy or back pain (p. 183). Note that several bedtime rituals and Practice Six start in crocodile pose.
- She sends people with PTSD to qualified teachers (p. 53).

**F2. A recurring device.** Several scripts (Practices 1, 2, 5 and 6) and the Energetic Protection exercise open the same way. After settling, you draw a circle of protection around the body (light, fire or anything you choose). Then you let the sounds of the room sit *outside* the circle while you stay inside it. It works as a pratyāhāra technique and a safety cue at once.

**G. Voice.** Invitational and devotional rather than clinical: "Feel ...", "Notice ...", "Imagine ...", with exact timings in brackets. A typical guided moment, paraphrased: you are led along the breath, deeper each time, toward a door at the back of the heart's cave. You push it open and sit before a flame, then ask it a question and wait for what comes. Dense, image-rich and full of mantras compared with Satyananda's terse commands or iRest's neutral "welcoming". There is very little "I am practising yoga nidra"-style formula.

**H. Teaching guidance.** Teachers should do each practice for at least 40 days, share it first with a few people and get feedback (Introduction, p. 9). The Conclusion argues that a playing recording may at some point *limit* how far one can go, because turīya is beyond thought (p. 172). She gives steps for self-guiding: two 40-day sādhanas of one practice, memorizing the script, the mental alarm clock, and 30 minutes of silence.

## What's distinctive
- **Built around transitions.** Of the books in this compendium, it is the only one I know of that pairs every practice with **bedtime and wake-up rituals**, treating the hypnagogic and hypnopompic thresholds as the main training ground. By contrast, Satyananda asks you to stay awake during the session, and Miller holds that awareness is present even if you drop off (compendium file 08, §3).
- **Rituals around the practice:** altar-building, the ancestor circle, the protective circle, nature-amplification outdoors, and the "Householder's Flow" of micro-practices. Brody has a 40-day programme and Dinsmore-Tuli has the nest and the goddess, but neither combines Sri Vidya mantra (*srim*, *hrim*) and Himalayan techniques (śavayātrā, the crocodile-pose 1:2 breath) with this ritual layer.
- **Element + kośa + chakra + state of consciousness** built into each practice, in a clean six-step progression from earth to the void.
- **A Black woman teacher's perspective** on who gets to rest: race-based stress, ancestral exhaustion, prisons and accessibility. (Her press list includes Black + Well Voices and Black Girl in Om.) This is mainstream-friendly but not secular.
- **Lineage handled with candour.** For a lineage-honouring book, it is unusually open about Swami Rama's documented allegations, and its resource list points to Dinsmore-Tuli's *Yoni Shakti* for the history of harm in yoga nidra.
- **A self-guiding chapter** that argues for eventually going *beyond* recordings, an idea rarely found in audio-led books.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - A coherent, reusable chapter template.
  - Realistic short formats for busy people.
  - Concrete trauma and accessibility options.
  - Honest that medieval texts give no method (p. 17), and that yoga nidra-as-technique is modern and plural (appendix of six schools).
  - Credits its sources: Dinsmore-Tuli for the nest, Rajmani Tigunait for mantra placement, Swami Rama for the Sleep Flow, Bonnasse for the 108 countdown. It also cites Birch and Hargreaves' historical essay, which is rare in a practitioner book.
  - Beautiful design: a PubWest book design gold award, 2022, according to the author's press page.
  - Free audio for all six main practices.
  - It seems to avoid the stock claim that one hour of yoga nidra equals four hours of sleep. A Google Books search for "four hours" turned up only the "twenty-four-hour" practice cycle.
  - Strong reader reception: Goodreads 4.52 from 442 ratings and 61 reviews (October 2026). Readers single out its accessibility, its trauma-informed inclusivity (including prison and pregnancy adaptations) and its use as a reference to return to.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **Little formal criticism exists.** I found no review in a journal, *Yoga Journal* or *Yoga International*. The press archive lists features, gift-guide mentions, licensed excerpts and dozens of podcasts, so reception is mostly promotional or by readers.
  - Goodreads readers note three problems. It is hard to follow written practices without audio. A digital copy is a poor fit for a rest practice. And the book can leave you feeling you "must" practise daily or fail.
  - **Science chapter (my assessment against compendium file 06):** it lays brain waves, sleep stages and the four states on top of one another. It says that at the transition into the state of yoga nidra the student drops into delta-wave non-REM sleep yet stays aware of their surroundings (p. 34). Support comes from the Swami Rama/Menninger demonstrations and a personal communication from Dean Radin about Swami Veda Bharati (p. 27). These are anecdotes. The EEG literature mostly shows alpha and theta, with more theta in experienced practitioners [@kachera2025].
  - **Epigenetics:** a 2018 study of the sons of Civil War prisoners of war (an early-death result) is used to argue that many of us carry inherited, DNA-level stories that relaxing is unsafe (p. 60). That is a stretch.
  - **Etymology:** *nidrā* is glossed as *ni* "void" + *dru* "to draw forth" (p. 2, glossary). That is not standard Sanskrit (*ni* + √*drā*, "to sleep").
  - **Ritual details** (which foot first into bed, mirror gazing, crystals, protective circles, invoking ancestors) will read as esoteric to secular or clinical readers.
  - **Mantras and bīja placements** come with a "consult a teacher" caveat that a book can't fully meet.
  - **The practices are long** (up to 50 minutes) and image-dense. Sankalpas are supplied rather than drawn out of the reader.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The chapter template** (invitation → benefits → when to use → optional preparation → sankalpa → timed script → immediate freewriting → outdoor variant → bedtime and wake-up micro-rituals). It solves the "a script floating alone" problem and gives readers a week's worth of use from one chapter.
  - **The stage list keyed to the eight limbs** (pp. 45–47) as a teacher-facing map. Note her caveat that order should respond to need. Compare it in your table with Satyananda (rotation and opposites) and iRest (Inner Resource): Stanley's version puts pratyāhāra first and ends in journaling.
  - **"Sleep Flow" as separate from yoga nidra.** This is a tidy answer to the "is falling asleep failure?" debate (compendium file 08, §3). Give readers a named practice *for* sleep, and keep yoga nidra for staying aware at the threshold.
  - **Concrete trauma options:** the eyes-open seated nest facing the door, permission to leave, open eyes. Also low-resource adaptations (rolled T-shirt, books under a towel). These are short and easy to adopt. Credit the source.
  - **Micro-practices for householders** (2–3 minutes at transitions, and using chores as portals), if you want a "daily life" chapter.
  - **The self-guiding steps,** and the argument that recordings are a beginning, not the destination.
  - **Her honest-but-respectful lineage paragraph** (naming Swami Rama's allegations while explaining why she still cites him) as a model of tone for debate §6.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Don't repeat** the "ni = void, dru = draw forth" etymology.
  - **Don't repeat** delta-while-aware as an established finding, or the epigenetics leap. If you mention them, label them as tradition or anecdote and cite the meta-analyses in compendium file 06.
  - **Don't copy** the bīja-mantra placements, the "do not place fire or the sun" guidance, or the Netra Tantra and Sri Vidya material. They come from initiatory lineages. Unless you hold that training, describe them as what Stanley teaches and send readers to her.
  - **Ritual specifics:** offer them as optional colour, or leave them out if your audience is secular or clinical (debate §4).
  - **Ready-made sankalpas:** her printed ones are lovely, but decide your own stance. If you take the "discovered sankalpa" line, note that this book mostly *supplies* sankalpas and makes them optional (see Confidence).
  - **Six long practices:** if your readers are beginners, consider shorter core scripts. Her shortest main script is already 15–20 minutes.
