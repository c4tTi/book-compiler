## In one paragraph
*Radiant Rest* is a practice handbook and a spiritual manifesto for rest. Stanley is a former Hollywood film producer who has taught yoga nidra since the early 2000s. She presents yoga nidra in three ways at once: as a technique, a state of consciousness and a goddess (Introduction, p. 3; see the quotes below). The techniques only prepare you for the state, which comes through grace. The book is written for beginners, for busy "householders" and for teachers (who are told to practise one script consistently for 40 days before teaching it). It moves from history and the subtle-body map (Part One), through the obstacles to rest (trauma, race-based stress, ancestral exhaustion, feeling undeserving) and how to set up a nest and an altar (Part Two), to six practices ordered from gross to subtle (Part Three). The practices follow the five kośas from physical to bliss (per the publisher), and several are tied to an element; Practice Six goes beyond the elements (elements printed for Practices 1–2 only in what I saw; kośas for 3–5 inferred from chapter themes). Each is surrounded by rituals for falling asleep and waking. The promise is deep rest as a birthright, and beyond that, waking up to one's own radiance.

What the chapters contain, from the Introduction's own outline (pp. 5–9) and the snippets:
- **Ch. 1** is a short history. The medieval texts that name yoganidrā (the *Devī Māhātmya*, *Haṭhapradīpikā*, *Yoga Tārāvalī* and *Maṇḍala Brāhmaṇa Upaniṣad*) give no complete or detailed instructions for entering the state (p. 17). The modern teachers covered are Satyananda, Swami Veda Bharati (p. 18), Richard Miller, Rod Stryker (taught by Mani Finger; his own style is "Enlightened Sleep"), Amrit and Kamini Desai, and Dinsmore-Tuli and Tuli (pp. 18–19). Swami Rama and his teacher "Mataji" follow (pp. 20–21). Then comes the goddess Yoganidrā: the *Devī Sūktam*, and a version of the *Devī Māhātmya* story in which Brahmā praises her until she appears (she is the one who holds Viṣṇu in his meditative sleep), and the suggestion that you frame a practice as a prayer to the Divine Mother (pp. 21–23). This section also cites Birch and Hargreaves' essay on the history of *yoganidrā* (p. 23).
- **Ch. 2** sets out the states of the *Māṇḍūkya*. It lays brain-wave stages and sleep stages over them, describes the Menninger experiments with Swami Rama, the koshas, and "gateways" to turīya. It includes a self-inquiry section mid-chapter (pp. 30–31, per reviewer check) and ends with the stage list (pp. 45–48) and a closing section on Nidra Shakti (p. 48, per reviewer check).
- **Ch. 3** covers the obstacles to relaxing: trauma, race-based stress (drawing on Gail Parker), ancestral narratives, and the sense of not deserving rest. Its exercises are Body Awareness, Pratyāhāra and Energetic Protection.
- **Ch. 4** is the Householder's Flow: a 24-hour cycle of 2–3 minute "mini practice portals".
- **Ch. 5** covers the nest, props, the altar, and falling asleep.
- **Chs. 6–11** are the six practices.
- **The Conclusion** teaches you how to guide yourself without a recording.

## Core ideas
- **Beyond technique.** Yoga nidra is a technique, a state and a goddess. For Stanley the state arrives by grace and can't be forced; what she teaches are the preparatory methods (p. 3).
- **Laya yoga: dissolving from gross to subtle.** The practice moves awareness from the physical body inward through the five kośas. The six practices are ordered to match, roughly: earth and the physical body first, then prana, then (inferred from the chapter themes) mind, wisdom and bliss, and last the "void" beyond the elements.
- **The Māṇḍūkya map.** Waking, dreaming, deep sleep and turīya, each linked to the syllables of AUM and to a centre in the body (third eye for waking, throat for dreaming, heart for deep sleep). The **junctions between states** are the gateway to turīya. Here she draws on Swami Lakshmanjoo's Kashmir Shaivist idea of a gap or junction between waking and dreaming, and between dreaming and deep sleep (p. 41). Hence her stress on the moments of falling asleep and waking.
- **Transitions as portals.** Bedtime and wake-up rituals use the hypnagogic and hypnopompic states. Every pause in the day (between breaths, at sunrise or sunset) is one of her *little nidra moments*.
- **Rest as birthright and reclamation.** Stanley writes that she never saw anyone in her family rest and had to learn how (p. 58). Self-inquiry prompts ask whether you feel you deserve rest (pp. 61, 71). Ancestors can be invited to rest with you and asked for protection. Race-based stress is not the same as PTSD (via Gail Parker, p. 55). The framing is close to "rest as resistance" without being polemical; Tricia Hersey is not cited.
- **Devotion over discipline.** Practice is reframed as a 24-hour flow for householders. She openly criticizes the way yoga is sold as a wellness product instead of a lifelong path (Yoga Journal excerpt).
- **Nature as teacher.** "Nature-amplification" practices take the visualizations (moon, stars, earth, water) outdoors to the real thing. She calls this "rewilding".
- **Self-inquiry and freewriting** after every practice. Memory (*smṛti*) of what arose is treated as part of the practice.
- **Sankalpa is used lightly.** It is often offered but optional, and it is let go as practice deepens into non-doing (p. 46; see quote 2). The glossary (p. 196) ties sankalpa to a sincere, committed resolve aimed at one's deepest truth.
- **Lineage, told honestly but devotionally.** She honours the Himalayan lineage and the Divine Mother. She also notes the documented abuse allegations against Swami Rama (p. 20). For context (not from the book): in September 1997 a Pennsylvania jury awarded a woman about US$1.9 million over sexual assaults by Swami Rama at the Himalayan Institute in 1993, when she was 19 (*Hinduism Today*, December 1997). She points readers to Dinsmore-Tuli's *Yoni Shakti* under the heading "History of Harm in Yoga Nidra" (Recommended Resources, p. 213).
- **Where she stands on "is it ancient?" (`08` §1):** the "Partly" position. She grants that the medieval texts give no complete instructions (p. 17) while rooting the state and the goddess in old texts and some techniques in Himalayan teaching.

## The practice as this book teaches it

**A. Her generic sequence (ch. 2, "Basic Practice Steps", pp. 45–48).** She stresses that this is the common order, not a rule. Teachers should respond to whatever the student needs at the time rather than follow it mechanically (p. 45). The steps are mapped onto Patañjali's limbs:
0. **Pre-practice** (p. 45, per reviewer check). Gentle movement, mantra or breathing to prepare the body, with a grateful attitude toward the practice.
1. **Supported posture.** Śavāsana, side-lying, restorative shapes or any fully supported position, with plenty of props. This is *āsana* as a steady, effortless "seat".
2. **Diaphragmatic breathing.** Watch the navel rise and fall until the breath is effortless, smooth, even and continuous (*prāṇāyāma*).
3. **Pratyāhāra.** Withdraw from sound, sight and the other senses, like the tortoise drawing in its limbs. She calls this the most important step, the start of a gradual dissolution (p. 46).
4. **Sankalpa.** A resolve, intention or affirmation. It is optional, and released in deeper states.
5. **Systematic relaxation of the body**, part by part. Then awareness of breath: feel that the body is breathing you, and give in to the involuntary breath. (The snippet doesn't show whether breath awareness is a heading of its own.)
6. **Visualization.** An image, points of light or a mantra (*dhāraṇā*).
7. **Resting awareness.** Settle at the heart, throat, third eye, or lower at the womb (*dhyāna*).
8. **Surrender.** Dissolve into silence and spaciousness, possibly toward samādhi or turīya, which comes, if it comes, through grace.
9. **Welcoming ourselves back.** Space, time, breath, body.
10. **Sankalpa smṛti.** Remember the sankalpa in the transition back to waking.
11. **Freewriting or journaling**, usually 3–15 minutes, straight after practice.
12. **Gratitude and self-love** (p. 48, per reviewer check). Thanks for the practice and love for oneself.

Compared with Satyananda: the generic list has no Satyananda-style rapid naming rotation (starting at the right thumb). Instead, step 5 relaxes the body part by part, and Practices 3 and 5 use Himalayan point-to-point journeys through the body (marma points / śavayātrā). I did not see how many points she uses. For comparison, Swami Rama's own exercise is a numbered 61-point journey, and Swami Veda Bharati teaches 31 then 61 points (see `rama1988.md`, `vedabharati2015.md`). There are **no pairs of opposites** and **no rapid images** in the generic list. Pratyāhāra is a named stage before body relaxation, and the list closes with journaling and then gratitude and self-love. She credits rotation and opposites to the "Bihar Tradition" in the appendix (p. 180).

**B. How each practice chapter is built.** The template is very clear:
- an invitation naming the theme (and, in Practices 1–2 at least, the element and kośa);
- **Benefits**, **Practice this** (when to use it: anxiety, before an exam, decisions, low energy) and **Teach this** (notes for teachers). Every practice chapter has a Teach this section (pp. 104, 113, 124, 136, 147, 158, per reviewer check). Practice Four's version says to teach it only after 90 consecutive days of daily practice (p. 136);
- optional **Preparing for Deep Relaxation** (poses, prāṇāyāma, bīja mantras such as *lam* and *gam*, or *aum hrim*);
- a **ready-written sankalpa** for that practice;
- the timed script, with minutes for each segment in brackets;
- **Self-Inquiry** questions (10–15 minutes of fast writing, straight after);
- a **Nature-Amplification** practice;
- a **Bedtime Ritual** and a **Wake-Up Ritual** of 2–15 minutes.

Techniques by practice (from snippets):
- **Practice One.** Optional preparation: grounding poses (child's pose, seated forward bend, garland pose), 1:2 breathing (5 min), an *apāna vāyu* meditation (3 min), bīja mantras *lam* and *gam*. In the nest with a timer: ten letting-go breaths, stillness, sensing the space the body takes up (2 min), and drawing a circle of protection. The script ends with three deep breaths with a sigh and gratitude to the earth (p. 111), then 3–10 min freewriting. Outdoors: lie in sand, grass or on a rock.
  - *"I Am Held" ritual (pp. 109–110):* get into bed left foot first, crocodile pose, nine audible gentle sighs with attention at the heart, gratitude to the earth for its stability. *"I Am Supported":* get out of bed right foot first.
- **Practice Two.** Aims: build pratyāhāra and practise dissolution (laya). Opens with a circle of protection and a minute of moving from sound to sound, with all sounds left outside the circle. AUM is breathed in "waves" along a printed chart pairing body zones with the five elements, from the soles up to a point twelve inches above the crown (a note explains *dvādaśānta*, "twelve finger-widths" beyond the body). Ends in spacious awareness at the energy centres. Outdoors: "Elemental Activation" by moving water. Moon breathing through the left nostril at bedtime.
- **Practice Three.** Based on the Himalayan "Journey of the Corpse"; the book's term is "Shavaratra" (Journey of the Corpse; usually spelled śavayātrā) (p. 126, per reviewer check). Awareness and prana move point to point through marma-linked points, with "points of light" or other images; she warns not to place fire or the sun, and to consult a teacher. The point sequence begins at the eyebrow centre (p. 128). Mantra placement comes from a Netra Tantra teaching by Pandit Rajmani Tigunait. Breath countdown from 20 to 0. Outdoors: star-gazing, then "placing" a star at the third eye; and a Fire Offering in which each passing thought is offered to a real or imagined fire (p. 134). Mauna ritual: devices out of the bedroom; Mirror Gazing uses the mental alarm clock.
- **Practice Four.** Chant *aum hrim* first. Breath count from 20 to 0. Breath drawn from the crown to the navel, then to the spiritual heart. A guided journey to a door into the "cave of the heart" and an eternal flame, drawing on the *Chāndogya Upaniṣad*'s small space within the heart-lotus. Ask questions and receive a gift, then freewrite. Also sky-gazing and "Clearing the Heart Space" (3–10 min).
- **Practice Five.** Alternate-nostril breathing (2 min per side). A point-by-point rotation through the body (the list includes the toes and the third eye), filling it with cooling moon nectar and the vibration of the bīja *srim*; meant to be savoured, not rushed. A moon-breath practice uses *aum* on the inhale and *srim* on the exhale through the left nostril. "Moon Dream" uses the throat centre as the seat of dreaming; "Soma Beauty Ritual" carries the contemplation "I embody bliss" into the day.
- **Practice Six.** Recommended after months of steady practice. Starts in crocodile pose, then fetal position breathing through the right nostril. Contact points with the floor. *Ah* rising and *ham* falling along the spine. Breathing at each chakra from the third eye down to the base. Ten minutes resting in, and as, the dark void, then back to the heart. "I AM": sleep with attention at the heart, set the mental alarm clock. "I Am Awake": remember the void; every transition holds a little yoga nidra.

**D. Other printed practices with no audio.**
- **Pranava Practice** (5–30 min, ch. 2): chant A–U–M, moving down third eye → throat → heart, then upward, each syllable linked to a state.
- **Mental alarm clock** (ch. 2): picture an analogue clock set to your waking time.
- **Body Awareness Exercise** (5 min).
- **Pratyāhāra Practice** (10–15 min, may be done seated with eyes open).
- **Energetic Protection** (three circles).
- **Ancestor-protection practice.**
- **Sleep Flow** (5 min, credited to Swami Rama's teaching): crocodile pose, 4-count inhale and 8-count exhale, belly awareness.
- **Sustained-awareness night practices:** the 108 countdown to sleep (a note credits Bonnasse's *Yoga Nidra Meditation*), and the "Dream Stone", a crystal held through the night. She advises limiting these to two or three nights in a row.
- **Suṣumnā meditation** (10 min).

**E. Falling asleep (ch. 5, pp. 95–99).**
- Yoga nidra and sleep are kept apart. Approach yoga nidra as a transformative practice that also rests you. If the aim is to fall asleep, call it a separate **"Sleep Flow"**.
- Don't build the nest on your bed.
- Practise yoga nidra before noon where possible, so it isn't too close to bedtime.
- She calls yoga nidra "essential" for staying rested with insomnia (see the claims check below).
- A reclining nest option for people who tend to fall asleep, cough or snore sets the props so the head sits above the heart (p. 89).
- For persistent sleep problems she suggests a sleep study and an Ayurvedic herbal tonic (p. 98).
- Her ideal is to stay aware at the edges of sleep. Even so, falling asleep is treated as normal, not as failure. I saw no scolding about it in any passage.

**F. Trauma and accessibility.**
- She lists choices for trauma survivors (p. 54): eyes can stay open, you can leave the room, you can end the practice.
- An **"Eyes Open" nest** for PTSD: seated against a wall, facing the door (p. 89).
- Adaptations for wheelchair users (p. 89), and for people in prison with no props: a rolled T-shirt or socks (the first pass placed this on p. 92; not re-checked).
- Pregnancy (after the second trimester) and back problems: lie on the left side with a bolster between the knees (p. 88). The appendix says crocodile pose (Makarasana) is not for pregnancy or back pain (p. 183), but p. 90 says it may support students with back pain while advising against it in pregnancy; the book is inconsistent. Several bedtime rituals and Practice Six start in crocodile pose.
- She sends people with PTSD to qualified teachers (p. 53).

**G. Recurring opening: circle of protection.** Practices 1 and 2 and the Energetic Protection exercise open the same way in the snippets I saw. After settling, you draw a circle of protection around the body (light, fire or anything you choose), then let the sounds of the room sit *outside* the circle while you stay inside it. It works as a pratyāhāra technique and a safety cue at once. The first draft also listed Practices 5 and 6, but I have no snippet or page showing it there; check before you rely on it.

**H. Voice.** Invitational and devotional rather than clinical: "Feel ...", "Notice ...", "Imagine ...", with exact timings in brackets. A typical guided moment, paraphrased: you are led along the breath, deeper each time, toward a door at the back of the heart's cave. You push it open and sit before a flame, then ask it a question and wait for what comes. Dense, image-rich and full of mantras compared with Satyananda's terse commands or iRest's neutral "welcoming". There is very little "I am practising yoga nidra"-style formula.

**I. Teaching guidance.** Teachers should do each practice for at least 40 days, then try it out on a few students or a colleague and ask for feedback (Introduction, pp. 9–10). Practice Four carries the stricter 90-day condition (p. 136). The Conclusion argues that a playing recording may at some point *limit* how far one can go, because turīya is beyond thought (p. 172). She gives steps for self-guiding: two 40-day sādhanas of one practice, memorizing the script, the mental alarm clock, and 30 minutes of silence.

## What's distinctive
- **Built around transitions.** Bonnasse (French 2015, English 2017) first made bedtime, night and waking the field of practice (see `bonnasse2015fr.md`), and Stanley credits him for the 108 countdown. Stanley is the first in this set to attach a named bedtime and wake-up ritual to every script. By contrast, Satyananda asks you to stay awake during the session, and Miller holds that awareness is present even if you drop off (`08` §3).
- **Rituals around the practice:** altar-building, the ancestor circle, the protective circle, nature-amplification outdoors, and the "Householder's Flow" of micro-practices. Brody has a 40-day programme and Dinsmore-Tuli has the nest and the goddess, but neither combines Sri Vidya mantra (*srim*, *hrim*) and Himalayan techniques (śavayātrā, the crocodile-pose 1:2 breath) with this ritual layer.
- **Kośas as separate practices.** Miller moves through the kośas inside one practice; Brody (by the summary's inference from chapter titles) gives each kośa a chapter of a 40-day calendar; Stanley gives each kośa a standalone practice, several tied to an element, ending with a practice beyond the elements. The kośa labels for Practices 3–5 are inferred (see Confidence).
- **A Black woman teacher's perspective** on who gets to rest. The book contains race-based stress (via Gail Parker), ancestral exhaustion and an ancestor-protection practice, prison adaptations and other accessibility notes. (Her press list includes Black + Well Voices and Black Girl in Om.) Compare Brody, whose frame is gender and women's leadership rather than race, and Hersey's *Rest Is Resistance* (2022), which makes rest a political stance; Stanley does not cite Hersey and keeps the frame spiritual and devotional.
- **Lineage handled with candour.** For a lineage-honouring book, it is unusually open about Swami Rama's documented allegations, and its resource list points to Dinsmore-Tuli's *Yoni Shakti* for the history of harm in yoga nidra.
- **Self-guiding as the goal.** Dinsmore-Tuli & Tuli (2022) give a staged nine-step weaning plan from the recording, and Moore's readers record their own voice. Stanley's twist is two 40-day sādhanas of one memorized script plus the argument that turīya lies beyond any recording.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - A coherent, reusable chapter template.
  - Realistic short formats for busy people.
  - Concrete trauma and accessibility options.
  - Honest that medieval texts give no complete or detailed instructions (p. 17), and that yoga nidra-as-technique is modern and plural (appendix of six schools).
  - Credits its sources: Dinsmore-Tuli for the nest, Rajmani Tigunait for mantra placement, Swami Rama for the Sleep Flow, Bonnasse for the 108 countdown. It also cites Birch and Hargreaves' historical essay, which is rare in a practitioner book.
  - Beautiful design: a PubWest book design gold award, 2022, according to the author's press page.
  - Free audio for all six main practices.
  - It seems to avoid the stock claim that one hour of yoga nidra equals four hours of sleep. A Google Books search for "four hours" turned up only the "twenty-four-hour" practice cycle. Other wordings (e.g. "three hours", "hours of sleep") were not searched; see Reviewer notes.
  - Strong reader reception: Goodreads 4.52 from 442 ratings and 61 reviews (October 2026). Readers single out its accessibility, its trauma-informed inclusivity (including prison and pregnancy adaptations) and its use as a reference to return to.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **Little formal criticism exists.** I found no review in a journal, *Yoga Journal* or *Yoga International*. The press archive lists features, gift-guide mentions, licensed excerpts and dozens of podcasts, so reception is mostly promotional or by readers.
  - Goodreads readers note three problems. It is hard to follow written practices without audio. A digital copy is a poor fit for a rest practice. And one reviewer felt the book turned practice into a daily obligation they would fail at.
  - **Science chapter (my assessment against `06`):** it lays brain waves, sleep stages and the four states on top of one another. She claims practitioners reach delta-wave deep sleep while staying aware (p. 34). Support comes from the Swami Rama/Menninger demonstrations and a personal communication from Dean Radin about Swami Veda Bharati (p. 27). These are anecdotes. The EEG literature mostly shows alpha and theta, with more theta in experienced practitioners [@kachera2025].
  - **Epigenetics:** a 2018 study of the sons of Civil War prisoners of war is used to argue that many of us carry inherited, DNA-level stories that relaxing is unsafe (p. 60). The study is almost certainly Costa, Yetter & DeSomer, *PNAS* 115(44): 11215–11220 (2018), doi:10.1073/pnas.1803630115 (I read the abstract; I did not see Stanley's note to confirm the match). It found that sons of ex-POWs held in the worst camp conditions were somewhat more likely to die after age 45, with no effect on daughters; the authors favour an epigenetic explanation but cannot rule out psychological or cultural effects. It says nothing about inherited beliefs or "stories" that relaxing is unsafe, so treat her claim as metaphor.
  - **Ancient origins of the point journey.** She traces the Journey of the Corpse to the *Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā* and *Suśruta Saṃhitā*. Partly supported: medieval texts do teach pratyāhāra by moving breath and attention through 18 marmasthānas (*Vasiṣṭhasaṃhitā* 3.61–74, parallel *Yogayājñavalkya* 7.6–31; see `mallinson2017.md`). The *Suśruta Saṃhitā* is a medical text that catalogues marmas, not a meditation; the śavayātrā name and the 31/61-point forms are modern Himalayan-tradition teaching (see `01` on the Himalayan tradition and `rama1988.md`).
  - **Etymology:** *nidrā* is glossed as *ni* "void" + *dru* "to draw forth" (p. 2, glossary). That is not standard Sanskrit (*ni* + √*drā*, "to sleep").
  - **Ritual details** (which foot first into bed, mirror gazing, crystals, protective circles, invoking ancestors) will read as esoteric to secular or clinical readers.
  - **Mantras and bīja placements** come with a "consult a teacher" caveat that a book can't fully meet.
  - **The practices are long** (up to 50 minutes) and image-dense. Sankalpas are supplied rather than drawn out of the reader.
  - **Claims check (against `06`):**
    - *Benefits boxes:* I did not read each practice's Benefits box in full, so I can't list its claims. What I saw names uses (anxiety, before an exam, decisions, low energy), grounding, a mental reset and one-pointed focus (Practice Three), and intuition (Practice Four). Calming and stress relief are consistent with general relaxation research (`06` §6.4); exam performance, decision-making and intuition are untested (`06` §6.3). Read the boxes before quoting any of them.
    - *Insomnia ("essential"):* the 2026 sleep meta-analyses rate certainty as very low (Singh et al. 2026) or flag moderate-to-high risk of bias (Dutta et al. 2026) (`06` §6.0). Treat "essential" as the author's experience, not evidence.
    - *Ayurvedic herbal tonic (p. 98):* unverified medical advice. Leave it out of your book, or pair any mention with "see a clinician".
    - *Delta-while-aware and epigenetics:* see above.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The chapter template** (invitation → benefits → when to use → teaching notes → optional preparation → sankalpa → timed script → immediate freewriting → outdoor variant → bedtime and wake-up micro-rituals). It solves the "a script floating alone" problem and gives readers a week's worth of use from one chapter.
  - **The stage list keyed to the eight limbs** (pp. 45–48) as a teacher-facing map. Note her caveat that order should respond to need. Compare it in your table with Satyananda (rotation and opposites) and iRest (Inner Resource): Stanley's version names pratyāhāra as its own stage before body relaxation and ends with journaling followed by gratitude and self-love.
  - **"Sleep Flow" as separate from yoga nidra.** Lusk draws the same line between a sleep aid and the practice (`lusk2015.md`); Stanley goes further and names and scripts the sleep practice. Useful for the "is falling asleep failure?" debate (`08` §3).
  - **Concrete trauma options:** the eyes-open seated nest facing the door, permission to leave or end the practice, open eyes. Also low-resource adaptations (rolled T-shirt, books under a towel). These are short and easy to adopt. Credit the source.
  - **Micro-practices for householders** (2–3 minutes at transitions, and using chores as portals), if you want a "daily life" chapter.
  - **The self-guiding steps,** and the argument that recordings are a beginning, not the destination.
  - **Her honest-but-respectful lineage paragraph** (naming Swami Rama's allegations while explaining why she still cites him) as a model of tone for debate §6.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Don't repeat** the "ni = void, dru = draw forth" etymology.
  - **Don't repeat** delta-while-aware as an established finding, or the epigenetics leap. If you mention them, label them as tradition or anecdote and cite the meta-analyses in `06`.
  - **Don't present** the śavayātrā as a text from the *Suśruta Saṃhitā*; "medieval roots, modern Himalayan form" is accurate.
  - **Don't copy** the bīja-mantra placements, the "do not place fire or the sun" guidance, or the Netra Tantra and Sri Vidya material. They come from initiatory lineages. Unless you hold that training, describe them as what Stanley teaches and send readers to her.
  - **Ritual specifics:** offer them as optional colour, or leave them out if your audience is secular or clinical (debate §4).
  - **The crocodile-pose contradiction** (p. 90 vs. p. 183): if you borrow the pose, give your own clear guidance for back pain and pregnancy.
  - **Ready-made sankalpas:** her printed ones are lovely, but decide your own stance. If you take the "discovered sankalpa" line, note that this book mostly *supplies* sankalpas and makes them optional (see Confidence).
  - **Six long practices:** if your readers are beginners, consider shorter core scripts. Her shortest main script is already 15–20 minutes.
