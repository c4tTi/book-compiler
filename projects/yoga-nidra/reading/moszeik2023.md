## In one paragraph
This is a German doctoral thesis. It tests whether recorded yoga nidra, practised alone at home, measurably improves stress, mood, sleep, mindfulness and the daily cortisol curve in ordinary adults. The design is an online randomised trial run in 2018–19. **362** people were analysed in four groups: an **11-minute** yoga nidra (n = 101), a **30-minute** yoga nidra (n = 80), a **10-minute music** active control (n = 74) and a **waitlist** (n = 107). Participants practised for two months, "ideally daily", and were measured before, right after, and three months later. A subsample of **229** gave saliva samples (5 a day on two days, at each time point). The thesis also asks *for whom* the practice works: by personality, prior meditation experience and how often people actually practised.

Its central message is sobering and well documented. The effects are **real but very small**. Against the music control they almost disappear. They depend on dose and on the person.

**How much is about yoga nidra:** all of the empirical work (methods, results and discussion, roughly pp. 80–173) is about yoga nidra. Description of the *practice itself* is short: the definition-and-mechanisms section (pp. 37–51) and the description of the recordings (pp. 89–93) come to about 20 pages, under 10% of the thesis. About 20 more pages are general yoga philosophy (Patañjali, the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad via Swami Rama) and mindfulness theory.

## Core ideas
- **11 min vs. waitlist (Table 8, p. 126):** small improvements in stress (d = −0.14), anxiety (−0.16), depression (−0.14), rumination (−0.15), "nonreactivity" (0.13) and "nonjudging" (0.12).
  - The confidence intervals for life satisfaction (0.10) and sleep disturbance (−0.08) include zero.
  - Cortisol did not change.
  - The effects largely held at the three-month follow-up.
  - This replicates the author's earlier 30-day study (Moszeik et al. 2020).
- **11 min vs. music (Table 9, p. 128):** only depression differed (d = 0.13), and partly because the music group's depression scores *rose*. Music vs. waitlist improved only life satisfaction (d = 0.07), and that did not last.
- **30 vs. 11 min (Table 10, p. 131):** the only gain was "acting with awareness" (d = 0.10, 95% CI −0.01 to 0.44). The author's hypothesis that longer means better was mostly not confirmed.
- **30 min vs. controls (pp. 132–133):**
  - vs. waitlist: d = 0.08–0.19; the largest effect was on stress (−0.19); it also showed a flatter cortisol awakening response.
  - vs. music: d = 0.06–0.09 (stress, depression, rumination, awakening response), with **no CI fully excluding zero**.
- **Cortisol depends on dose, and its direction is ambiguous (pp. 139–140, 155–156):** cortisol effects appeared only when practice frequency was modelled.
  - Short form: more practice went with lower total cortisol but a *steeper* morning rise.
  - Long form: more practice went with total cortisol falling *less*.
  - The author reads both "more activation" and "more relaxation" as favourable. That is post-hoc interpretation, not a clean finding.
- **Adherence was modest (pp. 105, 139–141):**
  - During the two months, 4% practised on all 57 days, about 30% on 39–56 days, and nearly half on only 9–38 days.
  - Three months later, 43% had stopped. Only 4 people (2.7%) still practised almost daily, and the 11-minute version was the one people kept using.
- **Personality matters (pp. 134–137, 165):**
  - Conscientiousness was the most consistent amplifier.
  - Neuroticism weakened the short form's effect on stress but went with *more* benefit from the long form.
  - Openness helped both forms.
  - Agreeableness mainly moderated the music condition.
  - These are exploratory, one-sided, uncorrected correlations.
- **Prior experience cuts both ways (p. 138):** experienced meditators gained more from the 11-minute form and less from the 30-minute one.
- **Small effects can still matter:** the author argues, citing Funder & Ozer (2019), that small effects of a cheap, scalable practice can add up (pp. 169–170). She also concedes that **about 310 per group** would have been needed to detect such effects reliably (p. 170).
- **Sankalpa as autosuggestion:** she frames sankalpa as self-suggestion and contrasts yoga nidra with hypnosis, where another person suggests the change (pp. 40–41). She did not measure whether resolutions came true and says this should be tested (p. 157).

## The practice as this book teaches it
The thesis teaches no practice to the reader, but it documents the two recordings in unusual detail.

**Reference sequence (Satyananda 2009, pp. 42–43):** preparation; first relaxation of body and breath; sankalpa; rotation of awareness through the body; breath awareness (counting); pairs of opposites (warm/cold, heavy/light, positive and negative emotions); observing the inner space and visualisation; sankalpa repeated; externalisation.

**11-minute version** (from her 2016 thesis, quoted p. 90):
- It keeps body, breath, sankalpa and the observation of thoughts and feelings.
- The body rotation covers only the right and left sides, without front and back.
- Breath is observed for about 20 seconds, against about two minutes in longer versions.
- It was meant for beginners, and it is the version people kept using after the study.

**30-minute version** (developed for this study with three test listeners, pp. 90–92; 2025 paper):
- a full rotation
- longer breath counting
- opposites (one line pictures the whole trunk as heavy as lead)
- a **chakra sequence** of about 5 minutes. Each of the seven centres is paired with a quality such as security, creativity, strength or contentment, and listeners reflect on it.
- rapid images (participants mention a sunflower and a red triangle)
- **gratitude** just before the final sankalpa
- a **smile as the first movement** on returning (participant feedback, pp. 148–149)

**Voice and pace:** deliberately fast, monotone and directive, with few pauses, "so the mind has no time to wander" (p. 90). She admits this is unfamiliar and sometimes unpleasant for beginners. The e-mails reframe the speed as a tool and promise that the pauses leave room for insight (p. 203).

**Sleep:** the resolve to stay awake is presented as an invitation, not a duty, and dozing off is accepted (p. 38). Participants reported falling asleep after the body rotation at first, and later staying awake for the second half (p. 147).

**Sankalpa handling (e-mails, p. 201):**
- one short, positive "I am …" sentence, kept for all eight weeks
- sample sentences included "I am calm and relaxed", "I am free of old experiences" and "I am successful"
- listeners were told to imagine how it feels once the resolve has come true, and to trust the brain to fulfil it

**Posture and timing:** 60% always practised lying down. Evening (38%) and afternoon (31%) were the most common times. Following Kündig (2010), she advises doing the long form before 2 pm (pp. 106, 70).

**Observer stance:** an "inner cinema seat", watching whatever "film" appears without expectation or judgement (pp. 41–42).

## What's distinctive
- It is the only book-length study in the project that holds yoga nidra to a **modern trial standard**: randomisation, an active control, effect sizes with intervals, a follow-up, a biological marker and a sample in the hundreds.
- Its **literature table** (Table 2, pp. 55–60) is the most systematic list of yoga nidra studies up to about 2022. Of 45 empirical studies she found, only 13 were randomised and only 5 had an active control (p. 53). She also notes that most papers do not say what the "yoga nidra" actually contained.
- It is the only source that compares **two lengths of the same lineage practice** directly, and it reports what real people did with a recording at home: dose, drop-off and obstacles.
- Its qualitative tables of the **main benefit** and **main obstacle** (pp. 145–146) are rare evidence of how people actually experience home practice. Most named benefits were "switching off / rest / calm". The top obstacle by far was lack of time or routine.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - It is transparent: full tables, honest small numbers, and the author's own admission of low power and of the music group's shortcomings (pp. 166–171).
  - It has a three-month follow-up and the largest German cortisol sample in a meditation trial, by the author's account (p. 15).
  - It gives a clear description of the recordings, which most yoga nidra trials lack.
  - The design and core results also appear in a peer-reviewed open-access RCT (Moszeik, Rohleder & Renner 2025). Two further papers from the project analyse personality and mindfulness facets (2025, 2026).
- **Limitations and critiques.** I found no published reviews, so these points are my own reading:
  - **Statistics:**
    - The trial was not publicly preregistered (2025 paper, §3.15).
    - All tests are **one-sided**, and nothing is corrected for multiple comparisons across about 14 outcomes, four comparisons and dozens of moderator tests.
    - Many "significant" results sit at p ≈ .04–.05, with intervals that touch zero.
    - The personality and cortisol-by-frequency results should be treated as hypotheses.
  - **Attrition:**
    - 426 were randomised, but 61 dropped out before the first survey, and the losses were uneven: the music group fell from 99 to 74 and the 30-minute group from 100 to 80 (2025 paper).
    - Completed surveys fell from 337 to 265 to 229.
  - **Sample:** self-selected and highly educated (46% graduates); 81% women; 45% in Bavaria; recruited partly through yoga studios. A third already had substantial meditation experience, and participants were more conscientious and agreeable than German norms.
  - **The active control is weak and unequal:**
    - It is 10 minutes of Indian-style music from Santulan's SOM programme. That programme describes itself as its own interpretation of yoga nidra and claims sound sets the body's fluids vibrating (p. 93). Many participants found the music strange or unpleasant (p. 149).
    - The yoga nidra groups received e-mails about sankalpa and the "observer"; the music group got a short "Afternoon Relaxation" note (pp. 201–202). Expectancy was therefore not equal.
    - The 30-minute practice was compared with 10 minutes of music, so length is confounded, as she acknowledges (p. 154).
  - **Allegiance:** the author is a committed practitioner with Satyananda-lineage teachers. She designed, voiced, delivered and evaluated the recordings herself.
  - **The theory chapter repeats lineage claims with little scrutiny:**
    - one hour of yoga nidra equals up to four hours of sleep, with only the note that Satyananda estimated it from his own practice (p. 38)
    - the right-thumb start "relaxes" a large homunculus area of the brain (pp. 39–40); the participant e-mails repeat this (p. 202)
    - Napoleon and Einstein used the state (p. 43; e-mail, p. 202)
    - Swami Rama perceived his surroundings in deep sleep (p. 42)
    - dream = theta, deep sleep = delta (p. 30)
    - dissolving and "reprogramming" samskaras (p. 48)
    - chakras mapped onto endocrine glands and cerebrospinal fluid (pp. 91–92)
    - Satyananda's list of conditions helped, including addiction and suicidality (p. 44)

    Her own data support none of these, and `06` §6.3 flags most of them.
  - **Hypnosis contrast:** the contrast with hypnosis (p. 41) is stated as a principle, not tested. The recordings themselves deliver directive suggestions in the teacher's voice.
  - **Safety:** coverage is thin. A five-row side-effects table adapted from Sedlmeier (p. 36) and Satyananda's warning about escaping into the "intact world" of yoga nidra (p. 44) are all there is. No adverse events were recorded.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The honest numbers.** Use them in your research box: tiny effects against a waitlist, near zero against music, and the 30-minute form not clearly better than 11 minutes. This is the single best German-language source for "better than nothing, about as good as other relaxation" (`06` §6.0).
  - **A small correction for the compendium.** "Only depression beat music" applies to the 11-minute form. The 30-minute form also edged out music on stress, depression and rumination, but with d < 0.1 and intervals crossing zero.
  - **The adherence data.** Use them for a chapter on building a practice. Even motivated volunteers rarely practised daily, half dropped off within months, and the **short** form survived best. That supports offering an 11–15-minute core practice with a longer version as an option.
  - **The obstacles table (p. 146) and the participant voices** on speed, immobility and falling asleep. They are good material for a troubleshooting FAQ for beginners.
  - **Concrete design ideas from the long form:** gratitude before the closing sankalpa, and a smile as the first movement. Credit them as features of her recording.
  - **The literature table (pp. 55–60)** as a map of studies to 2022. Add the 2025–26 meta-analyses from `06`.
  - **For debate 10 (hypnosis),** cite her as stating the lineage view: self-suggestion, not suggestion from someone else. Don't cite her as evidence for it.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - Every mechanism claim in §2.2: the four-hours-of-sleep claim, the homunculus, the brainwave ladder, chakra–gland mapping, "reprogramming". She cites, but does not test, any of them.
  - The cortisol "benefits". Their direction changes between groups, so don't write "yoga nidra lowers cortisol" on the strength of this study.
  - The personality findings. They are exploratory and uncorrected. "Conscientious people benefit most" may simply mean "people who practise benefit most".
  - The sample sankalpas, such as "I am successful". They show the self-optimisation framing that `08` debate 8 questions, so contrast them with Miller's heartfelt desire or Stanley's approach.
  - The fast, directive delivery. It is presented as the authentic method, yet participants found it hard at first. If you prefer an invitational voice (`07` §7.4), this thesis is evidence that pace is a real barrier for beginners.
  - Her yoga-history passages (dating, folk etymology of "Patañjali"). Use Birch & Hargreaves and Singleton instead.

In English: whether ancient meditation, yoga nidra, NSDR or the new nap ("of the 21st century", omitted above), the nervous system is glad of every moment of rest.
