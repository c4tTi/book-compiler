## In one paragraph
This is a textbook for students and teachers of Indian postgraduate "yoga science" courses and for would-be researchers. The blurb also names insomnia patients and psychiatrists. The book has two halves. The first eight chapters cover theory: history, scripture, sleep, dream, samādhi, states of consciousness, other authors' views and "scientific bases". The next nine cover research: a review of earlier studies, then the author's own doctoral experiments, set out hypothesis by hypothesis with t-tests. A single short chapter on doing the practice closes the book. The book promises a lot: yoga nidra is the best of all relaxation methods, a state between sleep and samādhi, and it has been shown experimentally to change blood pressure, pulse, brain waves, blood values, memory, well-being, anxiety and stress. It is one of the few Indian academic monographs on the subject (as far as I found). Its value for you is less as a guide to practice than as a window on how the practice is framed and "validated" inside Indian yoga universities.

## Core ideas
- **Yoga nidra is a state, not just a method.** It sits between sleep and samādhi, half asleep and half awake, a kind of sleep in which awareness stays on. The book says it is neither sleep, concentration nor hypnosis, and defines it as an altered state of consciousness. At the same time it uses the word for a taught technique.
- **Two layers of origin.** The foreword places yoga nidra in the Vedic age; ch. 1 (p. 1, the only page of it seen) opens with the Vedic antiquity of yoga in general. In his journal papers, though, Kumar says plainly that the present form was designed by Swami Satyananda from tantric practices. He also notes that in the Haṭhapradīpikā (4.49, where khecarī is to be practised until yogic sleep arises) the word means samādhi. The textual sources he cites are the Devī Māhātmya/Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa (Viṣṇu in yoganidrā at the end of the aeon; Brahmā praising the goddess Yoganidrā) and the Haṭhapradīpikā.
- **A psychological model of purification.** Repressed emotion and stored impressions (*saṃskāras*) cause modern psychosomatic illness. Yoga nidra brings them up to be witnessed and released, stripping away the conscious layer to reach the unconscious and finally superconscious levels.
- **Relaxation as stress physiology.** It reduces sympathetic arousal, and with it pulse, breathing rate and blood pressure. The preface adds that it balances the two brain hemispheres and the two branches of the autonomic nervous system.
- **Rotation of consciousness is a simplified nyāsa.** The therapeutic second stage uses felt states to rebalance the elements. Heaviness evokes earth/water for someone anxious or ungrounded; lightness evokes air/space for someone heavy or depressed. Healing images are placed on affected body parts.
- **It is "proved".** The author's six-month study of postgraduate yoga students is presented as confirming effects on almost every measure: pulse, respiration, BP, alpha EEG, skin conductance, haemoglobin, ESR, leucocyte count, memory, well-being, anxiety, stress, regression, guilt, arousal and fatigue. A separate study of hypertensive patients is added.
- **Spiritual endpoint.** Beyond health, the practice prepares for pratyāhāra, dhāraṇā and meditation, and aims at turīya, the state beyond waking, dream and deep sleep.
- **Guru-dependence and staying awake.** It is best learned from a guru. Self-practice tends to end in sleep, and then nothing is gained.
- **"1 hour = 4 hours of sleep"?** I did not find this claim in the pages I saw or in the three papers. But the 2022 review he co-authored attributes the claim to this book (its ref. 15), so it is probably somewhere in the text: check before saying he does or does not make it. The claim is unsupported (`06` §6.3). It is older than this book: Satyananda made it in Japan in 1969, and the 6th edition repeats it, as quoted by Bhushan 2001 (`reading/satyananda1976.md`); a 1977 Bihar School *YOGA* magazine item gives a related "half an hour = two hours" version (`reading/satyananda1974.md`); and a 1983 *Akhand Jyoti* article repeats it [@akhandjyoti1983]. *Akhand Jyoti* is the Gayatri Pariwar monthly that the foreword writer edits, from the same Shantikunj institution as DSVV.

The surrounding prose is prescriptive, moralising (the mind is said to be filled with negative input all life long; saṃskāras are compared to household dirt to be swept out daily) and guru-centred.

**Example of the therapeutic logic (not script wording),** paraphrased from the ch. 9 model: someone anxious and ungrounded is guided to feel the body growing heavy, to call up earth and water. Someone stuck or depressed is guided into lightness, to call up air and space.

## What's distinctive
- **A book-length report of a single Indian doctoral research programme on yoga nidra** (the only one I know of), with the result tables printed. None of the other books in this project reports **blood values** (haemoglobin, ESR, total leucocyte count) or uses the **Eight State Questionnaire** constructs (regression, guilt, arousal, fatigue). The design: 110 DSVV postgraduate yoga students aged 20–30, 40 men + 40 women practising, 15 + 15 controls, quota sampling. A hypertension study of 40 patients is added.
- **Against its nearest neighbours.** Satyananda 1976 also carries a research section, but as reports and appendices by others from the 1970s–90s (`reading/satyananda1976.md`); Kumar's is one author's own controlled study, with tables. Panda 2003 (`reading/panda2003.md`), by an independent Indian author whose material is visibly Bihar School, goes much further on clinical applications (about 35 conditions, each with a prescribed sankalpa) but offers no trial data at all. Kumar is the data-heavy, application-light member of the trio.
- **An Indian-academic voice.** It combines Gayatri Pariwar/DSVV "scientific spirituality" with Bihar School practice and Ayurvedic and Purāṇic citations. Western yoga nidra teachers such as Richard Miller (iRest) do not appear in the pages I saw. The Western authors who do appear are psychologists and popular writers (Colin Wilson, Robert A. Baron).
- **Sanskrit verses in Devanagari plus transliteration** for the sleep and scripture material. These are handy leads, but unchecked.
- **The element-based reading of the "opposites" stage.**
- **A sleep-medicine primer inside a yoga nidra book.** Dated, but unusual.
- **Where Kumar sits in the debates (`08`):** §1, a two-layer view (ancient word and state, Satyananda's modern form); §2, both state and method; §3, the hardest line in this project, sleep = failure and "no progress"; §10, says it is not hypnosis, without arguing the point.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Gathers in one place scriptural, Ayurvedic and sleep-physiology material that popular books skip.
  - Shows its data, so you can check them. Few yoga nidra books do this.
  - In his papers the author is candid that the present technique was designed by Satyananda. That is more honest than the "ancient Vedic practice" framing of the foreword.
  - The reference lists of the author's papers are a useful map of the small Indian yoga-journal literature (IJTK, Yoga Vijnana, *Dev Sanskriti Journal*, etc.). I did not see the book's own bibliography.
  - The author is a working academic who has supervised dozens of dissertations and co-authored the 2022 review in *Sleep and Vigilance*, which cites this book (ref. 15).
- **Limitations / critiques** (no published review of the book found; points below are my own checks against primary records):
  - **Weak study design.** Students were not randomised: "stratified-quota" allocation was used, with only 15 controls per sex. Both groups were yoga students doing daily āsana and prāṇāyāma, so the comparison is "yoga plus 30 min of yoga nidra" against "yoga". The tables compare post-test means only: the book's tables are described as post-mean values and the 2012 paper's figure is labelled "post ESR values". The 2008 paper says both groups took psychological tests before starting, but reports no pre-test results. No baseline appears in the tables I saw, and there is no blinding and no correction for dozens of tests. Every hypothesis seen is declared proved.
  - **The statistics don't add up.** I recomputed ordinary two-group t-tests from the printed means and SDs, with 40 vs 15 per sex. In 7 of the 8 tables I could see, the result is smaller than the printed t. Female anxiety comes out at t ≈ 1.1 (printed 2.2), female ESR ≈ 0.5 (printed 2.82), male pulse ≈ 1.9 (printed 3.40). Only male ESR and female diastolic BP stay significant at the claimed 0.01 level. Even the printed values t = 1.98 (male well-being) and t = 2.2 (female anxiety) fall short of 0.01, yet are reported "at 0.01". (Possible caveat: a different test, e.g. on change scores, may have been used without being shown.) The same anxiety figures (4.90 vs 5.60, t = 2.5) appear in the 2008 paper with no split by sex, and in the book as the figures for male subjects only. The 2022 review describing the 2008 study names different scales (Hamilton) and prints t-values as P-values.
  - **Studies attributed to yoga nidra that tested something else.** The book (p. 57) and the 2012 ESR paper report that a Tel Aviv study by Cooper showed yoga nidra lowering serum cholesterol in cardiac patients; the 2008 paper makes the same claim about "cardiac patients", citing Cooper & Aygen without naming Tel Aviv. PubMed shows that **Cooper & Aygen (1979)** tested **Transcendental Meditation** in 23 people with high cholesterol, not cardiac patients. The book attributes yoga nidra findings to **Carrington et al. (1980)**, which compared clinically standardised meditation, a breath-counting meditation and progressive muscle relaxation in telephone-company staff. **Bali (1979)**, given in the book as "1997", tested unspecified "relaxation" in 18 men. The "American pilot" was a single case of general **yoga relaxation** (Brownstein & Dembert 1989). These citations circulate widely in yoga nidra literature; this book passes them on uncorrected.
  - **Physiology that outruns the evidence:**
    - The body is said to become magnetised and to fill with electromagnetic pulsation (p. 118).
    - The brain's capacity for brain-wave relaxation is said to be only ten minutes for beginners, and a small noise is said to damage brain fibres (p. 46, in the views-of-authors chapter).
    - The practice harmonises the two hemispheres (preface).
    - The Copenhagen brain images, credited to Troels Kjær and colleagues at the Kennedy Institute, are presented as confirming meditation as a fourth major state of consciousness (p. 59). No study is cited on the page. Kjær co-authored both Kennedy Institute PET studies of yoga nidra, so the source is almost certainly Lou et al. 1999 (nine experienced yoga teachers, per the PubMed abstract) and/or Kjaer et al. 2002 (n = 8); see `06` §6.1. The Lou 1999 abstract speaks of distinct activity patterns for meditation and for ordinary rest, not of a fourth major state.
    - **Alpha EEG (ch. 11):** more alpha is consistent with the alpha/theta reports in `06` §6.1, but `06` notes that only increased theta in experienced practitioners holds up across the 2025 EEG review. Ch. 11's data come from the DSVV student study, which I did not read.
    - In the ESR paper (part of the book's own study): a lower ESR in healthy students, whose values are normal anyway, is read as stronger immunity, explained through better iron absorption.
    - In a **separate 2009 paper by the author** (Kumar & Joshi; not one of the book's underlying studies): alpha EEG and skin-conductance changes after 40 days of yoga nidra plus Pranakarshan prāṇāyāma, in 40 Haldwani students with no control group, lead to the conclusion that the practice improves immunity. These uncontrolled pre/post data add nothing to the EEG evidence.
  - **Careless compilation:**
    - "Regression" is defined through Galton's statistical regression to the mean (p. 108) before the psychological sense.
    - "Guilt" gets a legal-dictionary definition.
    - A passage on respiratory biofeedback expands "rfb" as "residual free bubble" (p. 70).
    - Some descriptive passages read as stitched together from other writers. Sources are named for some passages (Wilson, Mathew, Baron); I could not check the others.
  - **Safety and authority:** benefits depend on reverence for the guru, and people with mental illness should do three hours a day. Both sit badly with current trauma-informed guidance (`07` §7.1), which advises short practices and clinical support for psychosis and dissociation.
  - **Reception:** no journal or long-form review found. Goodreads shows 1 rating and 0 reviews. Publisher and retailer pages carry only the blurb.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **Scriptural leads** for your history chapter: Devī Māhātmya ch. 1 (the goddess Yoganidrā in Viṣṇu's eyes; Brahmā's hymn) and Haṭhapradīpikā 4.49 (yoganidrā as samādhi). Verify the verses in Pargiter 1904 and in Mallinson & Singleton, *Roots of Yoga*, before quoting. Kumar's own two-layer view (old word and state, Satyananda's modern form) supports the "ancient roots, modern form" position in `08` §1, coming from inside an Indian lineage-trained academic.
  - **The Ayurvedic sleep verses** (Caraka 21.51; Suśruta Cikitsā 24.88) if you write a sleep chapter. Check them in a critical edition and translation.
  - **The elements reading of the "opposites" stage** as a teaching image: heaviness for the anxious, lightness for the stuck. Present it as a traditional or therapeutic heuristic, not as physiology.
  - **A case study for your research chapter.** The book shows why the early Indian yoga nidra literature, which the 2025–26 meta-analyses pool, carries a high risk of bias: non-random small controls, post-only comparisons, many outcomes, generous significance claims. One paragraph on this explains to readers why "73 studies" does not mean strong evidence.
  - **A warning box on recycled citations.** Cooper & Aygen = TM; Carrington = other meditation and PMR; Bali = generic relaxation. Your readers will meet these "yoga nidra studies" elsewhere.
  - **For the compendium:** the book is not yet in `02`. Suggest a ★ entry under D (Scholarship and history), or a new Indian-academic subsection, for its history leads and as a risk-of-bias case study.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - Every numerical result and every "hypothesis proved". Cite the meta-analyses in `06` §6.0 instead.
  - The claim that it is the best of all relaxation therapies. The best evidence says yoga nidra is not clearly better than other active relaxation.
  - Magnetism, brain fibres, the ten-minute capacity, hemisphere balancing, ESR/immunity.
  - The guru-reverence condition and the three-hours-a-day advice for mentally ill people. Write the opposite into your safety chapter.
  - The Vedic-age origin claim of the foreword (for yoga nidra) and the Vedic-antiquity framing of yoga that opens ch. 1.
  - The sleep-stage material, which uses pre-2007 terminology; use current AASM N1–N3.
  - The "1 hour = 4 hours of sleep" claim if you find it in the book (`06` §6.3).
  - The book as a source for the practice itself. Satyananda 1976 is the primary for this format, and Kumar's practice chapter is only six pages.
