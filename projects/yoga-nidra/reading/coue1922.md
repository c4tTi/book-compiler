# Self Mastery Through Conscious Autosuggestion — Émile Coué (1922)

> **Basis of this summary:** Full text read (free, legitimate source: the book is in the public domain). I read the Project Gutenberg text and checked page numbers and quoted wording against the open Internet Archive scan of the 1922 American Library Service edition; the essay, aphorisms, séance report and the Burnat-Provins and Paris pieces closely, the cure reports and patients' letters only by skimming and keyword search. Brooks (1922) and Yeates (2016c) were read in part for the followers' theory and the publication history; two research papers were checked for modern evidence. Links are listed at the end of the summary.

**Where to get it:** Free and legal from Project Gutenberg (eBook #27203; no page numbers, and it leaves out the front "How to Practice" card). The Internet Archive has an open scan of the 1922 New York edition (American Library Service), with the original pagination. Cheap reprints exist, e.g. Cosimo Classics 2007 (ISBN 9781602061156) and Sun Books (ISBN 0895400952), both listed on Open Library. A London Allen & Unwin edition and a 1920 English edition (per Wikipedia) were not seen. The US translator is not named, and Yeates (2016c, PDF p. 20) says the English *Self Mastery* was published without Coué's knowledge or consent. The French source, *La Maîtrise de soi-même par l'autosuggestion consciente*, first appeared in the Lorraine Society of Applied Psychology's journal in 1921; Coué sold offprints of it (Nancy, 1922) at his sessions (Yeates 2016c, PDF p. 20). Its subtitle in Yeates's reference list links it to Coué's 1912 paper "De la suggestion et de ses applications" (*Bulletin de la Société d'Histoire Naturelle et de Palethnologie de la Haute-Marne*), which Yeates calls a lecture. No audio.
**Tradition / lineage:** Not yoga. Nancy school of suggestion (Coué studied with Liébeault and Bernheim) plus American "suggestive therapeutics" (a footnote credits the preliminary experiments to "Sage of Rochester"). **Length / format:** About 93 printed pages: a 35-page essay by Coué, then aphorisms, case reports, testimonies and letters. One complete spoken "script", the curative monologue (pp. 19–22), plus self-practice instructions.

## In one paragraph
Coué was a pharmacist who ran a free suggestion clinic in Nancy, and this short book is the founding text of "Couéism". Its claim: our actions follow the **imagination** (which he equates with the unconscious), not the will, and when the two conflict, imagination wins. So you change yourself by giving the unconscious a simple idea, repeated effortlessly at set times, not by trying harder. Most of the book is promotion: cures, admiring séance reports, letters of thanks. **For a yoga nidra writer it matters for one reason:** it is the primary source for the repeated, positive, self-given formula that the compendium calls a "close cousin" of the sankalpa (`01` §1.2, `08` §10), and it shows exactly how the ritual was done.

## Structure
I saw the printed table of contents (unnumbered front page of the 1922 scan). In short: Coué's title essay (pp. 5–35, ending in case reports), his "Thoughts and Precepts" (p. 36), "Observations" (p. 43) and "Education As It Ought To Be" (p. 50); then a séance report (p. 55), patients' letters (pp. 62–79), "The Miracle Within" by Marguerite Burnat-Provins (p. 80; the contents page prints "Burnet-Provins"), and two reports of Coué in Paris (pp. 85, 88). Nothing mentions yoga. The pages that bear on the sankalpa are pp. 7–24 (will vs. imagination; how to make an autosuggestion; the spoken procedure and self-practice ritual) plus scattered passages (pp. 34, 36–39, 53, 60, 81, 84). Roughly three-quarters is cure stories.

## Core ideas
- **Imagination beats will.** Four "laws": imagination wins any conflict with the will; two mock-mathematical ones he admits are illustrations; imagination can be directed (p. 14). Everyday examples: the plank easy on the ground but impossible high up, the insomniac trying to sleep, the name you cannot recall, the cyclist steering into the obstacle (pp. 7–9).
- **No effort.** Effort brings in the will, which produces the opposite result; the suggestion must be made simply and without effort (pp. 36–39). His follower Baudouin later called this the "law of reversed effort" (as reported in Brooks 1922).
- **All suggestion is autosuggestion.** Another person's suggestion works only if the listener's unconscious takes it up (p. 11). Coué equates autosuggestion, so understood, with hypnotism (p. 12).
- **One idea filling the mind tends to become action** (p. 15).
- **A general formula beats specific ones.** "In every respect" covers every need, and the unconscious acts on the organ "which it can pick out itself" (p. 21).
- **Realism clause.** The aim must be within our power (footnote, p. 12); improvement is gradual and "within reason", not instant (p. 39).
- **The unconscious works at night.** Burnat-Provins reports Coué saying the evening repetition matters most (p. 84). Sleeping children are said to be open to suggestion because the conscious self rests while the unconscious stays awake (p. 34; also p. 53).

## The practice as this book teaches it
**1. Self-practice: the twice-daily formula (pp. 22–23, 60, 81; front card).**
- **When:** every morning before rising and every night on getting into bed; Burnat-Provins adds a muscle-relaxing position, in bed or an easy chair (p. 81).
- **How:** eyes shut, repeat the formula twenty times, counting on a string with twenty knots so that counting takes no attention. Move the lips (the séance report calls this indispensable, p. 60); use a monotonous, litany-like voice (p. 81).
- **Attitude:** don't attend to the words or the illness; stay passive, without will, but confident. The essay also asks the patient to imagine being in Coué's presence (p. 23); Yeates (2016c) says Coué dropped this in 1923.
- **Wording:** in the book, "Every day, in every respect, I am getting better and better" (pp. 23, 60, 81); the front card of the scan has "Day by day, in every way…". Yeates reports Coué tried over ten English versions before settling on "Every day, in every way, I'm getting better and better" in 1923.
- **For pain or distress during the day:** stroke the painful part (or the forehead) while saying "It is going" very fast for 20–25 seconds (p. 60; front card).

**2. The guided session (pp. 15–22).** Hypnotic technique: Coué calls it hypnotism (p. 12), and Yeates (2016c) shows he used hypnotism throughout.
- **Preliminary "experiments":** postural sway and a hand-clasp the subject "cannot" open, given in a commanding tone with fixed gaze.
- **The curative monologue:** opens with closed eyes and the reassurance that Coué will not put the subject to sleep, then runs through second-person, future-tense suggestions in a fixed order: appetite, digestion, excretion, sleep, mood, temper, intrusive thoughts, each organ, self-confidence. Typical rhythm (p. 20): a long sentence that names the time frame, then "you will…" plus a string of three adjectives and a promised good feeling on waking (see Short quotes). Voice: monotonous and soothing, meant to make the subject drowsy (p. 22).
- **The return:** a count of three, with suggestions of waking alert, not drowsy.
- **Sleep:** Coué stopped trying to put patients to sleep because it made them anxious; many fell asleep anyway (pp. 23–24).

**The drowsy-state theory is not in this book.** Coué gives the timing (bed, waking) and says the unconscious works at night. The theory comes from followers: Brooks (1922), presenting Baudouin's "outcropping of the subconscious", writes that it is greatest "just before we fall asleep and just after we wake" (Brooks's wording, not Baudouin's). Brooks also says to let the mind wander during repetition. Yeates (2016c) reports Coué's 1912 text already advised falling asleep while thinking the words.

## What's distinctive
- **The primary text behind the sankalpa comparison.** Compared with Satyananda's sankalpa as the satyananda1976 summary describes it:

  | | Coué | Satyananda |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Repetitions** | twenty | three |
  | **Delivery** | aloud, lips moving, mechanical, words not attended to | mental, with feeling |
  | **Tense** | progressive present ("I am getting better") | future ("I will…") |
  | **Will** | kept out entirely | the practice "trains the will" |

  Shared: a short positive phrase kept unchanged, general aims, placement at the threshold of sleep.
- **An early "script" with a yoga nidra shape:** closed eyes, "I won't put you to sleep", monotonous voice, a systematic tour of the body (by function), a counted return.
- **It undercuts "the teacher never suggests".** If every suggestion works only as autosuggestion (p. 11), every guided instruction is a suggestion, not just the sankalpa (`03` §3.12, `08` §10).

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Short, plain, free; concrete ritual details (times, posture, number, counting device, voice).
  - Vivid will-versus-imagination examples, especially insomnia.
  - Modest in places: the realism clause and gradual improvement. The book also reports Coué saying "I have never cured anyone" (Burnat-Provins, *The Miracle Within*, p. 83; Mme Leon has "I have never cured anybody", p. 93); it is not in his own essay.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **Uncontrolled cure claims:** tumours, tuberculosis, paralysis, Pott's disease; failures blamed on the "3 per cent" who cannot or will not understand (p. 23).
  - **Self-blame:** the séance report quotes Coué telling anyone who still makes bad autosuggestions to "accuse yourself" and say "Mea culpa" (p. 61).
  - **Ethically unusable material:** suggestions to sleeping children; the claim that a pregnant woman's mental image fixes the child's sex (p. 50).
  - **Contemporary critics** (Wikipedia, citing Yeates 2016a; not read at source): physicians objected to claims of curing organic disease, delayed treatment and undiagnosed group treatment; psychoanalysts (Abraham, Fenichel) objected too; a 1923 *Boston Herald* follow-up found most gains had faded.
  - **Thin modern evidence:** a 2022 review finds "little empirical evidence on autosuggestion" (Myga et al. 2022); people with low self-esteem felt *worse* after repeating "I'm a lovable person" (Wood et al. 2009, abstract).
  - **No evidence for the sleep-threshold timing.** `06` cites no research on suggestion given at sleep onset and lists "reprograms the subconscious" as a metaphor (§6.3). Treat the bedtime/waking placement as tradition and convenience, not a proven receptive window (see `03` §3.9 on hypnagogia, `08` §11 on what to promise).

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **Reversed effort and the insomnia / forgotten-name examples** to explain "don't try to relax, don't try to sleep" (pp. 7–9, 36–39).
  - **The historical facts for a sankalpa chapter:** a short phrase repeated at sleep onset and on waking, counted on knots. Label the receptivity idea a theory of Coué's followers (Brooks; Baudouin as reported by Brooks), not a finding. Yeates links the knotted string to the Hesychast prayer rope (his inference), a nice note beside the mala.
  - **Progressive wording ("I am becoming…")** as an honest option for readers who find "I am healed" false; it fits Brooks's warning not to set faith impossible tasks, the Wood (2009) finding, and the Bihar lineage's later "I am developing…" (see satyananda1976).
  - **The realism clause** as a safety rule for choosing a sankalpa.
  - **Coué's candour that suggestion is suggestion**, for a "cousin of hypnosis, with awareness kept" position (`03` §3.12); his "I won't put you to sleep" tactic deserves a footnote beside yoga nidra's "not hypnosis" reassurance.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **All cure claims and testimonials**, and any hint that a resolve "always" works; the "accuse yourself" line shows how this becomes self-blame (`03` §3.4, `08` §8).
  - **The hypnotic "experiments" and commanding voice**; they clash with invitational, trauma-aware guiding (`07`).
  - **Mechanical recitation versus the felt sankalpa:** two different models; choose deliberately.
  - **"Imagine you are in my presence"** (dropped by Coué himself) and suggestions to sleeping people, especially children: dependency and consent problems.
  - **Don't write "sankalpa comes from Coué".** Nothing here proves descent. Write "resembles": scholars note the overlap with Western autosuggestion (Birch & Hargreaves 2015, via compendium `01`; not read for this summary, so check Birch's exact wording before citing).

## Confidence
- **Certain (read in the text):** the contents, ritual details, formula wording and page numbers (1922 American Library Service edition; scan pages run 4 ahead of printed numbers); quoted fragments checked against Gutenberg and the scan; Coué's equation of autosuggestion with hypnotism (p. 12); the absence of a drowsy-state *theory* in Coué's own text.
- **From secondary sources:** 1921 journal publication, 1922 Nancy offprint, the English edition's lack of consent, the 1923 final English wording and the prayer-rope link (Yeates 2016c); the 1920 English edition and the critics' objections (Wikipedia); the outcropping theory (Brooks 1922); Birch & Hargreaves (via compendium `01` only).
- **To verify:** the London Allen & Unwin edition; whether the "Day by day" front card was in the first printing; Baudouin's *Suggestion and Autosuggestion* (1920) as the real source of "outcropping"; Birch & Hargreaves's actual wording.
