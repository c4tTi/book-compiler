The brief also lists a London edition from Allen & Unwin, which I did not see. Wikipedia says an English edition first appeared in England in 1920 (unverified). The US translator is not named. The French source is *La Maîtrise de soi-même par l'autosuggestion consciente* (Nancy, c. 1922). According to Wikipedia, its title essay goes back to Coué's 1912 lecture "De la suggestion et de ses applications". No companion audio comes with the book.
**Tradition / lineage:** Not yoga. It belongs to the Nancy school of suggestion: Coué studied with Liébeault and Bernheim. It also draws on American "suggestive therapeutics": a footnote credits the preliminary experiments to "Sage of Rochester". **Length / format:** About 93 printed pages. A 35-page essay by Coué is followed by aphorisms, case reports, testimonies and patients' letters. It contains one complete spoken "script", the curative monologue (pp. 19–22), plus the self-practice instructions.

## In one paragraph
Coué was a pharmacist from Troyes who ran a free suggestion clinic in Nancy. This short book is the founding text of "Couéism". Its claim is that our actions follow the **imagination**, which he identifies with the unconscious, rather than the will. Wherever the two are in conflict, imagination wins. So you change yourself by giving the unconscious a simple idea to accept: you repeat it, effortlessly, at set times. You do not try harder. The rest of the book is promotion: patients' cures, admiring reports from Coué's group sessions, and letters of thanks, with newspaper blurbs about celebrity cures on the last pages. **For a yoga nidra writer it matters for one reason.** It is the primary source for the repeated, positive, self-given formula that the compendium calls a "close cousin" of the sankalpa (`01` §1.2, `08` §10). It also shows exactly how the ritual was meant to be done.

**How much of it is about yoga nidra:** none directly. The book predates the modern practice by about fifty years and never mentions yoga. About 22 of its 93 pages bear on the sankalpa:
- will versus imagination, and how to make an autosuggestion (pp. 7–15);
- the spoken procedure and the self-practice ritual (pp. 19–24);
- scattered passages on sleep, effort and the formula (pp. 34, 36–39, 53, 60, 81, 84).

The rest (roughly three-quarters) is cure stories and testimonials.

## Core ideas
- **Imagination beats will.** Coué states four "laws". The first is that imagination always wins when the two conflict. The second and third use mock maths, and he admits these are only illustrations. The fourth is that imagination can be directed (p. 14). His examples are everyday: the plank that is easy to walk on the ground but impossible high up, the insomniac who tries to sleep, the name you cannot recall, the learner cyclist steering into the obstacle, giggles you cannot stop (pp. 7–9).
- **No effort.** Effort brings in the will, and the will triggers the opposite result. The suggestion must be made "simply", "naturally" and "without any effort" (pp. 36–39). His follower Baudouin later named this the "law of reversed effort" (Brooks 1922).
- **All suggestion is autosuggestion.** A suggestion from another person works only if the listener's unconscious takes it up as its own (p. 11). Coué also writes that autosuggestion, so understood, is "nothing but hypnotism as I see it" (p. 12).
- **One idea filling the mind tends to become real.** You cannot think two things at once, and a thought that fills the mind "tends to transform itself into action" (p. 15).
- **A general formula is better than specific ones.** "In every respect" covers every need, and the unconscious "picks out" the organ concerned by itself (pp. 21, 23).
- **Realism clause.** The thing must be "in our power" (footnote, p. 12), and results come only "within reason" as a gradual improvement, not an instant cure (p. 39).
- **The unconscious works at night.** As reported by Burnat-Provins, the evening repetition matters most (p. 84). Sleeping children are said to be open to suggestion because their conscious self is at rest while the unconscious stays awake (p. 34; also p. 53).

## The practice as this book teaches it
**1. Self-practice: the twice-daily formula (pp. 22–23, 60, 81; front card).**
- **When:** every morning before getting up, and every night on getting into bed. Burnat-Provins adds a position that relaxes the muscles, in bed or in an easy chair (p. 81).
- **How:** shut your eyes and repeat the formula twenty times. Count on a string with twenty knots so that the counting takes no attention. Move your lips; the séance report calls this "indispensable" (p. 60). Use a monotonous voice, "as if one were reciting a litany" (p. 81).
- **Attitude:** don't think about the words or about your illness. Stay passive, without passion or will, but with confidence. The essay's version also asks the patient to picture himself in Coué's presence while repeating (p. 23). According to Yeates (2016c), Coué dropped that instruction in 1923.
- **Wording:** inside the book the formula reads "Every day, in every respect, I am getting better and better" (pp. 23, 60, 81). The front card of the scanned copy says "Day by day, in every way…". Yeates reports that Coué tried more than ten English versions before settling on "Every day, in every way, I'm getting better and better" in 1923. The French is *Tous les jours, à tous points de vue, je vais de mieux en mieux*.
- **For pain or distress during the day:** stroke the painful part, or the forehead for mental distress, while saying "It is going, it is going…" very fast for 20–25 seconds (p. 60; front card).

**2. The guided session (pp. 15–22).** These are hypnotic techniques: Coué himself calls them hypnotism (p. 12), and Yeates (2016c) shows he used hypnotism all his life.
- **Preliminary "experiments":** postural sway (falling backward and forward), and a hand-clasp the subject "cannot" open. They are given in a commanding tone with a fixed gaze at the root of the nose.
- **The curative monologue:** "Sit down and close your eyes. I am not going to try and put you to sleep…" Then a fixed sequence of second-person suggestions: appetite, digestion, excretion, sleep, mood, temper, intrusive thoughts, each organ, and self-confidence. It is delivered in a "monotonous and soothing voice" meant to make the subject drowsy (p. 22).
- **The return:** a count of three, with suggestions that the subject will wake alert, not drowsy.
- **Sleep:** Coué says he stopped trying to put patients to sleep because the attempt made them anxious and resistant. Many fell asleep anyway (pp. 23–24).

**The drowsy-state theory is not in this book.** Coué gives the timing (bed, waking) and says the unconscious works at night. The theory comes from his followers. Baudouin's "outcropping of the subconscious" holds that the unconscious is most accessible "just before we fall asleep and just after we wake". Brooks (1922) popularised it, and he also tells the reader to let the mind wander during the repetition rather than pull it back. Yeates (2016c) reports that Coué's 1912 lecture already advised falling asleep while thinking the words.

## What's distinctive
- **It is the primary text behind the sankalpa comparison.** Among the project's books it is the only one that shows the autosuggestion ritual at source. It differs from Satyananda's sankalpa as the satyananda1976 summary describes it:

  | | Coué | Satyananda |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Repetitions** | twenty | three |
  | **Delivery** | aloud with moving lips, mechanical, emotionless, words not attended to | mental, with feeling |
  | **Tense** | progressive present ("I am getting better") | future ("I will…") |
  | **Will** | to be kept out entirely | the practice "trains the will" |

  They agree on three things: a short positive phrase, kept unchanged; general rather than specific aims; and placing it at the threshold of sleep.
- **An early "script" with a recognisable yoga nidra shape:** closed eyes; reassurance that you will not be put to sleep; a monotonous voice; a systematic tour of the body (by function, not by body part); a counted return to alertness.
- **Coué undercuts the "teacher never suggests" claim.** If every suggestion works only as autosuggestion (p. 11), then every guided instruction in a yoga nidra script is a suggestion too, not just the sankalpa. This matters for the hypnosis debate (`03` §3.12, `08` §10).

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Short, plain, free and quotable.
  - The ritual details are concrete: times, posture, number, the counting device, voice.
  - The everyday will-versus-imagination examples are vivid and still persuasive, especially the insomnia one.
  - Its tone is modest in places: the realism clause, "I have never cured anyone", gradual improvement.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **Cure claims with no controls:** tumours, tuberculosis, paralysis, Pott's disease, a dental haemorrhage stopped by suggestion; failures blamed on the "3 per cent" who cannot or will not understand (p. 23).
  - **Self-blame:** the séance report ends with "mea culpa" self-blame for anyone who still makes bad suggestions (p. 61).
  - **Ethically unusable material:** suggestions murmured to sleeping children, and the claim that a pregnant woman's mental image fixes the child's sex (p. 50).
  - **Contemporary critics** (as summarised on Wikipedia, citing Yeates 2016a): physicians objected to claims of curing organic disease, to delayed treatment, to group treatment without diagnosis, and to the "incantation" with a knotted string. Psychoanalysts (Abraham 1926; Fenichel) also objected. A *Boston Herald* follow-up in 1923 found that most patients' gains had faded.
  - **Modern evidence is thin:** a 2022 review in *Experimental Brain Research* concludes there is "little empirical evidence on autosuggestion" (Myga et al. 2022). An experimental study found that people with low self-esteem felt *worse* after repeating "I'm a lovable person" (Wood et al. 2009).

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **The reversed-effort principle and Coué's examples** (insomnia, the forgotten name). They plainly explain why yoga nidra says "don't try to relax" and "don't try to sleep". They also reassure readers who come for sleep (pp. 7–9, 36–39).
  - **The historical facts for your sankalpa chapter.** A short phrase, repeated at the threshold of sleep and on waking, counted on knots: a Western precedent for "plant it when the mind is receptive". Cite Brooks or Baudouin for the receptivity *theory* and label it a theory (`06` §6.3: "reprograms the subconscious" is a metaphor). Yeates links the knotted string to the Orthodox prayer rope. That is his inference, but it makes a nice cross-cultural note beside the mala.
  - **Progressive wording ("I am becoming…", "Every day… better").** Offer it as an honest option for readers who find "I am healed" false. It fits Brooks's warning not to set faith a task it cannot do ("I can hear perfectly" for the deaf). It fits the Wood (2009) backfire finding. And it matches the Bihar lineage's own later "I am developing…" (see satyananda1976).
  - **The realism clause** ("in our power", "within reason") as a ready-made safety rule for choosing a sankalpa.
  - **Coué's candour that suggestion is suggestion**, for a "cousin of hypnosis, with awareness kept" position (`03` §3.12). His tactic of saying "I am not going to put you to sleep" to lower resistance is worth a footnote next to yoga nidra's "not hypnosis" reassurance.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **All cure claims and testimonials,** and any implication that a resolve "always" works. Coué's "accuse yourself" shows how this turns into self-blame (`03` §3.4, `08` §8).
  - **The hypnotic "experiments" and the commanding voice.** They clash with invitational, trauma-aware guiding (`07`). Don't adapt the hand-clasp or sway tests.
  - **Mechanical, emotionless recitation versus the felt sankalpa.** These are two different models. Choose one deliberately rather than blending them.
  - **"Transport yourself into my presence"** (dropped by Coué himself) and suggestions given to people who are asleep, especially children: dependency and consent problems.
  - **Don't write "sankalpa comes from Coué".** Nothing here proves descent. Write "resembles", and cite Birch & Hargreaves (2015) for the scholarly view that the resolve stage looks borrowed from Western autosuggestion (`01`).
