## In one paragraph
This is the foundational academic history of "modern yoga", written for scholars. The jacket says it traces modern yoga to esoteric circles in late 18th-century Bengal and to Vivekananda's *Rāja Yoga* (1896). It then sets out a four-type typology, studies Iyengar Yoga, and reads a postural class as a "healing ritual of secular religion". **Yoga nidra content:** none that I found. Google Books' frequent-terms list has no "nidra", "Satyananda" or "relaxation". For your book it is a citation for the framework, not a source on the practice.

## Core ideas
- **Modern yoga is a new formation.** It grew out of Indian reform movements meeting Western esotericism and an individual-centred religiosity (jacket).
- **Vivekananda's *Rāja Yoga* (1896) is the starting point** (jacket; Altglas, per a fetch-tool summary).
- **Four types (2004):** psychosomatic, postural, meditational and denominational. The names come from the jacket. Wikipedia's table of pp. 187–189 gives the examples, including Sivananda's organisations and the Himalayan Institute as psychosomatic and Iyengar as postural.
- **Her own revision (2007, pp. 6–7 and nn. 25, 35)** adds a neo-Hindu type, calls the scheme provisional and names Kripalu as a psychosomatic example. Cite the article, not the book, for these points.
- **The class read as ritual** (jacket). Walsh reports that she maps the class onto separation, transition and incorporation, with Śavāsana as the last. I have not checked this in the book.

## The practice as this book teaches it
Not applicable. The book teaches no practice. Its only possible contact with yoga nidra is final relaxation at the end of a postural class, and I could not see how she treats it.

## What's distinctive
Among the project's sources, it supplies the standard scholarly vocabulary ("modern yoga", "modern postural yoga" and the typology). It says nothing specific about yoga nidra. For the history of relaxation use Singleton 2005/2010; for the Sivananda milieu use Strauss 2005.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:** Wikipedia reports that Newcombe credits it with founding the field. Walsh calls its framework clear and the book "exquisitely, meticulously researched".
- **Limitations / critiques:** Singleton (2010, as quoted on Wikipedia) says the typology "subsumes detail, variation, and exception". Nugteren (*Aries* 8(1), 2008 per Crossref) treats it as a preliminary overview (per Wikipedia; review not read). Walsh notes the jump from Vivekananda to Iyengar. She revised the typology herself by 2007. From the sources I saw, it has no treatment of Bihar/Satyananda or yoga nidra.

## For your own book
- **Take:** Only a background citation. Cite it where you introduce "modern yoga" or "modern postural yoga" as scholarly terms, and for the typology as a framework. If you want to place yoga nidra lineages within the typology, that placement is your own application. Cite the 2007 article for the five-type version and for Kripalu.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - Don't say she discusses yoga nidra, Satyananda or relaxation techniques.
  - Don't cite her for the claim that yoga nidra's relaxation comes from Western therapies. That argument is Singleton's (she credits Singleton 2005 in the 2007 article, p. 9). Three places in the compendium need adjusting: `01-history-and-lineages.md` (~line 110), the §F row of `02-source-books.md`, and §1 of `08-debates.md`. In each, cite [@singleton2005] for that claim and her only for the framework.
  - Don't attribute the ritual (separation/transition/incorporation) reading to her until you have checked it in a library copy.
