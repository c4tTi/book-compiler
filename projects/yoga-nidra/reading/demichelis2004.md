**Tradition / lineage:** None. This is an academic history of religions, based on her Cambridge PhD (Faculty of Divinity). She later co-founded the *Journal of Yoga Studies*.  **Length / format:** xvii + 282 pp.; bibliography from p. 261. No practices, scripts or audio.

## In one paragraph
This is the book that made "modern yoga" an academic subject. De Michelis traces the ideas behind Western yoga classes back to esoteric circles in late 18th-century Bengal (jacket). Walsh's review lists the Western ideas Indian thinkers absorbed: Mesmerism, Freemasonry, Positivism, "Theosophism", Transcendentalism, Christian Science and Unitarianism. She argues that fully-fledged modern yoga began with Vivekananda's *Rāja Yoga* (1896), which reconfigured the *Yoga Sūtras* "along the lines of a then-emerging New Age style of secularized and individualistically oriented religiosity" (jacket). She then offers a four-type **typology**, analyses Iyengar Yoga in detail, and closes by reading a postural class as a *healing ritual of secular religion*. It is written for scholars, and Walsh calls it "dry". **Yoga nidra content:** none that I could find. Google Books' frequent-terms list has no "nidra", "Satyananda" or "relaxation". What is useful to you is the typology (around p. 188) and the short Conclusion.

## Core ideas
- **Modern yoga is a new formation.** It grew out of Indian reform movements meeting Western esotericism and a secularised, individual-centred religiosity (jacket).
- **Vivekananda's *Rāja Yoga* (1896) is the starting point.** The jacket says so, and so does Altglas (fetch-tool summary), who calls his reading of the *Yoga Sūtras* the origin of present-day Western yoga teaching.
- **The 2004 typology has four types** (named on the jacket; examples from Wikipedia's table, pp. 187–189):
  - *Psychosomatic*: body–mind–spirit training. Examples include the Sivananda organisations and Swami Rama's Himalayan Institute.
  - *Postural*: Iyengar and Ashtanga.
  - *Meditational*: early TM.
  - *Denominational*: guru-centred and doctrinal, e.g. Brahma Kumaris and ISKCON.
- **The 2007 revision has five types.** It adds a "neo-Hindu" type and recasts psychosomatic yoga as "the early modern psychosomatic yoga of Vivekananda", with Sivananda's pupils, the Himalayan Institute and Kripalu as later examples (2007, p. 6, n. 25). The five are: early modern psychosomatic, neo-Hindu, postural, meditational and denominational. She calls the scheme provisional and notes that schools drift between types (2007, pp. 6–7, n. 25, n. 35).
- **Three phases after 1950** (2007, p. 5, citing ch. 6): popularisation (1950s to mid-1970s), consolidation (to the late 1980s), acculturation (after that).
- **The class as ritual.** The Conclusion interprets a postural class as a healing ritual of secular religion (jacket). Walsh maps it onto separation (centring), transition (āsanas) and incorporation (Śavāsana). I could not check what she means by "secular religion" or what is "healed", because I did not read the book.
- **The history of relaxation is Singleton's argument, not hers.** In 2007 she credits Singleton 2005 with it (p. 9).

## The practice as this book teaches it
Not applicable. There are no stages, scripts or sankalpa. The only point of contact with yoga nidra is final relaxation as the closing phase of the class. I could not see how she describes it.

## What's distinctive
Of the sources gathered so far (my judgement, not a claim the book makes), it is the one that gives Satyananda's milieu (Sivananda) and the Himalayan Institute a single named category, *psychosomatic* yoga (2004 typology, per Wikipedia's table of pp. 187–189). Kripalu is added to that category only in her 2007 article (n. 25), where it becomes "early modern psychosomatic yoga"; Wikipedia's table of the 2004 typology does not list Kripalu. Its *postural* category and its ritual reading of the class help explain why most Westerners first met yoga nidra as end-of-class Śavāsana. The neighbouring sources do other jobs: Singleton 2005/2010 (genealogy of relaxation and posture), Strauss 2005 (Sivananda ethnography), Alter 2004 (physiology claims) and Newcombe 2019 (Britain).

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:** It founded the field. Wikipedia reports that Newcombe credits it with this. Its concepts are clear and widely reused. Nugteren (*Aries* 8(1), pp. 107–109; Crossref gives 2008, Wikipedia's citation says 2007; content as paraphrased on Wikipedia) finds the typology workable. Walsh calls it "exquisitely, meticulously researched".
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - Singleton (2010, pp. 18–19, as quoted on Wikipedia) says the typology "subsumes detail, variation, and exception".
  - Nugteren treats the book as a preliminary overview.
  - Walsh notes that it jumps from Vivekananda to Iyengar.
  - The typology predates iRest, MBSR and NSDR, and she revised it herself by 2007.
  - From the contents list and the Google Books term list, the book appears to have no sustained treatment of Bihar/Satyananda. The Himalayan Institute appears in the typology as an example (pp. 187–189, per Wikipedia); I could not see whether it is discussed further. Check in a library copy.
