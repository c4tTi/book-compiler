## In one paragraph
This slim French manual from 1973 is the earliest book-length treatment of a relaxation method called "yoga nidra" that I know of, three years before Satyananda's *Yoga Nidra* (1976). Its author, Dennis Boyes, was born in Yorkshire, England, in 1939 (back cover). The BnF records his country as Great Britain and describes him as a Steiner-school educator, then a yoga teacher in Paris, and also a painter, poet and playwright. The book, Boyes says, is a practical treatise based on his Paris relaxation sessions. He wrote it at the request of students and teachers, and in the hope that hospitals, clinics and psychotherapy centres would one day offer relaxation (p. 3). It addresses the teacher who leads relaxation as much as the student. Anyone who wants to guide others, he insists, must first have trained seriously themselves (p. 9). The back-cover blurb recommends it especially for hypersensitive people. Its promise is a deep, sleep-like rest in which the practitioner stays awake (p. 8), opening onto the inner "spectator" (*turīya*) and, in an optional final part, "psychic development" (pp. 5, 56). After an introduction and four breathing techniques, it gives 42 methods graded in three "degrees". Boyes calls these stages "skin relaxation", "muscular awareness" and "organic awareness" (p. 4). The methods run from letting go and listening to the breath, through skin and muscle awareness, emptiness and non-effort (second degree), to the skeleton, organs and blood circulation (third degree). The last part covers triangles, visualisation, disidentification and *désobjectivation* (part 4). Boyes dedicates the book to his students, whom he also counts as his teachers. Their reports of experiences and difficulties in class helped him write it (dedication page).

## Core ideas
- **A method learned in India, then multiplied.** The methods derive from a basic method called "yoga nidra" that the author says he learned and practised in India. Starting from it, he says, he found many other methods by experimenting on himself, and he says their effectiveness was "proven" over his last five years of professional practice (p. 8). In India, he notes, the practice serves a spiritual aim, opening the mind to higher levels (p. 8).
- **Definition.** "Yoga nidra" means "yogic sleep": a sleep-like relaxation in which the practitioner stays awake (p. 8). The back-cover blurb, written in the third person and probably by the publisher, puts it as total muscular, nervous and mental relaxation with awareness kept alert. This is the "waking sleep" of the title.
- **Western suggestion vs. Eastern sensing (1973, pp. 3–7).** Western methods (Schultz, Jacobson), he says, on the whole use suggestion, imagination and thought. He gives Schultz's repeated formulas as examples. The Eastern methods he teaches rarely use suggestion and less imagination. They "walk" awareness through the body to feel its parts and sensations (heaviness, warmth) directly, without thought or mental image (pp. 4, 6–7). He grants that the Western methods may be just as effective. He also says that several methods in this very book do use images and thought (p. 7). The 1984 articles restate this contrast more sharply: many Western methods use suggestion, while methods from an authentic Eastern source do not (1984, no. 17).
- **Against hypnosis, for autonomy (1973).** Relaxation in the West is sometimes used hypnotically, he notes, because on the threshold between waking and dream the subconscious becomes wide open to suggestion. For that reason the teacher must watch both words and thoughts. Hypnosis, he says, does not fit yogic relaxation, because it reduces the person's autonomy (p. 8).
- **The witness.** Self-observation without judgement trains a "witness" or "spectator" position. This lets material surfacing from the subconscious be seen without identifying with it again (pp. 8, 11, 26). The 1984 articles develop this into seeing directly without words or images, because images and concepts strengthen identification with "name and form" (1984, nos. 15 and 17).
- **Positive attitudes planted in deep relaxation (1973).** Right after warning teachers not to over-insist on "relax completely" phrases, Boyes says the teacher *can* help students remove negative traits by asking them, when very relaxed, to take a positive attitude toward a given problem and to see themselves dealing with it effectively. Repeated, such attitudes end up soaking into the subconscious and replace negative tendencies (p. 19, snippet). Elsewhere he says yoga never works on symptoms or the negative, but always stresses the bright, positive side, so that a conscious order and positivity can act on the subconscious and undo old negative structures (p. 60, snippet). The Triangles method extends this to positive qualities (peace, joy, goodwill), with the remark that positive thoughts influence the subconscious, which returns them as events (p. 108, snippet). This is the closest thing in the book to a sankalpa: no term, no fixed formula, but a positive self-image repeated in deep relaxation to reshape the subconscious.
- **Observe a problem, don't fight it.** In a different register, observing a problem impartially, without wanting to remove it, lets it become an object that loosens its hold (p. 71, snippet; see also p. 74 on seeing one's problems "outside", at a distance). The book holds both approaches side by side. In 1984 he drops the first: he argues against suggesting a better future self and keeps only acceptance and observation (1984, no. 17; see "What's distinctive").
- **Graded inwardness, not a gross-to-subtle arc ending in emptiness.** The ToC is graded from first to third degree, then a psychic-development part ending in visualisation, disidentification and *désobjectivation*. Emptiness (Le vide, p. 68) sits midway, in the second degree, not at the end. The 1984 series describes a felt progression from coarse to fine sensations ending in emptiness, where relaxation turns into meditation (1984, nos. 14–15). That is a later account, and the 1973 ToC does not follow it in order.
- **Why tour the whole body (1984).** Strong sensations that rise from the unconscious, such as sudden heat, cold, tension or headaches, can only lodge in parts of the body that are still unconscious. Making every part conscious lets them be noticed and released (1984, no. 16). The rationale is psychological, not tantric (no *nyāsa*).
- **Two kinds of attention (1984).** "Penetrating" attention focuses on one area (he compares it to Theravada practice). "Panoramic" attention is open to the whole body and its surroundings, and it is where students tend to fall asleep (1984, no. 16).
- **Striving is an obstacle.** Too much intellectual effort does not belong in relaxation (pp. 7, 15). Practising in order to get something creates subtle resistance and can even cause agitation and insomnia (1984, no. 16).
- **Esoteric horizon.** The book brings in subtle bodies (an "etheric body") to explain how body sensations change in deep relaxation (pp. 23–24). It reads the left side of the body as "negative, feminine, defensive" and the right side as "positive, masculine, aggressive" (pp. 28, 89). The Triangles method has an occult framing involving spiritual "Masters". Boyes says it is not his own discovery and is practised by a worldwide network (p. 108). The 1984 series adds *prāṇa* in the *nāḍīs*, illness as slowed *prāṇa*, and "invisibility".

## The practice as this book teaches it
**What this rests on:** the opening of a class (pp. 9–10) and the breathing techniques (pp. 11–17) I read in full in the free extract. Everything from p. 18 on comes from one- or two-sentence search snippets, so the method descriptions below are assembled from fragments. A snippet near a method boundary may belong to the neighbouring method.

**Overall shape.** The book offers a menu of graded methods, not one fixed sequence like Satyananda's. A session has three parts. The first is a "mise en train" (warm-up) of letting go and breath awareness, which "precedes every session" (pp. 29, 55). The second is the method proper. The third is a gradual return. **Posture:** lying on the back on a folded blanket, preferably with a round cushion under the knees to ease the lower back, and a second blanket on top to keep warm (p. 9). Some breathing exercises can be done sitting or with the knees bent (p. 12). **Where it fits:** at home every morning on waking and every evening before sleep (p. 21). In a yoga class it comes mainly at the end, after the postures, with short relaxations at the start or between postures (p. 21). **Session length:** not stated in what I saw. Some timings are given: one or two minutes of listening to a bell (p. 39) and about twenty breaths per limb in "the circuit of awareness" (p. 86). Students are told not to talk or bustle for the first quarter hour after a session (p. 18).

**Worked example 1: opening a class (pp. 9–10, read in full; paraphrased).**
1. The student lies on their back, cushion under the knees, a blanket over them.
2. The teacher invites them to let the whole body drop to the floor like an object, then to release every muscle and nerve. (These are directive instructions, not pure observation.)
3. The face is released part by part: forehead, temples, around the eyes, cheeks, lips and jaw. The student then senses inside the head, simply noticing tensions there without effort. Boyes adds that this awareness alone dissolves them.
4. Shoulders, arms, hands, chest, abdomen, solar plexus, then hips and legs, aiming at complete letting go.
5. The student turns inward "as if falling asleep" and looks for a calm place inside. They let that calm spread through the head, throat, chest, solar plexus, arm and belly, without trying to grasp anything.
6. Breath: they observe the breath's movement and sound without directing it. Then comes an explicit imagined image, introduced with "Imaginez": being carried by the breath like a matchstick on the sea's waves, until the student is told they *are* the matchstick (p. 10). This continues until the mind "unites" with the breath's rhythm.
7. The teacher then moves straight into the chosen method.

**Worked example 2: "Sentir le corps" (sensing the body, method 3, pp. 31–33; assembled from snippets).**
1. The tour normally goes from the top of the body down to the feet. With headaches or tension in the head, it starts from the feet (p. 31).
2. Awareness goes into each part "directly", with no thought or mental image in between (p. 31). One version starts with the fingers of the right hand, one by one from the thumb (p. 31).
3. Palm, the inside of the wrist joint, then a slow, felt groping up the arm to the shoulder (p. 32).
4. Head: forehead, then the right eye and the round shape of the eyeball, then the same for the left eye (p. 32). Then the outer ear and the ear canal, explored as if by touch, then nose and lips (p. 33).
5. With an emotional student, sensing the inside of the head helps them step back from the emotions (p. 33).
6. The stated aim: by feeling the body so closely, the student comes to disidentify from it (p. 33).

**Pacing and voice.** In method 1, the teacher names each part and the student directs attention there. The sentences are "often punctuated by pauses and silences" (p. 29). The whole round is then repeated with a new verb each time: "relâchez" (release), "détendez" (relax), "relaxez" (let go), and a fourth (p. 30). In "Les sensations" the movement through the body is "very slow", with a long pause at each place (p. 66). Stage directions such as "pause prolongée" (long pause) recur from the second degree on (pp. 61–112). The teacher sometimes reminds the student to keep listening to the breath and to release further (pp. 65, 85, 93).

Notes on the table:
- **Rotation order.** The order in "Les sensations" (p. 66) is: right-hand fingers, palm, forearm, elbow, arm, shoulder, right hip, knee, leg, ankle, heel, sole, toes, then the same on the left, then forehead, eyes, nose, ears. It closely resembles the Bihar rotation skeleton (right side, then left side, then head and trunk). The difference is pace: Boyes moves very slowly, with long pauses, and asks for felt sensing rather than quick naming. This resemblance is a lead on a shared Indian source, not proof (see "Confidence").
- **Sankalpa.** Boyes rejects hypnotic suggestion (p. 8), and his heaviness and warmth methods tell the student to feel real sensation rather than suggest it (pp. 7, 55, 56). Yet he endorses repeated positive attitudes and self-images in deep relaxation as a way to change the subconscious (p. 19; cf. pp. 60, 108). So the 1973 book rejects hypnosis and Schultz-style formulas, not positive suggestion as such. The OCR of the free extract read cleanly, but a zero search result for "sankalpa" is still not proof of absence.
- **Counting.** The counted breath (p. 34) probably belongs to the rhythmic-breath method. Counting stops once the student starts to drop off; from then on they only listen. The blackboard methods (pp. 77–79) write the numbers 1 to 10 slowly in two lines of five and erase them with an imagined sponge, then go on from 11. Boyes says that erasing one's own numbers trains the ability to drop unwanted thoughts, fixed ideas and phobias (p. 79). The counting goes forward, not backward.
- **Imagery and suggestion.** The imagery methods include guide suggestions, for example the guide "suggests" that the student contemplate the immense blue space around them (p. 50).

**Sleep vs. awareness (1973).** Boyes prefers that students stay awake, so that the benefits are "registered" rather than left "floating" (p. 17). If sleep comes anyway, it should come through the method and not through wandering thoughts. Such sleep, he says, is better than ordinary sleep (pp. 17, 102). Only with great nervous fatigue is it desirable for the person to sleep (p. 17). Sleeping at the start is also allowed for people with insomnia or nervous over-excitement (p. 18). A student who begins to drop off is woken and told to just listen to the breath (p. 34). Sinking too far into inner emptiness without vigilance risks inertia and sleep (p. 54). The 1973 book already grounds this in consciousness continuing through waking, dream and deep sleep (pp. 22, 104). In 1984 he calls falling asleep the main obstacle once mental agitation is overcome, especially in panoramic attention (no. 16). This is the Māṇḍūkya frame the compendium places in the French milieu [@sauton1952].

**Teacher cautions and permissions (1973).** Don't over-insist on phrases like "relax completely", because they can provoke opposition (p. 19). By contrast, the same page allows the teacher to ask very relaxed students to take a positive attitude toward a problem and to see themselves effective in facing it, repeated until it impregnates the subconscious (p. 19; see "Core ideas"). Be wary of the images you use with a very impressionable or suggestible person (p. 37). Teachers must practise the techniques themselves to find the right words (pp. 9, 123). Avoid tape recordings in class (p. 19).

**Language style.** Directive and in the imperative: "Relâchez...", "Dirigez votre conscience...", "Sentez...". Ellipses mark pauses. The content is mostly observational ("simply observe", "without effort", "without thought or mental image"). There are no "my arm is heavy" formulas in what I saw. But imagery is used from the very first class: "Imaginez..." the matchstick on the waves (p. 10). The imagery methods openly suggest scenes with "Imaginez..." (pp. 40–42) and the guide "suggests" contemplating space (p. 50).

## What's distinctive
- **Date.** It is the only book-length yoga nidra text known in this project from before Satyananda's 1976 book. A Westerner wrote it in Paris, for Western classes.
- **A graded curriculum for teachers.** It has three "degrees", described as skin relaxation, muscular awareness and organic awareness (p. 4), plus an optional psychic-development part. The methods are written as teacher scripts with stage directions. It is a whole course in a pocket book.
- **Anatomical layers: the earliest and widest version.** Boyes has separate methods for skin, muscles, ribs, skeleton (vertebra by vertebra, p. 91), organs (heart, stomach, kidneys, p. 94) and blood circulation (p. 98). Some are combined with the pranic "regenerating" breath. Later books have parts of this. Satyananda 1976 adds an inner-organs round to one practice (Yoga Nidra 5, p. 122, per `satyananda1976.md`). Roecker uses bones, joints and organs as "healing energy" variants of a *nyāsa*-style rotation (`roecker.md`). Veda Bharati moves to "finer tissues, internal organs" after tense-and-release (`vedabharati2015.md`).
- **No sankalpa term, but a resolve-like practice in 1973, and a reversal by 1984.** The 1973 book contrasts Western suggestion with Eastern direct sensing (pp. 6–7), rejects hypnotic use (p. 8) and has no Schultz-style formulas. The OCR search found no "sankalpa", "résolution" or "formule". Yet it endorses asking deeply relaxed students to adopt a positive attitude toward a problem and to see themselves effective, repeated so that it impregnates the subconscious and replaces negative tendencies (p. 19; also pp. 60, 108). That is close in function to Satyananda's sankalpa, which belongs to the autosuggestion family [@birch2015; @coue1922]. Only in 1984 does Boyes reject suggesting a better future self, arguing that it sets up inner conflict, and keep only acceptance of what is present (no. 17). So 1973 to 1984 is a change of position, not continuity, and it anticipates today's "sankalpa optional" debate (`08` §8) from inside one teacher's career. The book also suggests scenes in the imagery methods (pp. 40–42, 50). Whether the heaviness, warmth and solar-plexus method uses any repeated formula can only be settled by reading pp. 56–58 in full; the snippets point the other way: the warmth is to be felt, not affirmed (pp. 55–56).
- **The witness at the centre, with a documented Advaita source.** "The spectator (*Turiya*)", emptiness, disidentification and *désobjectivation* come before Miller's non-dual framing. Boyes cites Jean Klein's *Sois ce que tu es* (p. 28). Klein was a teacher of Richard Miller [@miller2005], so this is a documented reading link between Boyes and the line that later produced iRest. Miller reportedly credits Boyes among the 20th-century people who "revitalised" yoga nidra (Wikipedia footnote citing Miller 2022; I could not see the passage).
- **A probable autogenic bridge.** The 1973 book names Schultz and contrasts itself with him (pp. 4, 6–7), yet includes a "heaviness, warmth, solar plexus" method. The German translation, almost certainly of this book, was sold as *Autogenes Yoga*. Its 1983 and 1985 editions are subtitled as a combination of yoga exercises with autogenic training (DNB records). That makes it the clearest documented bridge between yoga nidra and autogenic training in this project (`08` §10; `11` §11.1). The caveat is that the DNB records do not name the French original.
- **A rotation skeleton close to Bihar's, three years before Satyananda's book** (p. 66; see the table notes).

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Primary evidence for the history chapter: dated (1973), catalogued by the BnF, and searchable on Gallica, with a free extract of the opening pages.
  - Practical and classroom-tested. The dedication says it was built from students' reports of their experiences and difficulties. The book quotes student letters throughout (e.g. pp. 37, 59, 80–82, 115).
  - Unusually frank about what goes wrong, for an early book. Painful memories can surface in people with childhood trauma (p. 26). Widening awareness can briefly bring fear of death or a sense of being lost (p. 27). Persistent images can be watched like a film until they fade (p. 28). Some students simply seek sleep (p. 62). The 1984 series adds sleepiness and striving.
  - A sensation-first approach already in 1973: the book says it works mainly through sensing and feeling rather than ideas (p. 4; pp. 7, 31–32). The 1984 articles develop this further. It sits close to body-scan mindfulness and iRest-style observation. The book still mixes in imagery methods (p. 7).
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **No independent review found.** Wikipedia says the *Revue 3e Millénaire* was "reviewing Boyes's approach" in 1984. In fact the four 1984 pieces are Boyes's own articles, some with short editorial introductions (nos. 16 and 17). Don't cite them as a review.
  - **Wikipedia's history claims overreach.** It says the modern form was "pioneered by Dennis Boyes" (citing Boyes's book itself) and that this is "the first known usage" of yoga nidra in a modern sense (citing Birch & Hargreaves 2015). Birch & Hargreaves do not mention Boyes at all (I checked the essay). Boyes himself says he *learned* yoga nidra in India (p. 8). Earlier uses are also reported: Mishra 1959 (see `mishra1959.md`) and Satyananda's 1964 talk (see `satyananda1976.md`). I did not check either myself. So Boyes is the first known *book*, not the inventor.
  - **Esoteric and unsupported claims:** the triangle networks of "Masters" (p. 108); subtle bodies (pp. 23–24); left/right "negative/positive" character readings (pp. 28, 89). The 1984 series adds *prāṇa* in the *nāḍīs* as the cause of illness, the idea that fixing attention in the head, chest or belly breeds neurosis, a chain from breath to blood to cells to social structures, and "powers" of invisibility. These come from the author's esoteric interests (he also published *Yoga et ésotérisme*, 1973) and have no evidential basis.
  - **Period scientism.** He mentions the Lonavla institute's laboratory, where "scientific experiments" are done "to prove" the effects of yogic practices on the body. He then reports one of its "discoveries", that relaxation removes harmful toxins from the blood (p. 6), with no reference. This is the "scientific yoga" rhetoric that Alter describes [@alter2004], not evidence.
  - **A real internal tension.** The book argues for direct sensing over suggestion and images (pp. 4, 6–7). Yet it uses an "Imaginez" image in the very first class opening (p. 10), includes guided imagery journeys, colours and a guide who "suggests" scenes (pp. 40–51), and recommends repeated positive attitudes in deep relaxation to reshape the subconscious (p. 19). Boyes admits in 1973 that several methods use images and thought (p. 7), and he warns that images need care with very impressionable or suggestible people (p. 37). The 1984 Boyes is more purist: he rejects images and suggesting a better future self (no. 17) and emphasising heaviness (no. 14).
  - **Claims vs. evidence** (check against the compendium):
    - *Sleep quality.* "Waking sleep" is said to be better than ordinary disturbed sleep (pp. 17, 102). No hours-equivalence claim turned up (searches for "heures" and "équivaut" found nothing of the kind). See `06` §6.3: there is no evidence for sleep-substitute claims, so don't repeat this one.
    - *Health claims in the breathing chapter.* The complete breath is said to help prevent colds, bronchitis and pleurisy, to check asthma and to lower high blood pressure (p. 14). The regenerating breath is offered "to heal" illness (p. 17). Relaxation is said to clear "fatigue toxins" (pp. 6, 21, 43). None is referenced. Measure these against the modest baseline in `06` §6.0: yoga nidra beats doing nothing but is not clearly better than other relaxation. The blood-pressure studies there concern yoga nidra as a whole and are methodologically weak.
    - *Psychological claims.* Shyness and inferiority complexes fade by themselves (p. 22). The insomniac fears letting go of the "I", which is a fear of death (p. 19). Both are untested.
    - *Effects chapter (pp. 21–25).* Not read in full. The snippets show the claims listed above and subtle-body theory. Read it for further sleep or healing claims and check them against `06` §6.3.
    - *Sensation-first scanning.* Plausible through interoception (`06` §6.4). Compare Gibbs 2025, yoga nidra vs. body scan for pain, in `06` §6.2: both reduced pain, only yoga nidra raised well-being, and it was a quasi-experimental single session.
    - *"Striving causes agitation and insomnia"* (1984, no. 16). A teacher's observation, untested.
    - *Where he sits in the debates.* Stay awake, with a pragmatic exception for exhaustion and insomnia (`08` §3, the Satyananda and Swami Rama camp). A 42-method menu (`08` §7, the variable-scripts camp, though graded). Psychic development kept as an optional final part (`08` §4, close to "both"). Against hypnotic use (`08` §10).
  - **Reception.** *Later credit:* Dinsmore-Tuli & Tuli (2020) name Boyes 1973 as the first European yoga nidra book (p. 103, n. 18, per `dinsmoretuli2020.md`; I did not re-check the page). Miller 2022 credits him per a Wikipedia footnote. *Silence:* in the parts other project summaries could check, Bonnasse 2015 (English edition), Dumas 2024 and Sesma 2022 do not mention Boyes. *Germany:* *Autogenes Yoga* (1976) is the earliest yoga nidra-derived method book in German that this project found (the DNB checks summarised in `rammbonwitt1984.md` looked only for titles containing "nidra"). Ramm-Bonwitt 1984 is the first German book with "nidra" in its title (`rammbonwitt1984.md`). *Editions:* the 1983 French edition followed the 1980 French translation of Satyananda, *Yoga nidra: apprenez à dormir* (Éditions Satyanandashram; per `rammbonwitt1984.md`). Check whether its 16 extra pages respond to it. The 1976 German translation has 158 pp. at 21 cm against the French 127 pp. at 18 cm. Whether it adds material is unknown.

Cite the 1973 book and the 1984 articles as separate sources. The 1984 series is free online on the magazine's site. Suggested bibliography key: `boyes1984` (Boyes, "Aspects psychologiques et spirituels de la relaxation", *Revue 3e Millénaire* nos. 14–17, 1984).

- **Take:**
  - **A more honest origin paragraph** (1973, p. 8; back cover). Suggested framing: "The first book on yoga nidra as a relaxation method appeared not in India but in Paris: Dennis Boyes's *Le yoga du sommeil éveillé* (1973), three years before Satyananda's *Yoga Nidra*. Boyes, a British teacher born in Yorkshire, said he had learned the method in India, where he went in 1967." This complicates both stories ("Satyananda invented it" / "a Frenchman invented it"). It fits the compendium's "ancient word, modern method" line (`08` §1).
  - **One teacher's turn on the resolve** (1973, pp. 6–8, 19, 71; Boyes 1984, no. 17). Present it as a reversal, not a precedent from the start. Boyes 1973 rejects hypnotic use and Schultz-style formulas, yet endorses repeated positive attitudes and self-images in deep relaxation (p. 19), while also teaching impartial observation of a problem (p. 71). Boyes 1984 drops the first: a resolve aimed at a better future self sets up inner conflict, while accepting what is present lets change happen by itself. If your book treats the resolve as optional, he is historical support next to Dinsmore-Tuli's "optional" position and Miller's "welcoming", but only his 1984 self. Note honestly that his 1973 methods use positive suggestion (p. 19) and suggested scenes (pp. 40–42, 50).
  - **A slow, felt rotation** (1973, pp. 29–33, 66). Name the part, pause, and ask for sensation without picture or thought. It is a useful contrast to fast naming. His tip to start from the feet when someone has a headache is practical (p. 31).
  - **Repeating a round with a new verb** each time ("release... relax... let go...") (1973, p. 30). It is a gentle way to deepen without new content.
  - **Setup details** (1973, p. 9): a cushion under the knees and a second blanket for warmth.
  - **The penetrating/panoramic distinction** (Boyes 1984, no. 16) as a teaching tool. It explains why students drop off when the instruction widens to "the whole body". Script deliberate shifts between focused and wide attention, with a cue to stay alert.
  - **The return sequence of hearing, then touch, then sight** (Boyes 1984, no. 16), and the 1973 cat-like stretches with a quiet quarter hour afterwards (pp. 17–18), as gentle ways to end.
  - **A graded structure** (beginner, intermediate, advanced) if your book will offer progression instead of one script (1973, ToC and p. 4). Boyes is a 1973 precedent for a "levels" design.
  - **Anatomical-layer scans** (skin, bones, organs, circulation) as variations for experienced practitioners (1973, ToC; pp. 90–100).
  - **Honest "what can happen" notes** for your safety chapter (1973, pp. 26–28): memories surfacing, brief fear or disorientation, persistent images. Pair them with `07`.
  - **The autogenic connection** (1973, pp. 4, 6–7, 56; DNB records for *Autogenes Yoga*), for your Western-roots section and for German-speaking readers. Say that the German book is "almost certainly" the translation.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - Don't call Boyes French: the BnF records him as British, and the back cover says he was born in Yorkshire; he taught in Paris. Don't call him the "inventor" or "pioneer" of yoga nidra either. Don't cite Wikipedia's "first known usage" sentence or call the 1984 articles a "review" (Wikipedia; Boyes 1984).
  - The body-scan sample on Wikipedia (sourced there to Godefroy's PDF) does match Boyes's own wording on p. 32: forehead, right eye, the eyeball's round shape, "without thought or mental image". The phrases I searched all appear on that page, though I saw them as search hits, not as continuous text. If you quote it, cite Boyes 1973, p. 32, not Wikipedia or Godefroy.
  - Leave out the esoteric material, or present it only as period colour: Masters and triangles (1973, p. 108), subtle bodies (pp. 23–24), left/right character readings (pp. 28, 89), and from 1984 invisibility, the *nāḍī* theory of illness and neurosis from "fixed" attention.
  - Don't repeat the health and sleep claims (1973, pp. 6, 14, 17, 22, 102). See "Claims vs. evidence".
  - Treat the idea that unconscious emotions lodge in unconscious body parts (Boyes 1984, no. 16) as Boyes's hypothesis, not a finding. If you use it, frame it as one teacher's image for why we scan the whole body.
  - Don't present him as part of a lineage. No teacher is named in what I saw. The Sivananda connection (his 1973 translation), Lonavla and the Bihar-like rotation order are leads, not proof that he learned from the Bihar School.
