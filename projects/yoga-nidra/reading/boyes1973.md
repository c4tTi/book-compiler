## In one paragraph
This slim French manual from 1973 is the earliest book-length treatment of a relaxation method called "yoga nidra" that I know of, three years before Satyananda's *Yoga Nidra* (1976). Its author, Dennis Boyes (born 1939), was British-born. According to the BnF authority record, he worked with children with special needs in Rudolf Steiner schools before teaching yoga in Paris, and he was also a painter, poet and playwright. The book is for ordinary students in a yoga or relaxation class. It promises deep relaxation in which the body rests completely and awareness stays alert. Through that relaxation it also promises a route into meditation, the inner "spectator" (*turīya*) and, in the last part, "psychic development". It is not one script. After an introduction on why relaxation is needed, how Western and Eastern methods differ, and four breathing techniques, it gives 42 methods. They are graded in three "degrees" of difficulty, plus a final part on psychic development. The methods move from simple letting-go and listening to the breath, through tours of the skin, muscles, skeleton, organs and blood circulation, to emptiness, disidentification and visualisation. Boyes dedicates the book to his students, whom he also counts as his teachers, and says their reports from relaxation classes helped him write it (dedication page). The book grew out of a Paris classroom, not an ashram.

## Core ideas
- **Yoga nidra is learned in India and adapted in the West.** The methods derive from a method called "yoga nidra" that the author says he learned and practised in India. He then developed variations by trying it out in his own teaching (p. 8; the snippet breaks off there). In India, he adds, the practice aims to open a person to higher levels of the psyche and to widen the inner life until it touches the infinite (p. 8).
- **Definition.** *Nidra* is "yogic sleep": total muscular, nervous and mental relaxation with awareness kept alert (back cover). This is the "waking sleep" of the title.
- **The body is experienced as sensation.** In the 1984 series, Boyes says practice moves from coarse sensations (heaviness, numbness, opacity), through a network of small tensions, to fine ones (currents, tingling, vibration). It ends in a felt emptiness (*le vide*), where relaxation turns by itself into meditation. The ToC of the 1973 book follows a similar gross-to-subtle arc: first degree to third degree, then emptiness and disidentification.
- **Sense directly, without images or words.** Notice sensations without calling them good or bad and without verbalising them (1984, no. 15). Images and concepts strengthen identification with "name and form" (1984, no. 17).
- **Against suggestion.** Western relaxation methods, he argues, rely on suggestion, and authentic Eastern ones do not. Suggesting a future, better self ("I will be courageous") sets up a conflict with what is, and only polishes the ego. Yogic practice accepts the problem and looks at it until it changes by itself (1984, no. 17). This amounts to an argument against a sankalpa-style resolve, though he does not use the word.
- **Why tour the whole body.** Feelings rising from the unconscious (sudden heat, cold, tension, headaches) settle in parts of the body that are not yet conscious. Making every part conscious lets such feelings be noticed and released instead of lodging there (1984, no. 16). This rationale is psychological, not tantric (no *nyāsa*).
- **Two kinds of attention.** "Penetrating" attention, focused on one area (he compares it to Theravada practice), sharpens and calms the mind. "Panoramic" attention, open to the whole body and surroundings, is higher, but it is where students tend to fall asleep. The skill is to move from one to the other without losing clarity (1984, no. 16).
- **Wanting results is an obstacle.** Practising in order to get something creates subtle resistance and can even cause agitation and insomnia (1984, no. 16).
- **Esoteric horizon.** The final part links relaxation to "psychic development". The snippet I saw from the "Triangles" method (p. 108) speaks of triangular networks through which spiritual Masters might make their presence felt in our world. The 1984 series also has notes on *prāṇa* in the *nāḍīs*, on illness as slowed *prāṇa*, and on "invisibility".

## The practice as this book teaches it
**Caution:** I saw the ToC and short snippets, not the method pages. The stage mapping below combines the ToC (what is in the book), the back cover and p. 8, and Boyes's own 1984 description of his method. The 1984 views come ten years later, after the 1983 enlarged edition, so they may not match 1973 exactly.

**Overall shape.** The book offers a menu of graded methods, each presumably a session in itself, not one fixed sequence like Satyananda's. The intended path runs from the first degree to the third, then on to the psychic-development methods. Posture is lying down (the German subtitle says "im Liegen"). The ToC shows preparatory exercises and breathing techniques before the methods. Session length is unknown.

**Sleep vs. awareness.** Boyes wants awareness to stay alert (back cover). In 1984 he names falling asleep as the main obstacle once mental agitation is overcome, especially in wide, panoramic attention. He grounds this in the idea that consciousness continues through waking, dream and deep sleep, and that silent observation is what makes it possible to stay awake even in sleep. This is the Māṇḍūkya frame that the compendium already places in the French milieu [@sauton1952].

**Language style.** The one sample available (Wikipedia, via Godefroy, unverified) is directive and in the imperative ("direct your attention... feel... go down..."). It is slow, with pauses, and asks for sensing rather than picturing. It is directive in form, but the content is observational, not suggestive. There are no formulas such as "my arm is heavy" in the sample.

## What's distinctive
- **Date.** It is the only book-length yoga nidra text we know from before Satyananda's 1976 book, and it was written in Paris by a Westerner for Western students.
- **A graded curriculum, not one script.** Three "degrees" plus a psychic-development part. It is a whole course in a pocket book.
- **Anatomical layers.** Separate awareness practices for the skin, muscles, ribs, skeleton, organs and blood circulation. I have not seen these as a set in other yoga nidra books.
- **No resolve, and an explicit argument against suggestion** (by 1984). Satyananda's sankalpa comes from the autosuggestion family [@birch2015; @coue1922]. Boyes goes the other way, half a century before today's "sankalpa optional" debate (`08` §8).
- **The witness at the centre.** "The spectator (*Turiya*)", emptiness and disidentification come before Miller's non-dual framing. Miller reportedly credits Boyes among the 20th-century people who "revitalised" yoga nidra (Wikipedia footnote citing Miller 2022; I could not see the passage).
- **The autogenic link in plain sight.** The ToC has a "heaviness, warmth, solar plexus" method, and the German publisher marketed the book as *Autogenes Yoga*. Later German editions describe it as a combination of yoga exercises with autogenic training (DNB record). It is the clearest documented bridge between yoga nidra and autogenic training in this project (`08` §10; `11` §11.1).

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Primary evidence for the history chapter: dated (1973), catalogued by the BnF, and free to read.
  - Practical and classroom-tested. The dedication says it was built from students' reports of their experiences and difficulties.
  - It has a chapter on difficulties and experiences (p. 26), and the 1984 series addresses sleepiness, striving and strong sensations surfacing. Few early books discuss what goes wrong.
  - Its non-suggestive, sensation-first approach looks very current: close to body-scan mindfulness and iRest-style "welcoming".
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **No independent review found.** Wikipedia says the *Revue 3e Millénaire* was "reviewing Boyes's approach" in 1984. In fact the four 1984 pieces are Boyes's own articles, with short editorial introductions. Don't cite them as a review.
  - **Wikipedia's history claims overreach.** It says the modern form was "pioneered by Dennis Boyes" and that this is "the first known usage" of yoga nidra in the modern sense, citing Birch & Hargreaves (2015). But Birch & Hargreaves do not mention Boyes at all (I checked the essay). And Boyes himself says he *learned* yoga nidra in India. Earlier uses exist too: Mishra 1959 [@mishra1959] 🔎 and Satyananda's 1964 talk [@satyananda1965] 🔎. So Boyes is the first *book*, not the inventor.
  - **Esoteric and unsupported claims:** the triangle networks of "Masters", *prāṇa* in the *nāḍīs* as the cause of illness, the idea that fixing attention in the head, chest or belly breeds neurosis, a chain from breath to blood to cells to social structures, and "powers" of invisibility (1984 series). These come from the author's esoteric interests (he also published *Yoga et ésotérisme*, 1973) and have no evidential basis.
  - **Period scientism.** He mentions the Lonavla institute's laboratory as proving yoga's effects on the body (p. 6). This is the "scientific yoga" rhetoric that Alter describes [@alter2004], not evidence.
  - **Possible internal tension.** The 1973 ToC includes heaviness, warmth, colour and visualisation methods, while the 1984 Boyes rejects emphasising heaviness and using images. Either the book frames these as observation, or his position changed. Unresolved.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **A more honest origin paragraph.** Suggested framing: "The first book on yoga nidra as a relaxation method appeared not in India but in Paris: Dennis Boyes's *Le yoga du sommeil éveillé* (1973), three years before Satyananda's *Yoga Nidra*. Boyes, a British-born teacher, said he had learned the method in India, where he practised from 1967." This complicates both stories ("Satyananda invented it" / "a Frenchman invented it"). It shows the method crystallising among several teachers between the 1950s and the 1970s, which fits the compendium's "ancient word, modern method" line (`08` §1).
  - **A precedent for making sankalpa optional.** If your book treats the resolve as optional, Boyes's 1984 argument is a strong early ally: a resolve aimed at a future self can set up inner conflict, whereas full acceptance of what is present lets change happen by itself. Use it as historical support next to Dinsmore-Tuli's "optional" position and Miller's "welcoming".
  - **The penetrating/panoramic distinction** as a teaching tool. It explains why students drop off when the instruction widens to "the whole body". You can script deliberate shifts between focused and wide attention, with a cue to keep alert.
  - **The return sequence** of hearing, then touch, then sight, as a gentle, sensory way to end a practice.
  - **A graded structure** (beginner, intermediate, advanced) if your book will offer progression instead of one script. Boyes is a 1973 precedent for a "levels" design.
  - **Anatomical-layer scans** (skin, bones, organs, circulation) as variations for experienced practitioners.
  - **The autogenic connection**, documented through the German title *Autogenes Yoga* and the "heaviness, warmth, solar plexus" method. Use it in your Western-roots section and for German-speaking readers.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - Don't call Boyes French (he was British-born and taught in Paris), the "inventor", or the "pioneer" of yoga nidra. Don't cite Wikipedia's "first known usage" sentence or call the 1984 articles a "review".
  - Don't quote the body-scan sample circulating on Wikipedia as Boyes's words until you have found it in the book. Its source is a third-party self-help PDF.
  - Leave out the esoteric material (Masters, triangles, invisibility, *nāḍī* theory of illness, neurosis from "fixed" attention) or present it only as period colour.
  - Treat the "unconscious emotions lodge in unconscious body parts" rationale as Boyes's hypothesis, not a finding. If you use it, frame it as one teacher's image for why we scan the whole body.
  - Don't present him as part of a lineage. No teacher is named in what I saw. The Sivananda connection (his 1973 translation) and Lonavla are leads, not proof that he learned from the Bihar School.
