## In one paragraph
This is the mass-market German introduction to yoga nidra. It is a slim, photo-led GU guide for stressed lay readers (the blurb opens with an overflowing desk, children to collect and a tax return). It was written by one of Germany's best-known yoga teachers and yoga authors, and it has run to at least nine printings. Its promise, from the publisher, is that "yogic sleep" brings the mind into deep rest in about 30 minutes, relaxes body and soul and restores energy. The book has three parts. A short theory part covers Satyananda's origin story and the tantric idea of *nyāsa*, then explains stress through the stress response, the autonomic nervous system, the vagus nerve, the gut's own nervous system and the heart. A practical part follows with gentle asanas to wind down and five relaxation postures with props. The last part explains "the ten phases of yoga nidra" and gives a basic form, a short form and six swappable "inner journeys" for the guided-meditation slot. The author's own voice on the CD guides both forms. The back cover lists the outcomes it promises, including "demonstrably strengthening the immune system".

## Core ideas
- **Yoga nidra is deep relaxation with inner awareness.** *Nidrā* means sleep and sleep-like states, especially the threshold between waking and sleeping. Satyananda's own glosses ("sleepless sleep", "conscious, dynamic or psychic sleep") are quoted (p. 6).
- **The Satyananda origin story, told as history.** Satyananda (1923–2009) "developed" the method and brought it to the West from the mid-1970s. A photo caption says he "rediscovered the old technique", and a section heading calls it "one of the oldest relaxation methods". She retells the night-watchman story: he slept in a Sanskrit school while the boys chanted, later knew the chants without having consciously learned them, and Sivananda explained that the subtle body can hear and remember. She adds that he then "studied biology and medicine" and searched the yoga texts (pp. 6–7).
- **Nyāsa as the source.** Tantric masters touched points on the pupil's body in a fixed order while speaking a mantra. Satyananda noticed that rapid, regular circling of attention through the body energised the body and relaxed the mind deeply. This became the rotation (p. 7).
- **Relaxation is a nervous-system event, and every stage has a target.** A box on p. 27 lists ten elements of "every yoga nidra sequence" and assigns each a route to vagal tone. The quiet setting; deliberately tuning in to relaxation and the positive resolve (both "via the frontal brain"); attention wandering through the body and "sensation training" (via mindfulness); calm, deep breathing (directly on the vagus). Feeling pairs of opposites works via the "middle, 'neutral' part of the frontal brain". The rapid image sequence "neutralises" the limbic system so it "fires" less intensely. Visualisations strengthen the "positively" oriented left frontal part. Guided meditations stabilise the calm baseline and stimulate the brain's reward system.
- **The gut and the heart.** Stress halts digestion and stiffens the upper abdomen so the diaphragm cannot descend. Deep, calm breathing activates the vagus, moves the gut and puts you in touch with your "gut feeling". The "gut brain" has over 100 million nerve cells and more fibres running up to the head than down. She concludes it "has the say" (pp. 26–27). A section on "relaxing from the heart" follows (p. 29; not seen).
- **Total relaxation may not be the goal.** The headings ask whether we can be completely relaxed and offer an "active route to *Wohlspannung*" (p. 19–21; not seen). The word suggests a healthy, pleasant tone rather than slackness, close to the German eutony and body-therapy vocabulary.
- **Mindfulness and body awareness as a "sixth sense".** The opening phase trains *Achtsamkeit* so that the body becomes a "safe harbour". Grounding in the body is "even more important" when automatic brain patterns run, as in depression, anxiety disorders, strong inner restlessness and "all forms of PTSD" (pp. 54–55).
- **Sankalpa as affirmation.** One sentence, clear, precise and in the present tense, "planted" in the mind by steady repetition "in the sense of an affirmation" (p. 55).
- **Comfort before form.** You choose the posture you can stay still in for at least 30 minutes, change it if today it doesn't suit, and may even practise seated (pp. 49, 54–55).

## The practice as this book teaches it
I saw only the start of the ten-phase chapter and none of the two scripts. The table marks what I saw and what is inferred. Compare with Satyananda's eight stages in `04` §4.1.

**Before the practice (book, pp. 36–51; seen in part):** optional asanas to wind down, built on lengthening the exhalation, gentle stretches, twists, forward bends, down dog and shoulder stand. Then you pick one of five relaxation postures: corpse pose or alternatives; back-relief (supine, bolster or "jumbo cushion" under the calves so they lie horizontal, optional heat pad, at least 15 minutes or a whole yoga nidra); prone (legs slightly apart, head turned or forehead on stacked hands; not in advanced pregnancy or with acute abdominal inflammation; she explicitly allows yoga nidra in this position if you breathe better); supine with cushions and weights; supine with chest opening. The back-relief instructions add imagery for the start of yoga nidra: picture the back muscles letting go with each breath and the painful pressure on the spinal joints easing. They give an exit for the end: push the bolster away with a foot, lie on your side for a few breaths, stretch luxuriously, then sit up.

The p. 27 box lists exactly ten elements, but they are probably **not** the same as "the ten phases" on pp. 54–58. The box begins with "a quiet environment", which is a condition rather than a phase, and it has no ending and no second sankalpa. Read the two lists together only as evidence of the order: settling and mindfulness, sankalpa, rotation, breath, sensations, opposites, rapid images, visualisation, guided meditation.

**Forms and lengths.** The basic form (*Grundform*, "Yoga Nidra 1", pp. 59–65) presumably runs about 30 minutes (the publisher's figure and the 30-minute posture rule). The short form (*Kurzform*, "Yoga Nidra 2", pp. 66–69) has an unknown length. The six inner journeys are timed individually: the river about 8 minutes, the self-care journey about 5. The back cover describes the CD as the basic form, a short form "and options for individual sequences". The DNB blurb says the CD has the two yoga nidra versions and two meditations. This suggests the CD is built so you can combine tracks, but I have not seen a track list.

**The inner journeys (two seen, paraphrased).**
- *Being in the river of life* (about 8 min). You picture a river that is your life and sit on its bank. Its water is clear in places and murky in others; the murky stretches stand for what clouds or worries you, and you trust the clear water to clear them in time. It runs fast over shallows and rocks, which take attention and skill, and sometimes drops over a waterfall, which takes trust as well. You find a calm stretch, immerse yourself, feel the water's life and energy, and let it carry you towards the ocean from which life came.
- *Giving yourself love and care* (about 5 min). You see yourself lying on the mat, sit down beside yourself in your mind and look at yourself kindly, as a mother looks at her child. You acknowledge your effort, persistence and creativity, promise to look after yourself, and are told this is not selfishness. A tip recommends it before sleep. The shorter journeys can be memorised and used as breaks during the day.
- The other four by title only: the sacred mountain, connecting with expanse and space, expanse and relaxation in the heart space, meeting your inner knowing.

**Sleep.** A sidebar, "Do you always fall asleep straight away?" (p. 55), is kind and practical. Some people fall asleep as soon as they lie down. Although they take in a lot of the instructions "on an unconscious level", they are often annoyed at missing something. Her fix is to sit, leaning against a wall, padded with cushions and blankets, which she says clearly lowers the risk of dozing. She notes that this is not part of classical yoga nidra.

**Voice and language.** Formal *Sie*, imperative but soft ("Stellen Sie sich vor…", "Spüren Sie…"). The mind "may" go outward; attention is "invited" to the thumb. There is no "I am practising yoga nidra" formula in what I saw. The register sits between Satyananda's directive style and the invitational style of `07` §7.4: a fixed structure, gently worded. One Thalia customer (3 stars, 2019) found the CD voice **too fast**, saying it left them feeling "almost a bit rushed". A 5-star reviewer (2016) found yoga nidra easier than progressive muscle relaxation. Those two reviews are the only reception data I could see.

## What's distinctive
- **The German mainstream version.** This is the book that put Satyananda's structure into the general German bookshop, translated into the German relaxation vocabulary of *Tiefenentspannung*, *Achtsamkeit*, *vegetatives Nervensystem* and *Wohlspannung*. It was published by Germany's leading self-help imprint and written by a BDY teacher-trainer rather than a lineage swami. No other book on your list does this for the German market.
- **A stage-by-stage nervous-system rationale.** Desai explains the stages through brainwave states. Trökes maps each element to the vagus, frontal lobe, limbic system or reward system (p. 27). It is the most explicit "each stage has a physiological job" scheme I have seen in this set, and also the least supported (see below).
- **An asana and posture chapter before the practice.** Five relaxation postures with props (including prone, back-relief with heat, and weights), an exit routine for each, and a short wind-down sequence. Satyananda's 6th edition has "General Suggestions" (p. 74) but no asana or posture chapter in its table of contents.
- **Comfort over orthodoxy, said openly.** She lets readers choose prone or seated positions, and says plainly where this departs from "classical" yoga nidra. That is rare in a book that otherwise follows Satyananda's sequence.
- **A swappable guided-meditation slot** filled with resource and self-compassion themes (heart space, inner knowing, river of life, self-love) rather than Satyananda's rapid images and chakra work. In structure it is closer to the practice menus of later English-language books such as Dinsmore-Tuli's.
- **A back-pain angle.** The author is a certified back-school instructor. The back-relief posture with pain-easing imagery is a small, practical bridge to the pain research in `06` §6.2.

## Strengths and limitations
- Strengths:
  - **Short, clear, usable.** A complete home-practice package (book plus a 70-minute CD in the author's own voice), with a basic and a short form for different days.
  - **Excellent practical detail on posture and props.** Concrete instructions for setting up, staying still for 15–30 minutes, warmth and getting up again. Contraindications are noted for the prone position.
  - **Non-shaming sleep advice** with a simple remedy (sit up), honestly labelled as non-classical.
  - **A grounding rationale for the opening phase** (contact points, here and now, "safe in a room"). This is close to how trauma-informed teachers now explain the start of practice (`07` §7.2).
  - **Credible author.** She has taught since 1974, trained BDY teachers since 1984, teaches voice and speech for yoga teachers, and the book has reached at least nine printings.
- Limitations / critiques (no serious review exists that I could find; these are my own checks against the compendium):
  - **Health claims beyond the evidence.** The back cover promises to "demonstrably strengthen the immune system" and "the latest scientific findings". The compendium has no yoga nidra study on immune function. The 2025–26 meta-analyses find small to moderate effects from weak studies, no clear advantage over other relaxation, and very small effects in the best German RCT (`06` §6.0). The "deep rest in only 30 minutes" promise is mild by comparison. In the pages I saw there is **no** "one hour equals four hours of sleep" claim and no brainwave claim. The compendium's `11` lumps this book with the sleep-substitute and brainwave titles, which looks inaccurate for Trökes and should be revised.
  - **Speculative neuro-mapping.** The p. 27 box assigns brain regions to stages: opposites to a "neutral" middle frontal area, rapid images to "neutralising" the limbic system, visualisation to the "positive" left frontal region, guided meditation to the reward system. None of this has been tested for yoga nidra. It belongs with the homunculus and "reprograms the subconscious" claims in `06` §6.3. Only the general claim, that slow breathing and relaxation shift the body towards parasympathetic activity, has reasonable support (`06` §6.4).
  - **The gut-brain passage overreaches.** That more vagal fibres run from gut to brain than the reverse is broadly in line with physiology. The conclusion that the "gut brain" therefore "has the say" is rhetoric, not science.
  - **History told as legend.** The night-watchman story, "rediscovered the old technique" and "one of the oldest relaxation methods" repeat the lineage narrative without the historians' caution (`08` §1; Birch & Hargreaves 2015; Singleton 2005). The claim that Satyananda "studied biology and medicine" has no source given, and I have not seen it in his own account. Check it before repeating it. The book appeared in 2014, before the Royal Commission hearing and report on the Satyananda ashram (`08` §6), so its silence there is a matter of timing. A teacher citing it today should add that context.
  - **Sankalpa as affirmation** imports self-help autosuggestion into the resolve without discussing it. The "I trust my body's self-healing powers" example sits close to the hypnosis question (`08` §10; `03` §3.12).
  - **PTSD named, safety missing (in the pages seen).** The opening phase is said to matter especially for depression, anxiety disorders and "all forms of PTSD", but I saw no cautions about trauma, dissociation or the pain/pleasure opposites (`07` §7.1). Check pp. 31–33 ("Jeder kann Yoga & Yoga Nidra üben") before concluding they are absent.
  - **A shoulder stand in a relaxation book for stressed beginners** needs clear cautions. I did not see p. 46.
  - **Death imagery in the posture name** ("Totenhaltung", corpse pose), which `07` §7.2 suggests softening for some groups. This is a minor point and standard German usage.
  - **Thin as a source.** At 80 photo-heavy pages, the theory is brief and the scripts are short. There are no references in the pages I saw, apart from a "books and addresses" page.
  - **Reception is barely documented.** No journal, magazine or long-form blog review turned up; the only reviews I could see are two customer reviews on Thalia. Amazon and LovelyBooks pages could not be read.

## For your own book
- Take:
  - **The posture-and-props chapter as a model.** Five named positions, each with set-up, a "how long" note, a contraindication line and an exit routine. Cite Trökes as a mainstream precedent for "comfort outranks form" (`05` Posture & props).
  - **The seated remedy for habitual sleepers**, offered without shame and labelled honestly as non-classical. This is a good template for your position on `08` §3. Drop or flag her claim that sleepers take in the instructions unconsciously; it is lineage belief, not evidence.
  - **The grounding rationale for the opening phase** (contact points bring you back to here and now and to the fact that you are safe in this room). Pair it with choice and exits (`07` §7.2), which she does not supply in the pages I saw.
  - **The modular design:** a basic form, a short form, and a swappable "guided meditation" slot with timed alternatives (5–8 min). It works well if your book comes with audio. Her themes (mountain, space, heart, inner knowing, river, self-care) are a ready list to compare with your own.
  - **The river-of-life image** is a gentle, non-trauma-heavy visualisation. Write your own version rather than adapting hers closely.
  - **A short "wind-down before you lie down" sequence** built on lengthening the exhalation. It is a reasonable on-ramp for restless readers, and easy to justify.
  - **German vocabulary** if you write for, or about, the German market: *Tiefenentspannung*, *Achtsamkeit*, *Wohlspannung* and *vegetatives Nervensystem* are the words German readers will expect (`11`).
- Skip or treat critically:
  - **"Demonstrably strengthens the immune system"** and "latest scientific findings": don't repeat them. Use the modest summary from `06`.
