**Tradition / lineage:** Satyananda-derived yoga nidra taught inside mainstream German hatha yoga (BDY). Anna Trökes (b. 1952; GND 122379047; "Troekes" in ASCII) has taught since 1974 and ran her own school in Berlin from 1977 to 2011. She has taught in BDY teacher training since 1983 and is a Heilpraktikerin (licensed non-medical practitioner) and back-school instructor. Her formative teachers, per her vita and two press portraits, were Gérard Blitz and T. K. V. Desikachar, Roger Clerc and Boris Tatzky (*Yoga der Energie*), and Patrick Tomatis. For yoga nidra specifically, the book's back cover says she learned it personally from Swami Satyananda Saraswati in the 1980s. In the sources I checked, that link appears **only in this book's author note**; her online vita and the two portraits do not mention Satyananda. The first chapter (p. 6) opens with an epigraph from Swami Prakashananda Saraswati, head of the Satyananda Ashram in Germany.

## In one paragraph
This is a mass-market German introduction to yoga nidra: a slim, photo-led GU guide for stressed lay readers (the blurb opens with an overflowing desk, children to collect and a tax return), written by one of Germany's best-known yoga teachers and authors. It has run to at least nine printings. The publisher's promise is that "yogic sleep" brings the mind into deep rest in only 30 minutes, relaxes body and soul and restores energy. The book has three parts. A short theory part covers Satyananda's origin story and the tantric idea of *nyāsa*, then explains stress through the stress response, the autonomic nervous system, the vagus nerve, the gut's own nervous system and the heart. A practical part follows with gentle asanas to wind down (from lengthening the exhalation to a shoulder stand) and five relaxation postures with props. The last part explains "the ten phases of yoga nidra" and gives a basic form, a short form and six swappable inner journeys for the guided-meditation slot. The author's own voice on the CD guides both forms. The back cover's list of outcomes includes "Das Immunsystem nachweislich stärken" (demonstrably strengthening the immune system).

## Core ideas
- **Yoga nidra is deep relaxation with inner awareness.** *Nidrā* means sleep and sleep-like states, especially the threshold between waking and sleeping. Satyananda's own glosses (sleepless sleep; conscious, dynamic or psychic sleep) are cited (p. 6).
- **The Satyananda origin story, told as history.** Satyananda (1923–2009) developed the method and brought it to the West from the mid-1970s. A photo caption credits him with rediscovering an old technique, and a section heading calls it one of the oldest relaxation methods. She retells the night-watchman story: he slept in a Sanskrit school while the boys chanted, later knew the chants without having consciously heard them, and Sivananda explained that the subtle body can hear and remember. She adds that he then studied biology and medicine and searched the yoga texts (pp. 6–7).
- **Nyāsa as the source.** Tantric masters touched points on the pupil's body in a fixed order while speaking a mantra. Satyananda, she says, experienced his own body as highly energised and his mind as deeply relaxed when attention circled quickly through the body. This became the rotation (p. 7).
- **Relaxation is a nervous-system event, and most stages have a target.** A box on p. 27 lists ten elements of every yoga nidra sequence and says all of them act positively on vagal tone. It assigns most of them a route (the first, the quiet setting, has none): tuning in to relaxation and the positive resolve act via the frontal brain; attention wandering through the body and sensation training via mindfulness; calm, deep breathing directly on the vagus; pairs of opposites via the middle, "neutral" part of the frontal brain; the rapid image sequence "neutralises" the limbic system so it fires less abruptly; visualisations strengthen the "positively" oriented left frontal part; guided meditations stabilise the calm baseline and so stimulate the brain's reward system.
- **The gut and the heart.** Stress halts digestion and stiffens the upper abdomen so the diaphragm cannot descend. Deep, calm breathing activates the vagus, moves the gut and puts you in touch with your gut feeling. The "gut brain" has over 100 million nerve cells, and more nerve pathways run from belly to head than from brain to gut; she concludes that the gut brain is in charge (pp. 26–27). A section on relaxing from the heart follows (p. 29; not seen).
- **Total relaxation may not be the goal.** The headings ask whether we can be completely relaxed and offer an active route to *Wohlspannung* (pp. 19–21; not seen). The word suggests a healthy, pleasant tone rather than slackness, close to German eutony and body-therapy vocabulary.
- **Mindfulness and body awareness as a sixth sense.** The opening phase trains *Achtsamkeit* so that the body becomes a safe harbour. Grounding in the body matters even more when automatic brain patterns run, as in depression, anxiety disorders, strong inner restlessness and "all forms of PTSD" (pp. 54–55).
- **Sankalpa as affirmation.** One sentence, clear, precise and in the present tense, planted in the mind by steady repetition in the manner of an affirmation (p. 55).
- **Comfort before form.** You choose the posture you can stay still in for at least 30 minutes, change it if today it doesn't suit, and may even practise seated (pp. 49, 54–55).

## The practice as this book teaches it
I saw only the start of the ten-phase chapter and none of the two scripts. The table marks what I saw and what is inferred. Compare with Satyananda's eight stages in `04` §4.1.

**Before the practice (book, pp. 36–51; seen in part):** optional asanas to wind down, built on lengthening the exhalation, gentle stretches, twists, forward bends, a stretch titled "the dog that stretches" (p. 45, not seen, so I can't say which dog variant it is) and shoulder stand. Then you pick one of five relaxation postures. By title only (not seen): corpse pose or alternatives (p. 47); supine with cushions and weights (p. 50); supine with chest opening (p. 51). Seen: **back-relief** (p. 48: supine, bolster or "jumbo cushion" under the calves so they lie horizontal, optional heat pad, stay at least 15 minutes or a whole yoga nidra) and **prone** (p. 49: legs slightly apart, head turned or forehead on stacked hands, stay a few minutes; not in advanced pregnancy or with acute abdominal inflammation; she explicitly allows yoga nidra in this position if you breathe better). The back-relief instructions add imagery for the start of yoga nidra (back muscles letting go with each breath, painful pressure on the spinal joints easing) and an exit for the end (push the bolster away with a foot, lie on your side for a few breaths, stretch, then sit up). The prone position also ends with an exit (stretch, come up to sitting over the side).

The p. 27 box lists exactly ten elements, but they are probably **not** the same as "the ten phases" on pp. 54–58. The box begins with the quiet environment, which is a condition rather than a phase, and it has no ending and no second sankalpa. Read the two lists together only as evidence of the order: settling and mindfulness, sankalpa, rotation, breath, sensations, opposites, rapid images, visualisation, guided meditation.

**Forms and lengths.** The basic form (*Grundform*, "Yoga Nidra 1", pp. 59–65) presumably runs about 30 minutes (the publisher's figure and the 30-minute posture rule). The short form (*Kurzform*, "Yoga Nidra 2", pp. 66–69): length not seen in the book. Thalia's listing, as returned by a web-fetch tool (I could not load the raw page), has a contents line "2 Abläufe: Yoga Nidra (ca. 15 Min) & (ca. 30 Min)", which would make the short form about 15 minutes; treat that as unverified. Of the six inner journeys, the two I saw carry individual timings (about 8 and about 5 minutes); the other four were not seen. The back cover describes the CD as the basic form, a short form and options for individual sequences. The DNB blurb says the CD has the two yoga nidra versions and two meditations. This suggests the CD is built so you can combine tracks, but I have not seen a track list.

**The inner journeys (two seen, summarised).**
- *Being in the river of life* (about 8 min): a river-of-life visualisation that moves from watching your life's course from the bank, through its clear and troubled, calm and turbulent stretches, to immersion and trust in being carried.
- *Giving yourself love and care* (about 5 min): a self-compassion visualisation in which you regard yourself with a parent's warmth and promise to look after yourself; a tip recommends it before sleep. The same tip says the shorter journeys can be memorised and used as breaks during the day.
- The other four by title only: the sacred mountain, connecting with expanse and space, expanse and relaxation in the heart space, meeting your inner knowing.

**Sleep.** A sidebar, "Schlafen Sie immer gleich ein?" (Do you always fall asleep straight away?, p. 55), is kind and practical. Some people fall asleep as soon as they lie down; although, she says, they take in much of the instructions on an unconscious level, they are often annoyed at missing something. Her fix is to sit, leaning against a wall, padded with cushions and blankets, which she says clearly lowers the risk of dozing. She notes that this is not provided for in classical yoga nidra but is very effective.

**Voice and language.** Formal *Sie*, imperative but soft ("Stellen Sie sich vor…", "Spüren Sie…"). The mind "darf" (may) go outward; attention is "invited" to the thumb. There is no "I am practising yoga nidra" formula in what I saw. A sample of how a journey is guided, the opening of the river journey (p. 74):

> "Lassen Sie vor Ihrem inneren Auge das Bild eines Flusses entstehen. Es ist der Fluss Ihres Lebens." ("Let the image of a river arise before your inner eye. It is the river of your life.") — Anna Trökes, *Yoga Nidra*, p. 74.

Short declarative sentences, second person formal, image first and its meaning stated at once. The register sits between Satyananda's directive style and the invitational style of `07` §7.4: a fixed structure, gently worded. One Thalia customer (3 stars, 2019) found the CD voice **too fast**, saying it left them feeling almost a bit rushed. A 5-star reviewer (2016) found yoga nidra easier than progressive muscle relaxation. Those two reviews are the only reception data I could see.

## What's distinctive
- **Compared with the other German mass-market yoga nidra books on your list** (Skuban 2012, `reading/skuban2012.md`; Kündig 2010, `reading/kuendig.md`; Röcker 2007, `reading/roecker.md`). Note that each of those summaries rests on limited access too.
  - *Lineage:* Trökes and Kündig follow the Satyananda format; Röcker builds on Satyananda but adds Jungian, music-therapy and "alpha-level" layers; Skuban follows Swami Rama's Himalayan line and drops sankalpa and imagery altogether.
  - *Printed scripts:* Trökes prints two (basic and short form, not read by me); Kündig prints both of hers; a Skuban reader reports that his practice exists only as audio; for Röcker it is unknown.
  - *Posture and props:* Skuban has short subsections on posture, motionlessness and props (TOC); Kündig describes a bolster and a side-lying alternative. Trökes gives a whole illustrated chapter of five named relaxation postures, preceded by a wind-down asana sequence.
  - *Health claims:* Kündig's subtitle and blurb sell sleep equivalence ("in 30 minutes completely refreshed"; "as after three hours of sleep"); Röcker frames the practice as inner reprogramming at the "alpha level". Trökes avoids both in the pages I saw, but her back cover claims a demonstrable immune-system effect.
  - *What is unique to Trökes in this set:* the stage-by-stage vagus/brain map (p. 27) and the posture-and-props chapter; also the most explicit nervous-system and gut-brain framing.
- **Mainstream German vocabulary.** Satyananda's structure rendered in *Tiefenentspannung*, *Achtsamkeit*, *vegetatives Nervensystem* and *Wohlspannung*, published by GU, which calls itself "Der erste Ratgeberverlag – seit 1722" (the first advice publisher, since 1722), and written by a BDY teacher-trainer rather than a lineage swami.
- **A stage-by-stage nervous-system rationale.** Desai explains the stages through brainwave states. Trökes maps most elements to the vagus, frontal lobe, limbic system or reward system (p. 27). It is the most explicit "each stage has a physiological job" scheme I have seen in this set, and also the least supported (see below).
- **An asana and posture chapter before the practice.** Five relaxation postures with props (including prone, back-relief with heat, and weights); the two I saw (pp. 48–49) each end with an exit routine. Satyananda's 6th edition has "General Suggestions" (p. 74) but no asana or posture chapter in its table of contents.
- **Comfort over orthodoxy, said openly.** She lets readers choose prone or seated positions, and says plainly where this departs from classical yoga nidra. That is rare in a book that otherwise follows Satyananda's sequence.
- **A swappable guided-meditation slot** filled with resource and self-compassion themes (heart space, inner knowing, river of life, self-love) rather than Satyananda's rapid images and chakra work. In structure it is closer to the practice menus of later English-language books such as Dinsmore-Tuli's.
- **A back-pain angle.** The author is a certified back-school instructor. The back-relief posture with pain-easing imagery is a small, practical bridge to the pain research in `06` §6.2.

## Strengths and limitations
- Strengths:
  - **Short, clear, usable.** A complete home-practice package (book plus a 70-minute CD in the author's own voice), with a basic and a short form for different days.
  - **Practical detail on posture and props (in the two postures I saw).** Concrete set-up, how long to stay, warmth and getting up again; a contraindication note for the prone position.
  - **Non-shaming sleep advice** with a simple remedy (sit up), honestly labelled as non-classical.
  - **A grounding rationale for the opening phase** (contact points, here and now, safe in a room). This is close to how trauma-informed teachers now explain the start of practice (`07` §7.2).
  - **Credible author.** She has taught since 1974, has taught in BDY teacher training since 1983, and the book has reached at least nine printings.
- Limitations / critiques (no serious review exists that I could find; these are my own checks against the compendium):
  - **Health claims beyond the evidence.** The back cover promises to strengthen the immune system "nachweislich" (demonstrably) and offers the "Neueste wissenschaftliche Erkenntnisse" (latest scientific findings). The compendium has no yoga nidra study on immune function. The 2025–26 meta-analyses find small to moderate effects from weak studies, no clear advantage over other relaxation, and very small effects in the best German RCT (`06` §6.0). In the pages I saw there is **no** "one hour equals four hours of sleep" claim and no brainwave claim. The compendium's `11` lumps this book with the sleep-substitute and brainwave titles, which looks inaccurate for Trökes and should be revised.
  - **The 30-minute promise is a timing statement, not a sleep equivalent.** GU's blurb says yogic sleep leads the mind into deep rest "in nur 30 Minuten" (in only 30 minutes). In context this describes session length (matching the p. 54 rule to choose a posture you can hold for at least 30 minutes); unlike Kündig's "in 30 minutes completely refreshed" and "as after three hours of sleep", it does not equate the practice with sleep. `06` §6.3 finds no evidence for sleep equivalence, and `08` §11 sets out the choice between big and modest promises. For your own book, a safe wording is: "A full practice takes about 30 minutes. Many people feel rested afterwards; it is not a substitute for sleep."
  - **Speculative neuro-mapping.** The p. 27 box assigns brain regions to stages: opposites to a "neutral" middle frontal area, rapid images to "neutralising" the limbic system, visualisation to the "positive" left frontal region, guided meditation to the reward system. None of this has been tested for yoga nidra. It belongs with the homunculus and "reprograms the subconscious" claims in `06` §6.3. Only the general claim, that slow breathing and relaxation shift the body towards parasympathetic activity, has reasonable support (`06` §6.4).
  - **The gut-brain passage overreaches.** That more nerve pathways run from gut to brain than the reverse (she does not say vagal; my gloss is that this matches the predominance of afferent vagal fibres) is broadly in line with physiology. The conclusion that the gut brain therefore "das Sagen hat" (has the say) is rhetoric, not science.
  - **History told as legend.** The night-watchman story, the caption "entdeckte die alte Technik … wieder" (rediscovered the old technique) and the heading "one of the oldest relaxation methods" repeat the lineage narrative without the historians' caution (`08` §1; Birch & Hargreaves 2015; Singleton 2005). The claim that Satyananda studied biology and medicine has no source given, and I have not seen it in his own account. Check it before repeating it. The book appeared in 2014, before the Royal Commission hearing and report on the Satyananda ashram (`08` §6), so its silence there is a matter of timing. A teacher citing it today should add that context.
  - **Sankalpa as affirmation** imports self-help autosuggestion into the resolve without discussing it. The "I trust my body's self-healing powers" example sits close to the hypnosis question (`08` §10; `03` §3.12).
  - **PTSD named, safety missing (in the pages seen).** The opening phase is said to matter especially for depression, anxiety disorders and all forms of PTSD, but I saw no cautions about trauma, dissociation or the pain/pleasure opposites (`07` §7.1). Check pp. 31–33 ("Jeder kann Yoga & Yoga Nidra üben") before concluding they are absent.
  - **A shoulder stand in a relaxation book for stressed beginners** needs clear cautions. I did not see p. 46.
  - **Death imagery in the posture name** ("Totenhaltung", corpse pose), which `07` §7.2 suggests softening for some groups. This is a minor point and standard German usage.
  - **Thin as a source.** At 80 photo-heavy pages, the theory is brief and the scripts probably short (7 and 4 pages, with photos; not read). There are no references in the pages I saw, apart from a "books and addresses" page.
  - **Reception is barely documented.** No journal, magazine or long-form blog review turned up; the only reviews I could see are two customer reviews on Thalia. Amazon and LovelyBooks pages could not be read.

## For your own book
- Take:
  - **The posture-and-props chapter as a model.** In the two positions I saw (pp. 48–49): set-up, a how-long note, an exit routine, and for the prone position a contraindication line. The other three are known by title only. Cite Trökes as a mainstream precedent for "comfort outranks form" (`05` Posture & props).
  - **The seated remedy for habitual sleepers**, offered without shame and labelled honestly as non-classical. This is a good template for your position on `08` §3. Drop or flag her claim that sleepers take in the instructions unconsciously; it is lineage belief, not evidence.
  - **The grounding rationale for the opening phase** (contact points bring you back to here and now and to the fact that you are safe in this room). Pair it with choice and exits (`07` §7.2), which she does not supply in the pages I saw.
  - **The modular design:** a basic form, a short form, and a swappable guided-meditation slot; the two alternatives I saw are timed (about 5 and 8 min). It works well if your book comes with audio. Her themes (mountain, space, heart, inner knowing, river, self-care) are a ready list to compare with your own.
  - **The river-of-life image** is a gentle, non-trauma-heavy visualisation. Write your own version rather than adapting hers closely.
  - **A short "wind-down before you lie down" sequence** built on lengthening the exhalation. It is a reasonable on-ramp for restless readers, and easy to justify.
  - **German vocabulary** if you write for, or about, the German market: *Tiefenentspannung*, *Achtsamkeit*, *Wohlspannung* and *vegetatives Nervensystem* are the words German readers will expect (`11`).
- Skip or treat critically:
  - **"Demonstrably strengthens the immune system"** and "latest scientific findings": don't repeat them. Use the modest summary from `06`.
  - **"Deep rest in only 30 minutes"** as a selling line: state session length instead and add that it is not a sleep substitute (`06` §6.3; `08` §11).
  - **The stage-by-stage brain map** (frontal lobe, limbic "neutralisation", left frontal positivity, reward system). If you want a physiological rationale per stage, limit it to what is supported (parasympathetic shift, interoception, sleep-onset practice) and call the rest hypotheses.
  - **"The gut brain has the say."**
  - **The origin legend as fact**, "rediscovered" and "one of the oldest relaxation methods". Use the "ancient roots, modern form" framing (`08` §1). Verify the biology-and-medicine detail before using it.
  - **Sankalpa-as-affirmation.** Decide your own position (`08` §8). If you keep affirmation-like resolves, say plainly that this is suggestion (`08` §10).
  - **Naming PTSD as a use case** without safety guidance. If you mention trauma, add the `07` §7.1 table.
  - **Pace on the CD.** One reviewer found it rushed. Leave long pauses in your own recordings (`07` §7.3).
