**Tradition / lineage:** Independent. Her documented training is **French**, not Bihar. The publisher's bio says she earned a teaching diploma from the **Fédération Française de Hatha Yoga (FFHY)** and has taught yoga in Paris since 1981. The 2006 newspaper profile names her teacher-trainer as **Sri Mahesh**, "regarded as the pioneer of Hatha Yoga in France". The BnF identifies him as Shri Mahesh (Mahalingappa Ghatradyal, 1924–2007), a yoga teacher and director of the Centre de relations culturelles franco-indien in Paris. The book's technique vocabulary, though, is clearly **Bihar School**: nyāsa as the origin, the koshas, sankalpa "rules", "Chidaksha-Dharana" (chidākāśa dhāraṇā), chakra visualisation, and *antar mauna* in her later tantric book. "Satyananda-influenced, via Paris" is a fair label, as an inference (see "Confidence"). **Length / format:** 1984/1987: 143 pp. pocket paperback, about 45 pp. of practice. 2007/2010: 379 pp., about 60 pp. of practice. Two written yoga nidra sessions in each edition plus visualisation practices, and companion audio (1986 cassette; 2006 CD, two guided sessions).

## In one paragraph
This is the first book written in German with "yoga nidra" in its title. It appeared in an esoteric paperback series in 1984, eight years after Satyananda's *Yoga Nidra* and eight years before Satyananda's book was translated into German (1992). Its author was a German yoga teacher with degrees in German and English studies, living near Paris since 1978 and trained by the French Hatha Yoga federation. She is also a prolific historian and collector of puppet theatre. The 1984 book is a compact study more than a script collection. About 80 pages of theory place yoga nidra within tantra (nyāsa, mantra, mudrā, chakras) and Patañjali's eight limbs. It then explains how the practice works (sheaths, stages of relaxation, sankalpa), compares it with Jungian depth psychology, free association and hypnosis, and adds a short physiology of relaxation and sleep. About 45 pages of practice follow: sun salutation, concentration and relaxation exercises, two yoga nidra sessions and a set of visualisations. It promises balance of body, soul and mind and, according to the later blurb, access to the deeper, unconscious layers of the mind. In 2007 Schirner published a completely rewritten edition two and a half times as long. It added a CD, a new subtitle stressing visualisation, and a long survey of Western psychotherapy (psychoanalysis, Jung, Rogers, Gestalt, bioenergetics, behaviour therapy), while the practice part stayed small. It is best read as a document of how yoga nidra entered German-speaking yoga: through France, framed by depth psychology, and published as esoteric self-help.

## Core ideas
These are drawn from the tables of contents, the blurbs and the author's website, not from the running text.
- **Yoga nidra is "psychic sleep", a "sleepless sleep".** It means deep relaxation with full awareness, not sleep. The publisher's editor sums up the CD as "deepest relaxation with full consciousness" (CD blurb).
- **Its roots are tantric and yogic.** The theory opens with tantra (nyāsa, mantra, mudrā, maṇḍala, chakras) and then Patañjali's eight limbs. Yoga nidra is presented as a meeting point of the two: tantric technique serving rāja-yoga's withdrawal of the senses and self-knowledge. In outline this is Satyananda's own account of the practice's origin.
- **Ancient and mythic framing.** The blurb calls yoga nidra a path known "for centuries" and the book "age-old holistic yoga knowledge". The cover and her website use the Purāṇic image of **Vishnu asleep on the serpent Śeṣa**, with Brahmā on the lotus from his navel, as the emblem of yoga nidra.
- **Relaxation works in levels or stages.** In 1984 a section on the **koshas** (sheaths) comes straight before "the stages of relaxation". In 2010 the stages are named as four kinds of awareness: **body, breath, senses, thinking**. From the headings this looks like a theory of the middle of the Satyananda sequence (rotation, breath, sensations and feelings, visualisation), possibly mapped onto the sheaths. That reading is mine.
- **Sankalpa = "decisions or guiding maxims".** It gets its own chapter in both editions, and in 2010 explicit "rules for the sankalpa". The German word *Leitsätze* is the everyday vocabulary of autosuggestion and of formula-based self-instruction in autogenic training.
- **Yoga nidra as depth psychology.** The practice is said to reach the unconscious, which she explains through Jung (collective unconscious, symbols, individuation) and, from 2007, through psychoanalysis, dreams, transference and humanistic therapy. The blurb promises that it can **dissolve psychological blockages** and further self-discovery. The individuation process, she says, becomes genuine self-discovery (author's website).
- **Visualisation is the engine.** The 2007 subtitle is "relaxing body, mind and soul through visualisation". The website says visualisation lets you act deliberately on your physical and mental state, and relaxation joins the physical with the psychological. The practice part grew most in visualisations: chidākāśa, the chakras with their symbols, a Shambhala journey, numbers and colours, imagined āsanas.
- **Tantra affirms the world.** Unlike yoga forms that renounce the world, tantra seeks spiritual widening of consciousness within material life (website blurb). This is the reason she gives for using imagery and the senses.
- **Comparison with hypnosis and therapy.** Both editions have a section comparing yoga nidra with hypnosis. In 2007 she adds a chapter comparing the guru with the therapist (empathy, authenticity, "the master as mirror") and a chapter on **risks on the yoga path**. Their contents are unknown.

## The practice as this book teaches it
**Caution:** I did not see the practice pages. What follows comes from the tables of contents (what is there and in what order), the blurbs, two reader reviews of the CD and practice, and a guided meditation in her 2011 book *Tantrische Yoga-Meditationen* (publisher's sample), used only as evidence of her guiding voice.

**Session frame.** In both editions the practice part opens with a **sun salutation** before the yoga nidra sessions (16 pages in 1984). The 1984 edition then has short concentration and relaxation exercises leading into Exercise I. The 2010 edition puts teaching notes first: the voice, falling asleep during practice, *śavāsana*, general advice. The sun salutation follows, then the sessions. So an active warm-up is built into the method. The Satyananda template in `04` §4.1 begins straight from lying down. Posture for yoga nidra: *śavāsana* (2010 heading). Session length: unknown. There are two numbered sessions in each edition (1984: 8 and 5 pages; 2010: 8 and 8 pages), probably a basic and a fuller one. The 2006 CD has two guided meditations, probably the same two. The 1986 cassette's content is not described anywhere I could see. I cannot report its scripts.

**Sankalpa handling.** Present and theorised, and treated as rule-bound by 2007. Wording, placement and the "comes true" claim are not checked.

**Sleep guidance.** The method aims at staying aware ("sleepless sleep"). A dedicated note on falling asleep exists. Its advice is unknown.

**Language style (indirect evidence only).**
- One reviewer of the 2007 book-with-CD (Amazon.de, "3861shakti", 2012) found the CD's **negatively phrased instructions** ("mache nicht…", do not…) distracting and its **pace a little too fast**. She also wished the book's sentences with "nicht" had been phrased positively.
- Her guided meditation in the 2011 tantric book (pp. 194–197) addresses the reader with the formal *Sie*, in a steady stream of imperatives ("close your eyes… concentrate on your breath… visualise…"). It mixes in reflective questions: do you recognise yourself in the reflection? what do you feel in your body now? It relies on structured imagery: a blue lake as wide as the sky, your mirror image, a wind that dissolves the image. It uses Bihar-style tools: backward breath counting, *yoni mudrā*, candle gazing and holding the afterimage at the eyebrow centre. Example, paraphrased: picture a calm lake, recall a moment of craving and look at your face in the water, let a wind erase the image, then recall a moment of generosity and see your face again, calm.
- If the yoga nidra scripts are similar, the style is **directive and imagery-rich, with emotional and self-inquiry content**, closer to a guided imagery journey than to Satyananda's terse naming, and well short of invitational ("you might notice…"). Verify in the book.

## What's distinctive
- **The first German original.** In the DNB catalogue it is the earliest book with "nidra" in the title, and the DNB files it under its "Yoga-Nidrā" subject heading. It preceded the German Satyananda translation (1992, Satyananda-Yoga-Zentrum/Ananda) by eight years. One caveat: a yoga nidra *method* was already available in German as Boyes's *Autogenes Yoga* (O.W. Barth, 1976), the translation of his 1973 French book (see `boyes1973.md`). So say "first German-language book titled after yoga nidra", not "first yoga nidra book in German".
- **Yoga nidra came to Germany via Paris.** A German author trained by the French Hatha Yoga federation and teaching in Paris wrote it. In Paris in 1980, Satyananda's book had appeared in French as *Yoga nidra: apprenez à dormir* (Éditions Satyanandashram, 208 pp.), and Boyes had been teaching his "sommeil éveillé" there since 1973. Whether she used either is unknown; her bibliography (1984, pp. 142–143) would tell.
- **Depth psychology as the frame.** No other book in this project reads yoga nidra so thoroughly through Jung (collective unconscious, symbols, shadow, anima/animus, individuation). From 2007 it adds psychoanalysis, Rogers, Gestalt, bioenergetics and behaviour therapy, and a guru-versus-therapist comparison. Satyananda borrowed "subconscious" loosely, and Miller's frame is non-dual. Hers is consistently psychotherapeutic.
- **Movement plus rest.** The sun salutation is part of the method. That fits her later books on the sun salutation (2006) and moon salutation (2009).
- **Eclectic visualisation.** A Tibetan-Buddhist **Shambhala visualisation** sits beside Hindu-tantric chakra imagery. This fits her own range: she also wrote on tantric Buddhism (*Das Lebensrad*) and Bhutan.
- **A record of how the German market changed.** In 1984 it was an esoteric pocket paperback at DM 9.80, catalogued under psychology, philosophy and religion. By 2007 it was a large book with a CD, catalogued under medicine and health, and in 2010 the DNB tagged it "Ratgeber" (self-help guide). The same book was reshaped for the wellness market.
- **Risks on the yoga path.** The 2007 edition has a short safety-type chapter (pp. 271–274), rare among popular books of that period. Content unknown.

## Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:**
  - Historically important for the German reception, and dated, catalogued and verifiable.
  - Sets the practice in its theory (tantra, rāja-yoga, the sheaths) instead of offering bare scripts. Readers praise it as clear, well researched and thorough on the history of yoga nidra (Amazon.de reviews 2013–2025).
  - Has practical teaching notes on voice, falling asleep and posture (2010, pp. 278–281).
  - Offers a whole toolkit of visualisations, if you want variety.
  - Reception: 14 ratings, average 4.9/5, for the Schirner editions on Amazon.de; 2 ratings, 5/5, for the Bauer edition. One teacher reports using the 1984 book in her yoga classes with success (2022). On Goodreads, 1 rating (3/5), no reviews.
- **Limitations / critiques:**
  - **Title promises more practice than the book delivers.** The most substantial review (Klaus Jürgen Becker, Amazon.de, 2016, 5 stars) calls the revised edition a fine read on Vedanta and tantra, but says yoga nidra practice takes only about 30 pages. He wanted more on how it works and on sleep and waking consciousness, and called the subtitle misleading. The table of contents bears this out: practice is pp. 277–336 of 379, and the two yoga nidra sessions take 16 pages.
  - **Delivery of the CD:** negative phrasing and a slightly fast pace ("3861shakti", 2012, 4 stars). Her own reason, that the brain cannot process "not", is pop psychology. The practical point still stands.
  - **No serious review found.** I found no journal, magazine or long-form blog review of any edition. (Web search was unavailable for part of this work; see "Confidence".)
  - **Claims that outrun the evidence:** that it reaches "deeper, unconscious layers", dissolves "psychological blockages", and opens "consciousness-transcending experiences" (blurbs). `06` §6.3 classes "reprograms the subconscious" as a metaphor. The best German trial found only very small effects [@moszeik2025; @moszeik2023].
  - **"For centuries" / "age-old".** The word *yoganidrā* and the Vishnu image are old, but the guided method is modern (`08` §1) [@birch2015; @singleton2005]. The book's self-presentation follows the lineage story.
  - **A single page each** on yoga nidra and mental illness (1984, p. 75) and on hypnosis (1984, p. 76). These are big topics for one page. The physiology section (1984, pp. 79–85) will be dated.
  - **Proportions.** The 2007 revision mostly grew in psychology and philosophy, not practice, which matters if you want a working manual.

## For your own book
- **Take:**
  - **A reception paragraph for German readers** (`11` §11.1). Suggested framing: "The first German book named after yoga nidra appeared in 1984, by Ingrid Ramm-Bonwitt, a German yoga teacher trained in Paris. That was eight years after Satyananda's English original and eight years before its German translation. Yoga nidra reached German readers partly by way of France." Add Boyes's *Autogenes Yoga* (1976) as the earlier, differently titled forerunner.
  - **The "Leitsätze" gloss for sankalpa.** It shows that from the start German readers heard sankalpa in the language of autosuggestion and autogenic training's formula resolutions. That supports the compendium's "close cousin" position in the hypnosis debate (`08` §10). Cite the 1984 heading (p. 57) once you have checked how she uses the term.
  - **An active-then-rest class design.** A sun salutation before yoga nidra is a real teaching choice. It discharges restlessness before lying still. If you recommend it, give the reason, and offer a gentle alternative for tired or unwell readers.
  - **A teacher-facing section on voice and on students falling asleep.** She gives these their own headings. Read pp. 278–281 (2010) and compare with `07`.
  - **The four "levels" (body, breath, senses, thinking)** as a simple way to explain *why* the stages come in this order. You can teach it without sheath metaphysics.
  - **Rogers's three conditions applied to guiding:** empathic non-judgemental understanding, warmth, congruence (2010, pp. 255–261). This is a good frame for the guide's attitude in a teaching chapter (`07` §7.4), wherever you take it from.
  - **A cautionary lesson about proportion and titles.** A reviewer's main complaint is a subtitle that promised practice and a book that delivered theory. Make your own subtitle honest.
- **Skip or treat critically:**
  - **Don't call her a Satyananda disciple** or the book a Bihar School text. Her documented training is FFHY / Shri Mahesh. The Bihar vocabulary is a borrowing, probably via the 1980 French translation. That is unproven.
  - **Don't call the Schirner edition a reprint.** It is a complete rewrite (143 → 379 pp.) with different pagination. Cite the edition you use.
  - **Leave out "dissolves psychological blockages" and "access to the unconscious"** as promises. Use the modest framing in `06` §6.0. If you quote her, quote her as an example of 1980s–2000s German self-help framing.
  - **Don't follow her single page on mental illness** without reading it. Current guidance on trauma, psychosis and dissociation is in `07`.
  - **The mythic origin.** Use the Vishnu-on-Śeṣa image as cultural imagery for the word's oldest meaning (the god's cosmic sleep), not as evidence that the method is old.
  - **Negatively phrased cues** ("don't move", "don't sleep"). Prefer positive instructions, for the practical reason the reviewer gives, not the brain-cannot-process-"not" myth.
  - **Eclectic imagery** (Shambhala beside Hindu chakras). If you borrow, name each tradition you draw on, so you don't present a personal blend as one tradition.
