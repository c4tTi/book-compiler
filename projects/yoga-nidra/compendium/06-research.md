# 6. Research: What Is Known

The research is **promising but thin**. Most studies are small, many lack
active controls, and many appeared in lower-impact journals. Several
test yoga nidra as part of a larger yoga program. Write about it with
matching modesty. That is also a mark of credibility for your book.

✅ All papers below were checked against PubMed/DOI records (September 2026). Details of
effects come from abstracts; read the full papers before quoting numbers.

---

## 6.0 The 2025–26 meta-analyses: the new baseline ✅

Between 2023 and 2026 the field produced its first real systematic reviews.
They agree on a pattern: **effects look moderate to large, but the studies
are weak, so the true effects are probably smaller.** Cite these rather
than individual small trials.

| Review | Scope | Headline | Authors' own caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ghai, Odyniec & Ghai 2026, *Ann NY Acad Sci* [@ghai2026] | Stress, anxiety, depression; 73 studies, 5,201 participants | vs. active comparators: stress g = −0.80, anxiety −1.35, depression −0.69 | "low methodological quality ... likely reflect inflated estimates" |
| Ghai & Ghai 2025, *Complement Med Res* [@ghai2025pain] | Pain; 12 studies, 1,176 participants | Large vs. passive controls (g = −2.05); **no significant difference vs. active controls** (g = −0.31) | effects "may represent inflated estimates" |
| Ghai & Ghai 2025, *Complement Ther Med* [@ghai2025cardio] | Blood pressure, heart rate, HRV; 28 studies | Systolic BP g = −1.65 vs. active controls | most studies methodologically limited |
| Singh et al. 2026, *Sleep Breath* [@singh2026] | Insomnia / poor sleep; only 5 usable studies | RCT pooled effects large but **not statistically significant** | GRADE certainty **very low**; trials concentrated in India |
| Dutta et al. 2026, *J Integr Complement Med* [@dutta2026] | Sleep disorders; 6 RCTs, n = 244 | Improvements vs. CBT-I, PMR, music | moderate–high risk of bias; adverse events poorly reported |
| Sony et al. 2026, *IJYT* [@sony2026] | Menstrual disorders; 7 RCTs | Improvements in well-being, hormones, blood parameters | more high-quality RCTs needed |
| Musto & Hazard Vallerand 2023, *J Nurs Scholarsh* [@musto2023] | Integrative (nursing) review; 29 studies | "Effective in most" studies | heterogeneous doses and populations |
| Arya et al. 2025, *Int J Yoga* [@arya2025] | Yoga (incl. nidra) for nurses and nursing students | Supports use for nurse well-being 🔎 (record only) | — |

An umbrella review of these reviews is pre-registered [@giridharan2026].

**How to read them for your book:**
- The one comparison that matters most, **yoga nidra vs. another active
  practice**, is where effects shrink or disappear (pain: n.s.; the
  German RCT below: d ≈ 0.1). Yoga nidra clearly beats doing nothing; it is
  not clearly better than other relaxation.
- A well-run German online RCT (n = 362; 11- or 30-min yoga nidra vs. a
  10-min music control vs. waitlist, two months) found **very small**
  effects: the 11-min version beat waitlist at d = 0.08–0.16 and beat
  music only on depression (d = 0.13); the 30-min version did somewhat
  better than the 11-min one; cortisol changes appeared only in those who
  practised often [@moszeik2025; @moszeik2023]. This is probably closer to the truth for
  healthy adults using a recording than the pooled estimates are.
- The meditation-research field as a whole has been criticised for hype,
  weak controls and vague definitions [@vandam2018]. Every criticism
  applies to yoga nidra studies too.

## 6.1 Neuroscience

### Dopamine release (PET) ✅🔎
- **Kjaer TW, Bertelsen C, Piccini P, Brooks D, Alving J, Lou HC (2002).**
  "Increased dopamine tone during meditation-induced change of
  consciousness." *Cognitive Brain Research* 13(2):255–259. [@kjaer2002]
- Eight experienced yoga nidra practitioners (yoga teachers, men aged
  31–50) were scanned twice on separate days: once during a 72-minute
  taped yoga nidra, once while **attending to recorded speech with eyes
  closed** (the control) [@kjaer2002; @auntminnie2002]. Raclopride binding
  in the ventral striatum fell by 7.9%, which the authors equate to a
  **~65% increase in endogenous dopamine release**, together with reduced
  desire for action and more EEG theta.
- **Subjective null result:** "the level of gratification and the depth of
  relaxation did not differ" between yoga nidra and the speech-listening
  control (abstract) [@kjaer2002].
- **Most-cited finding in the literature.** Limitations: n = 8 (the
  abstract gives no n), experienced practitioners, one study, never
  replicated. The control was attentive listening, not silent rest.

### Blood-flow patterns (PET) ✅🔎
- **Lou HC, Kjaer TW, Friberg L, Wildschiødtz G, Holm S, Nowak M (1999).**
  "A 15O-H2O PET study of meditation and the resting state of normal
  consciousness." *Human Brain Mapping* 7(2):98–105. [@lou1999]
- Nine "highly experienced yoga teachers", compared with the resting
  state of normal consciousness (eyes closed) [@lou1999]. Different stages
  of the practice (body, abstract joy, visual images) showed distinct
  regional activity, e.g., more posterior/sensory activation during
  visualization and less executive (prefrontal) activity overall.
- According to Swami Janakananda's Scandinavian school, the subjects
  practised the "Deep Yoga Nidra" of its CD *Experience Yoga Nidra*; its
  account speaks of "seven yoga teachers" measured, the abstract of nine
  [@nilsson1999]. ⚠️ Unresolved (perhaps exclusions; check the methods).

### EEG ⚠️🔎
- Studies report more **alpha and theta** during the practice, suggesting a
  relaxed state between waking and sleep. Some recent work suggests
  **"local sleep"** (sleep-like EEG patterns in parts of the brain while
  the person stays responsive). 🔎 e.g., Datta K et al. (2022),
  *Frontiers in Neurology* on local sleep during yoga nidra [@datta2022].
- **Swami Rama / Menninger** (1970): one subject, one 25-minute session.
  He snored, showed scattered delta in a variable stage 2–4 record, and
  afterwards repeated most of four sentences spoken in the room
  [@green1974]. *Beyond Biofeedback* (1977) does not report this session
  [@green1977]. Historically significant; scientifically anecdotal.
  A Himalayan-tradition psychologist argues it should be followed up with
  modern methods [@parker2019].
- **Systematic review (2025, preprint):** 12 EEG studies, 326 people; the
  only consistent result is **more theta in experienced practitioners**,
  who mostly stay awake [@kachera2025]. ⚠️ Only two studies met all
  quality criteria.
- **Italian retreat study:** no sleep spindles or K-complexes in 2-hour
  sessions; practitioners reported altered states and more dissociation
  [@zaccaro2021]. n = 6.

### fMRI: the default mode network ✅
- **Fialoke et al. (2024), *Scientific Reports*** [@fialoke2024]: 30
  meditators vs. 31 novices. During yoga nidra, meditators showed
  **decoupling of the default mode network** (less connectivity), novices
  did not, and the decoupling tracked lifetime hours of practice. The
  authors read this as "restful yet aware". Huberman publicised it as
  showing NSDR "is not just a light nap". ⚠️ A single study, and
  experience-dependent: it says little about a beginner with an app.

## 6.2 Clinical and applied studies

| Area | Example studies 🔎 | What they suggest | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stress, anxiety, well-being** | Moszeik, von Oertzen & Renner (2020), *Current Psychology*: 11-min online practice, n=341 vs. waitlist n=430 [@moszeik2020] | Reduced stress, better well-being and sleep quality vs. waitlist; effects very small to small | ⚠️ moderate, short-term |
| **PTSD (veterans)** | Stankovic (2011), *Int J Yoga Therapy* 21:23–37: iRest with combat veterans (qualitative feasibility) [@stankovic2011] | Lower rage, anxiety, reactivity; feasible | ⚠️ small, uncontrolled |
| **Military sexual trauma** | Pence et al. (2014), *Int J Yoga Therapy* 24:53–62: iRest with women veterans [@pence2014] | Reduced PTSD and depressive symptoms | ⚠️ pilot |
| **Sleep / insomnia** | Datta et al. (2021), *Natl Med J India*: RCT, n=41, yoga nidra vs. CBT-I [@datta2021] | Both improved; yoga nidra showed more deep-sleep (N2/N3) gains and lower cortisol | ⚠️ small |
| **Menstrual disorders** | Rani et al. (2011), *Int J Yoga* 4(1):20–25 [@rani2011] | Better psychological well-being in women with menstrual irregularities | ⚠️ small RCT |
| **Diabetes** | Amita et al. (2009), *Indian J Physiol Pharmacol* 53(1):97–101 [@amita2009] | Lower blood glucose alongside medication | ⚠️ small |
| **Teachers / professionals** | Ferreira-Vorkapic et al. (2018), *Int J Yoga* [@ferreiravorkapic2018] | Reduced anxiety and stress vs. control | ⚠️ small |
| **Chronic pain with TBI** | Nassif et al. (2015/16), *Military Behavioral Health*: iRest n=4 vs. usual care n=5 [@nassif2015] | Pain intensity and interference down | ⚠️ tiny pilot |
| **Chronic pain vs. body scan** | Gibbs et al. (2025), *Int J Yoga*: 45-min yoga nidra (n=12) vs. body scan (n=11) [@gibbs2025] | Both reduced pain and pain anxiety; only yoga nidra raised well-being | ⚠️ quasi-experimental, one session |
| **Functional dissociative seizures** | Gomathy et al. (2026), *Epilepsy & Behavior*: pilot RCT, n=50, YN vs. sham YN on top of psychoeducation [@gomathy2026] | Seizures fell sharply in **both** groups; no difference (p = 0.88) | ⚠️ pilot; a useful null result with a sham control |
| **Health-care workers (nursing)** | Livingston & Collette-Merrill (2018), *Holistic Nursing Practice*: 8-week iRest, n=15 [@livingston2018] | Mindfulness up; sleepiness and pain not significantly changed | ⚠️ pre/post only |
| **Students (college)** | Eastman-Mueller et al. (2013), *IJYT*: 8-week iRest, n=66 [@eastmanmueller2013] | Stress, worry, depression down; mostly in those already depressed | ⚠️ uncontrolled |
| **Medical students (India)** | Kumari et al. (2025), *BMJ Open*: protocol, 12-min YN vs. sleep education, n=160 [@kumari2025] | Results pending | registered CTRI/2022/07/044426 |
| **Autistic children** | Loftus et al. (2026), *Res Dev Disabil*: co-designed 6-week online YN, n=13 [@loftus2026] | No significant change in anxiety; medium effect sizes; children found "thinking about the body is hard" | ⚠️ feasibility |
| **Depression, anxiety (Iceland)** | Gunnarsdottir et al. (2022), nursing researchers: patients' experience of yoga and yoga nidra [@gunnarsdottir2022] | "For the first time I felt great hope" | ⚠️ qualitative, low-impact journal; record only 🔎 |

**Reviews:** 🔎 e.g., Pandi-Perumal SR et al. (2022), "The Origin and
Clinical Relevance of Yoga Nidra", *Sleep and Vigilance* [@pandiperumal2022]: a narrative
review concluding the evidence is encouraging but methodologically weak.

## 6.3 Claims to handle carefully ⚠️

| Popular claim | Status |
|---|---|
| "1 hour of yoga nidra = 4 hours of sleep" | **No evidence.** It is Satyananda's own claim: "One hour of yoga nidra is equal to four hours of sleep" (talk in Japan, 1969 [@satyanandasaraswati1969]), repeated in the Bihar School's *YOGA* magazine as "half an hour of yoga nidra equals two hours of sleep" (1977 [@shankardevanandasara1977]), as shavasana rest "almost twice as deep" as a night's sleep (1976 [@sahastrabudhe1976]) and as "one hour of yoga nidra during the day is equal to four hours of sleep at night" (Aug 1978 editorial [@yoga1978]). Swami Rama told the Menninger researchers in 1970 that with brain and mind quiet "a couple hours of sleep is enough" [@green1974]. Other groups repeated it (a 1983 Hindi monthly [@akhandjyoti1983]; Kamini Desai's 2017 book, as 45 minutes ≈ three hours, attributed to "yogis", PDF pp. 12, 97, 180 [@desai2017]), and it survives in book subtitles ("in 30 minutes completely refreshed" [@kuendig]). No study measures it; a sleep researcher quoted in the press says it cannot be tested, and the same article reports Huberman conceding there is no evidence for it [@worldcrunch] 🔎 (search snippet only). A sleep-physician-reviewed explainer notes that the research is on yoga nidra, not on "NSDR" as such, and that no study compares it with napping [@sleepfoundation]. Don't use, or quote as a claim and say so. |
| "Yoga nidra reaches delta (deep sleep) while aware" | Only in the Swami Rama anecdote and in claims; the EEG literature mostly shows alpha/theta. |
| "The resolve made in yoga nidra always comes true" | A traditional teaching; not testable as stated. |
| "Rotation follows the brain's homunculus, so it relaxes the brain systematically" | Plausible metaphor; not tested. |
| "Reprograms the subconscious" | Metaphor, not a mechanism. |
| "Increases dopamine by 65%" | Based on one n = 8 study, against a speech-listening control in which relaxation felt just as deep. You can cite it, but with context. Popular books stretch it: Desai (2017) presents it as specific to yoga nidra and suggests yoga nidra as an aid for Parkinson's disease and ADD/ADHD (PDF pp. 202, 291–292) [@desai2017]. |
| "Equivalent to therapy for PTSD" | No. It is studied as a **complement**, not a replacement. |

## 6.4 Plausible mechanisms (reasonably supported in general relaxation research) ✅
- **Down-regulation of the stress response** (parasympathetic shift),
  similar to other relaxation and meditation methods.
- **Interoception:** attention to inner body sensations, which is linked
  to emotional regulation.
- **Sleep-onset training:** practising the transition into sleep.
- **Expectancy and the relaxation response:** a supportive voice and a
  ritual matter, which is not a weakness but part of how it works. The
  "relaxation response" is Benson's classic model of what all such
  practices share [@benson1975]; treat the single shared mechanism as his
  hypothesis, since techniques also have specific effects [@lehrer1994].

## 6.5 Where the research is (and isn't) ✅
- **Registries:** ClinicalTrials.gov lists 22 studies mentioning yoga
  nidra (15 completed, 2 withdrawn). A search for "iRest" returns 27
  records, but only three or four concern iRest yoga nidra; the rest match
  unrelated terms (checked 4 Oct 2026); half have US sites,
  others are in Iceland, Turkey, the UK, Italy and Taiwan [@ctgov]. Most
  Indian trials are registered in CTRI instead [@ctri]. Registered but
  unpublished European work includes an Austrian-led sleep-lab RCT
  [@dielacher2025] 🔎.
- **Geography:** the sleep meta-analysis notes that its trials are
  concentrated in India and calls for trials in other settings
  [@singh2026]. Much of the literature appears in yoga-specific journals
  (IJYT, *Int J Yoga*) rather than mainstream medical ones.
- **Official summaries** do not rate yoga nidra separately. NIH's NCCIH
  groups it under relaxation and meditation, rates the evidence for
  relaxation techniques mostly low to very low, and notes stronger
  evidence for CBT in most conditions [@nccih2021; @nccih2022].

## Author's note

**Recommended:** one short, honest chapter or box: two or three
neuroscience findings (Kjaer 2002 and the 2024 fMRI study), the 2025–26
meta-analyses in one sentence each (6.0), and a clear list of myths. The
honest summary is: *better than nothing, about as good as other
relaxation, and studied mostly in small trials.* This builds trust.
**Cut:** long lists of small studies; they date quickly.
