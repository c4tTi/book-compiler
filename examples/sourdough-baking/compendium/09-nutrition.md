# 9. Nutrition and health claims: what the evidence supports

This chapter needs the most careful wording in the book. Marketing ("gut-friendly", "low GI", "gluten-reduced") runs well ahead of the clinical evidence.

## 9.1 Blood sugar (glycaemic response)
- ⚠️ **Mixed and weak evidence.** A 2026 systematic review (27 studies, 12 directly comparing sourdough and yeast bread) found some benefit in 10 of 12, but rated the evidence **low to very low certainty** and called it "neither sufficient nor convincing". Longer fermentation, not the microbes as such, may drive much of the effect [@starowicz2026]. D'Amico et al.: about 50% of GI studies show no effect [@damico2023].
- Whole-grain sourdough seems more likely to reduce post-meal glucose than white [@damico2023]. 🔎 This is secondary; check the primary studies before print.

## 9.2 FODMAPs and IBS
- ✅ Long sourdough fermentation can break down fructans (a FODMAP). Reductions of 50–95% are reported, depending on microbes and flour [@damico2023; @loponen2018].
- ✅ Monash University (the originators of the low-FODMAP diet) certify some traditionally fermented **spelt** sourdoughs as low FODMAP; proofing is typically >12 h. But ordinary spelt bread isn't low FODMAP, and a product has to be lab-tested to know [@monash_sourdough; @monash_ancient].
- ⚠️ In clinical tests, FODMAP-optimised sourdough didn't clearly beat regular sourdough for symptoms [@damico2023]. A small 2026 double-blind crossover RCT (n = 20, whole-grain wheat) found fewer diarrhoea symptoms and higher *Bifidobacterium* counts with sourdough than with yeast bread; the overall IBS score was only borderline [@watters2026]. Newer low-FODMAP formulation work continues [@galgano2026; @ribet2026].

## 9.3 Gluten and coeliac disease (a safety issue, not only nutrition)
- ✅ **Wheat, rye and barley sourdough is NOT safe for people with coeliac disease.** Independent tests of commercial "sourdough" found gluten levels far above the 20 ppm gluten-free threshold, one at about 104,000 ppm [@gfwatchdog; @beyondceliac].
- ✅ The Italian studies often cited to claim safety used lab-hydrolysed flour with selected lactobacilli *and fungal proteases*, which home or bakery fermentation can't reproduce [@gfwatchdog; @damico2023].
- ⚠️ Some people without coeliac disease report tolerating sourdough better. Possible reasons include lower FODMAPs, flour choice, or fewer additives, but it's unproven, and sourdough is not meaningfully "low gluten" [@rbc_myth2023; @ribet2026; @distasio2025].

## 9.4 Minerals and phytate
- ✅ Acidification activates phytase and degrades phytate, which improves mineral *bioaccessibility* in the lab [@damico2023; @katina2012].
- ✅ Short-term human iron-absorption studies show large effects: dephytinised sourdough rye bread gave about 2.8–3.5× more non-haem iron absorption than scalded high-phytate bread [@hoppe2025].
- ⚠️ But long-term trials didn't improve iron status. In one, ferritin even fell in the low-phytate sourdough rye group over 12 weeks [@nikolaou2026; @todorovic2025]. So: plausible acute benefit, no demonstrated effect on iron status.

## 9.5 Gut microbiome, satiety, weight
- ⚠️ Plausible mechanisms (fibre, exopolysaccharides, peptides), but knowledge gaps. No clinical evidence for weight management [@damico2023; @ribet2026]. Note that the live microbes don't survive baking, so the finished bread isn't a probiotic food [@damico2023].

## 9.6 Official status of claims
- ✅ **No sourdough-specific health claim has been authorised by EFSA.** A glycaemic claim was rejected because comparisons were against glucose, not ordinary bread [@damico2023]. The UK register likewise has none [@rbc_myth2023].
- ✅ Much published research uses optimised laboratory sourdoughs (pH < 4.5). Much commercial "sourdough" is pH > 5, so lab results may not apply to shop bread [@damico2023; @debondt2024].

## 9.7 Where the voices differ
| Voice | Position |
|---|---|
| Nutrition scientists (reviews) | Promising mechanisms, weak clinical proof [@damico2023; @starowicz2026] |
| Practitioner/health-oriented bakers | Emphasise digestibility and gut health [@kimbell2018; @whitley2006] |
| Campaigners | Benefits "might" exist, but only with genuine long fermentation [@rbc_myth2023] |
| Coeliac advocates | Firm: not safe [@gfwatchdog; @beyondceliac] |
| Industry/market reports | Use "easy digestibility, low GI" as sales drivers [@mordor_market; @rm_market] |
| Historical "natural bread" movements | Health claims have a long lineage (e.g. 1950s France) [@ruasse1957] |

- **Optional:** the iron and FODMAP detail as "what science says" boxes.
- **Cut:** mechanism-level biochemistry. Never write "sourdough is gluten-free", "low GI", or "probiotic".
