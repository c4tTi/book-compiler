# 1. History: from Neolithic accident to lockdown craze

## 1.1 Origins
- ✅ Leavened bread is ancient, and for most of history "leavened" meant *sourdough*: a piece of yesterday's dough kept back to raise today's. Wikipedia's synthesis puts one of the oldest excavated sourdough breads at c. 3700 BC in Switzerland, with the practice likely older in the Fertile Crescent and Egypt [@wiki_sourdough; @wiki_breadhistory].
- ✅ Egypt is the best-documented early baking culture. Delwen Samuel's microscopy of desiccated loaves and beer residues found that Egyptian bread was made "not only with flour from raw grain, but sometimes also with malt and with yeast", which revised the textbook picture of Egyptian baking [@samuel1996]. Treat popular claims of a single "discovery" moment in Egypt (dough left out, wild yeast drifted in) as folklore, not evidence [@wiki_sourdough].
- ⚠️ Popular histories (many SEO-driven blogs turned up in searches) often give exact dates ("1500 BC", "8,600 years ago") without sources. Prefer the scholarly overviews: Wirtz's historical chapter in the *Handbook of Dough Fermentations* [@wirtz2003], Rubel's *Bread: A Global History* [@rubel2011], and, for narrative colour but dated scholarship, H. E. Jacob's 1944 classic [@jacob1944].

## 1.2 Rome and the primary sources
- ✅ Pliny the Elder (*Natural History* XVIII.26, c. 77 CE) describes several leavens: millet kneaded with grape must, which "will keep a whole year"; wheat bran kneaded with three-day-old must and sun-dried; and, most commonly, "a little of the dough that has been kept from the day before" [@pliny77; @monaco2017]. That last line is a precise description of back-slopping, which is still how every home starter is maintained.
- ✅ Pliny also reports that Gauls and Iberians used beer foam as leaven, giving lighter bread [@wiki_breadhistory]. This matters for the book because it marks the start of the long competition between sourdough and brewer's barm/yeast.

## 1.3 Barm, commercial yeast and the eclipse of sourdough
- ✅ In medieval Europe barm from brewing gradually displaced sourdough in many places, and after about 1871 purpose-cultured commercial yeast took over, following Pasteur's work [@wiki_sourdough]. Rye regions (Germany, Scandinavia, Eastern Europe) kept sourdough because rye needs acidification to bake well (see ch. 12) [@hamelman2004; @spicher1982].
- ⚠️ Andrew Whitley's *Bread Matters* frames 20th-century industrial baking (fast, additive-assisted processes) as a loss of flavour and digestibility [@whitley2006]. It's an influential practitioner critique, not neutral history. Use it as a position to engage with (see `workbench/voice-and-positions.md`).

## 1.4 North America: Gold Rush, Klondike and San Francisco
- ✅ Isidore Boudin, from a Burgundian baking family, founded Boudin Bakery in San Francisco in 1849 and applied French technique to the fermented dough; the bakery still claims continuity with its 19th-century mother dough [@wiki_sfbread; @foundsf_boudin]. 🔎 The story that Louise Boudin carried the starter out of the 1906 earthquake appears only in popular retellings; verify before printing.
- ⚠️ Wikipedia notes that California prospectors may in fact have leavened with commercial yeast or baking soda more often than legend says [@wiki_sourdough].
- ✅ The Klondike (1896–99) made sourdough iconic in Alaska and the Yukon: prospectors carried starter pouches and guarded them against freezing, and old-timers came to be called "sourdoughs" [@wiki_sourdough; @dailymeal_alaska]. Robert Service's *Songs of a Sourdough* (1907) is the literary record of that usage [@service1907]; Ruth Allman's *Alaska Sourdough* preserves the home-cooking tradition [@allman1976].
- ✅ In 1971 USDA scientists Leo Kline and T. F. Sugihara isolated and named the souring bacterium of San Francisco sourdough (now *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis*) [@kline1971; @wiki_sfbread]. It has since been found in starters worldwide, so it is not unique to the Bay Area [@landis2021; @kqed_sf].
- Heirloom-starter lore persists online (e.g. "Carl Griffith's 1847 Oregon Trail starter") [@hn_oregontrail]; so does experimental archaeology, such as Seamus Blackley's attempt to bake with yeast recovered from Egyptian pottery [@hn_egyptian].

## 1.5 Revivals: 1970s homesteading, artisan bakeries, 2020 lockdown
- The 1970s US counterculture produced a wave of sourdough cookbooks [@wilford1971; @allman1976]. French *pain au levain* traditions were documented in the same decade [@robert1975].
- The 2000s artisan revival (Tartine, Ken's Artisan, King Arthur's teaching) produced today's "open crumb country loaf" template [@robertson2010; @forkish2012; @reinhart2001].
- ✅ In spring 2020, COVID-19 lockdowns and yeast shortages made sourdough a global craze. Flour and yeast sales doubled or tripled; bread dominated Google's trending recipes for 2020, and "sourdough" searches peaked in April–May [@wiki_pandemic; @sustain_2020; @collins2020]. Interest stayed above pre-2020 levels afterwards [@wiki_pandemic].

## Purpose note (beginner's guide)
- **Essential:** a 2–3 page "why this bread is old" opener: Pliny's back-slopping line, the Klondike "sourdoughs", 2020. It tells beginners they're joining a very old, forgiving practice.
- **Optional:** the San Francisco microbiology story (good sidebar that sets up ch. 3's "your local bugs don't make your bread special" finding).
- **Cut:** detailed Egyptian archaeology and medieval barm history, and undated SEO "history" claims.
