# 3. What lives in a starter: the microbiology

## 3.1 The cast
- ✅ A starter is a stable community of **lactic acid bacteria (LAB)** and **yeasts**. The yeasts (most often *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, also *Kazachstania* and others) make CO₂ and ethanol. The LAB make lactic and acetic acid (sourness, preservation) and outnumber the yeasts [@asm_microbiome; @gobbetti2012; @devuyst2023].
- ✅ In the largest survey to date (500 starters, 4 continents), each starter held a median of about 7 bacterial and 35 fungal variants. *S. cerevisiae* made up more than 50% of fungal reads in 77% of samples; common bacteria were *(Fructi)lactobacillus sanfranciscensis*, *L. plantarum* and *L. brevis* [@landis2021].
- ✅ Newer home-starter work finds *Kazachstania* rising to dominance in all starters regardless of flour or feeding frequency, while **flour type shifts the bacteria** (e.g. more *Companilactobacillus* with whole wheat, more *Levilactobacillus* with bread flour) [@taheri2026]. Flour-specific climax communities are also reported by McKenney et al. [@mckenney2023].
- Taxonomy note for the author: in 2020 *Lactobacillus* was split into many genera (*Fructilactobacillus*, *Lactiplantibacillus*, *Levilactobacillus*...). Older books use the old names. 🔎 Check names against a current source before print; the recent papers here use the new names [@taheri2026; @ganzle2014].

## 3.2 What the microbes do to dough
- ✅ Fermentation and the flour's own enzymes work together: amylases release maltose, which heterofermentative LAB and yeasts use; proteases (cereal ones, mostly) break down proteins; acidification changes enzyme activity [@ganzle2014].
- ✅ **Acetic acid bacteria** (AAB) are an under-appreciated player. Where present they explained most of the variation in aroma (vinegar notes) and correlated with *slower* rise [@landis2021]. ⚠️ De Vuyst et al. note that the presence and role of AAB in sourdough "can be criticized" [@devuyst2023], so the field isn't settled.
- ✅ LAB produce antifungal compounds, which is one reason sourdough bread moulds more slowly [@liu2022; @sciam_antifungal].

## 3.3 What doesn't matter as much as people think
- ✅ **Geography**: little biogeographic signal. Process variables (starter age, feeding frequency, flour, storage location) each explained less than 10% of variation; microbe–microbe interactions structured communities more [@landis2021]. The "San Francisco bugs" story is mostly myth [@kqed_sf; @wiki_sfbread; @kline1971].
- ✅ **Starter pedigree**: a bought or gifted starter drifts toward whatever your flour and routine select for [@ka_myths; @reese2020].
- ⚠️ **Bakers' hands** do contribute microbes, and the same 18 starters, made from identical recipes, produced breads with different flavour [@reese2020]. Interesting, but it doesn't give beginners anything to act on.

## 3.4 Types of sourdough (vocabulary for the book)
- ✅ Scientists distinguish **Type I** (traditional back-slopped, spontaneous: home starters), **Type II** (starter-culture-initiated, liquid, industrial) and **Type III** (dried sourdough preparations used as acidifier/flavour) [@devuyst2023; @gobbetti2012]. This vocabulary explains why "sourdough" on a supermarket label can mean very different things (ch. 14).
- ✅ The scoping review of 253 wheat-sourdough studies found huge variation in leavening amount, fermentation conditions and resulting acidity, which makes cross-study comparison (and health claims) hard [@debondt2024].

- Rob Dunn's lab (NC State) ran the Global Sourdough Project [@dunn_person; @dunnlab_gsp]. For audio: Gastropod's "Secrets of Sourdough" [@gastropod2017], Science Friday [@scifri_microbiome], and a Sourdough Podcast interview with chemist Emily Buehler [@buehler_podcast].
- Key academic names for further reading: Michael Gänzle (Alberta), Marco Gobbetti (Bolzano), Luc De Vuyst (VUB Brussels) [@ganzle2014; @gobbetti2012; @devuyst2023]. German classic: Spicher's *Handbuch Sauerteig* [@spicher1982].
