## 11.2 Myths and clichés the sources undercut

1. **Endless camel trains of silk.** Caravans were small; most trade was local; the state was the big spender ✅ [@hansen2005; @hansen2012].
2. **"The Silk Road" as a period term.** Nobody in 750 said it; Richthofen coined *Seidenstraße* in 1877 ✅ [@chin2013; @lareview-myth]. Characters would name specific roads, cities and kingdoms.
3. **Talas and paper** ❌ [@histinfo-paper; @bloom2001; @wp-talas].
4. **Talas as the end of Tang power** ⚠️ [@wp-talas].
5. **"Barbarian" generals as natural traitors**: traditional Chinese history's bias ⚠️ [@skaff2017].
6. **Exotic dancing girls as décor.** Sogdian dancers were often slaves or tribute ✅ [@hansen2005; @wp-whirl]. The power relations belong in the story.
7. **Sogdians as merely merchants**: also farmers, soldiers, officials ✅ [@skaff2003].
8. **Frankopan as a Tang source**: a big-picture bestseller, criticised as analysis-light; use it for scope, not detail [@frankopan2015; @lse-frankopan].

## 11.3 Craft advice from practitioners

- Research daily life first (breakfast, light, heat, fasteners), not just great events. Keep a "rules of the world" sheet ✅ [@hns-language].
- Audit vocabulary for anachronistic idiom [@hns-language]; use terms of address as the main marker of period [@charlie-histfic].
- Kay's "quarter turn to the fantastic" is one answer to the novelist's freedom-versus-accuracy problem [@kay2010; @scalzi-kay].

## 11.4 Sensitivities

- Modern politics: the Silk Road is used in Belt and Road and national-heritage narratives; Chinese claims to Central Asia were real "only intermittently" ✅ that critics argue this [@lareview-myth; @chin2013]. Uzbek, Tajik, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Uyghur and Tibetan readers may read the same events differently [@ehistorykz].
- Slavery was pervasive and legal; depicting it without softening is accurate [@hansen2003; @johnson1979].
