# 1. History: from temple charms to a global studio practice

Legend: ✅ well established · ⚠️ contested or weakly supported · 🔎 verify before relying on it

*Mokuhanga* (木版画, "wood-block print") is the Japanese word for woodblock printing in general; in English it has come to mean specifically the **water-based Japanese method**: pigment and rice paste brushed onto the block, damp handmade paper, registration by *kento* notches, and hand printing with a *baren*, with no press [@wiki-woodblock-japan; @vollmer2015]. Western woodcut, by contrast, usually uses oil-based ink rolled onto the block. That contrast is the natural opening for a workshop talk.

## 1.1 Buddhist beginnings (8th–16th centuries)

- ✅ The earliest dated Japanese printing is the *Hyakumantō Darani* (c. 764–770): Buddhist charms placed in a million small wooden pagodas commissioned by Empress Kōken/Shōtoku [@wiki-woodblock-japan]. Woodblock technology came from China with Buddhism, and for centuries temples in Nara, Kyoto and Kamakura printed mostly religious texts and images in black ink [@wiki-woodblock-japan; @narazaki-1966].
- ⚠️ Popular summaries say the technique "arrived in the 7th century" or "8th century"; the documented physical evidence is the 8th-century charms. Say "by the 8th century" to be safe.

## 1.2 Edo: commercial publishing and ukiyo-e (1603–1868)

- ✅ Movable type arrived around 1590 (Korean and Jesuit presses) and produced the celebrated *Saga-bon* around 1608, but by about 1640 woodblocks had displaced it for almost everything. Woodblocks handled Japanese script, kana and pictures on one surface more cheaply [@wiki-woodblock-japan].
- ✅ Printing served a large, literate urban readership: illustrated books, guidebooks, theatre ephemera, and single-sheet prints of courtesans, kabuki actors and, later, landscapes. These were the pictures of the "floating world" (*ukiyo-e*) [@met-ukiyoe-essay; @met-edo-essay; @wiki-woodblock-japan].
- ✅ **The four-person system.** Each print involved a designer (*eshi*), a carver (*horishi*), a printer (*surishi*) and a publisher (*hanmoto*). The publisher financed, planned and sold the work and often chose the subject [@met-ukiyoe-essay; @ota-tsutaya-2025; @adachi-basics]. Tsutaya Jūzaburō (1750–1797), who promoted Utamaro and was Sharaku's sole publisher, is the classic example [@wiki-tsutaya; @suntory-tsutaya-2010; @hokusai-museum-producers]. The Ota Memorial Museum notes that "Tsutaya Jūzaburō" was a house name carried through five generations [@ota-tsutaya-2025].
- ✅ **Full colour, 1765.** Multi-block colour prints (*nishiki-e*, "brocade pictures") appeared in 1765 around Suzuki Harunobu. They depended on the *kento* registration system [@met-ukiyoe-essay; @wiki-woodblock-japan]. Before that, colour was added by hand or with two or three blocks [@marks2010; @hillier-1960].
- ✅ **New pigments changed the look.** Prussian blue (*bero-ai*), an imported synthetic, became widely available in prints from about the 1820s–30s and underlies the blues of Hokusai's *Great Wave* and Hiroshige's landscapes. It is far more lightfast than the dayflower and indigo blues it replaced [@korenberg-2020; @heritage-kirazuri-2025; @paget2026; @dayflower-study].
- ✅ **Scale.** Publishers reportedly needed to sell at least around 2,000 impressions of a design to make a profit, and experts believe up to about 8,000 impressions of the *Great Wave* were made. Korenberg located 111 surviving originals and traced block wear and replaced colour blocks across them [@korenberg-2020]. (A popular figure of "5,000–15,000" circulates online; Korenberg's published estimate is the better one to quote.)

Good primary descriptions of Edo and Meiji craft practice in English: **Tokuno (1894)**, a Japanese Printing Bureau official's illustrated report to the Smithsonian on woods, tools, carving, printing, pigments, papers and costs (public domain) [@tokuno-1894; @si-tokuno-gift], and Strange's early history [@strange1897].

## 1.3 Meiji decline and Western "discovery" (1868–1910s)

- ✅ Photography, lithography and newspapers took over the practical jobs of woodblock prints after 1868 [@merritt-1990; @wiki-woodblock-japan].
- ✅ Meanwhile prints flooded West and fed *Japonisme*: Impressionists, Arts & Crafts designers and teachers [@wiki-woodblock-japan; @safford2003]. Arthur Wesley Dow studied Hokusai at the Boston Public Library, befriended Ernest Fenollosa, made his own colour woodblocks from 1892, and built the Japanese principles of line, *notan* and colour into his textbook *Composition* (1899) and his teaching at Teachers College, Columbia [@dow-britannica].
- ✅ Westerners went to Japan to learn. Helen Hyde (in Japan c. 1899–1914) worked with carver Matsumoto and printer Murata Shōjirō; Lilian May Miller learned to carve and print herself [@harn-hyde-miller]. In Britain F. Morley Fletcher's *Wood-Block Printing* (1916) taught "the Japanese practice" to Western artists [@fletcher1916], and in Canada Walter J. Phillips popularized the Japanese-style colour woodcut [@wiki-phillips; @phillips-1926]. Frances Gearhart learned via Dow's pupils [@wiki-gearhart].
- ⚠️ Many prints sold in this era were *reproductions from recut blocks*, some so good that experts struggle to tell them from originals. Useful in class when participants ask about the prints they own [@ua-reproductions; @artelino-impressions].

## 1.4 Two 20th-century paths: shin-hanga and sōsaku-hanga

| | **Shin-hanga** (新版画, "new prints") | **Sōsaku-hanga** (創作版画, "creative prints") |
|---|---|---|
| Start | c. 1915, publisher Watanabe Shōzaburō | 1904, Yamamoto Kanae's *Fisherman* |
| Production | Traditional division of labour (designer, carver, printer, publisher) | Artist draws, carves and prints (*jiga, jikoku, jizuri*) |
| Look | Ukiyo-e subjects with Western light, mood, perspective | Personal, modernist, often abstract or folk (mingei) |
| Market | Mostly Western collectors (Toledo shows 1930, 1936) | Japanese art world; post-war international prizes |
| Key names | Kawase Hasui, Yoshida Hiroshi, Hashiguchi Goyō, Ohara Koson | Onchi Kōshirō, Hiratsuka Un'ichi, Munakata Shikō, Maekawa Senpan |

Sources: [@wiki-shin-hanga; @wiki-sosaku-hanga; @artelino-watanabe; @clark-shin-hanga; @merritt-1990; @merritt1992; @artstory-sosaku; @yokogao-onchi; @kitayama2024; @sothebys-modern-prints; @newland1990; @uhlenbeck2018].

- ✅ Yoshida Hiroshi started with Watanabe in 1920 and then set up his own workshop. His *Japanese Wood-block Printing* (1939) is a first-hand technical manual in English [@wiki-yoshida-hiroshi; @yoshida-1939; @bull-yoshida-intro].
- ✅ Munakata Shikō (1903–1975) learned woodblock from Hiratsuka and won top prizes at São Paulo (1955) and Venice (1956) [@munakata-aomori; @honolulu-munakata].
- ⚠️ **A debate worth raising in class:** critics describe shin-hanga as a romanticised, "unchanging" Japan made for foreign buyers [@asianreview-shin-hanga; @wiki-shin-hanga]. Sōsaku-hanga won more critical prestige after the war, partly with backing from the American occupation [@wiki-sosaku-hanga]. The strict "do it all yourself" ideal was also bent in practice: Onchi sometimes used professional carvers and printers [@wiki-sosaku-hanga].
- ✅ Today's international mokuhanga is mostly **sōsaku-hanga in method** (one artist doing everything, often on plywood) using **ukiyo-e tools and materials**. Your workshop sits in that lineage [@mckenna-fundamentals-book; @vollmer2015].

## 1.5 Preservation in Japan and the global revival (1970s–now)

- ✅ In 1978 the ukiyo-e carving and printing techniques were designated a *Selected Conservation Technique* under the Cultural Properties Protection Law, held by a society built on the Tokyo and Kyoto craft associations [@choshugijutsu; @bunka-sentei]. Tokyo certified "Edo moku-hanga" as a traditional craft in 1993, and it was nationally designated in 2007 [@tokyo-edo-mokuhanga].
- ✅ The Adachi Institute (founded 1928) keeps professional carvers and printers working on reproductions and new commissions. The Adachi Foundation for the Preservation of Woodcut Printing was set up in 1994 because ageing craftsmen had too few successors [@adachi-institute; @nippon-adachi; @adachi-internship-ja].
- ✅ Since the 1990s the water-based method has spread internationally, promoted as non-toxic and press-free. MI-LAB residencies and the International Mokuhanga Conferences (the first was in 2011, 🔎 from memory; 2017, 2021 and 2024 are documented, 2027 is planned for Kansas) created a network of teachers [@moreno2026; @milab-transartists; @imc-2024-echizen; @vollmer-milab; @tokyoweekender-revival]. See chapter 9.

## Purpose note (weekend workshop)

- **Essential (15–20 min talk):** water-based vs oil-based; the four-person Edo system versus the one-artist sōsaku approach; 1765 and kento; Prussian blue and the *Great Wave*; the fact that participants will work as sōsaku artists with Edo tools.
- **Optional:** Western lineage (Dow, Hyde, Fletcher, Phillips), which suits Anglophone audiences; the shin-hanga/sōsaku debate; reproductions.
- **Cut:** detailed dynastic and publishing history, and collecting.
- **Visual aids:** pull images from ukiyo-e.org or the ARC portal [@ukiyoe-org; @arc-portal] and show the same design at two states or in two impressions.
