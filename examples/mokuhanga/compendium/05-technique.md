# 5. Technique: from drawing to edition

Legend: ✅ well established · ⚠️ contested or weakly supported · 🔎 verify before relying on it

The sequence below is the one shared by the manuals [@vollmer2015; @salter2001; @boswell2019; @mckenna-fundamentals-book; @wilhide2024; @yoshida-1939; @fletcher1916] and by professional studios [@tanuki-process; @adachi-basics; @tokuno-1894]. The steps are summarized from the sources read. For exact measurements, the manuals are the authority.

## 5.1 Design and colour separation

- ✅ Start with a drawing that breaks down into a **keyblock** (outline, often printed in sumi or a dark colour) plus **colour areas**. Professional prints average about 11 blocks and 20 colours [@tanuki-process]. A first workshop print should use **two blocks or two to three colours**: Yoonmi Nam's intro class ends with a two-layer edition [@highpoint-2025], and Vollmer demonstrates a two-block kingfisher [@vollmer-goldenroad-video].
- ✅ Several colours can be carved on one block if they are far enough apart. Designing for this is a good puzzle for participants [@boswell2019; @salter2001].
- ✅ Keep the image a few centimetres in from the block edge to leave room for the kento [@mcclains-kento-pdf].

## 5.2 Transfer

- Traditional: a thin paper drawing (*hanshita*) pasted face-down and oiled to show the lines [@takezasado-tools; @adachi-basics]. Colour blocks are then made from keyblock proofs (*kyōgō*), printed onto each new block using the kento so everything aligns [@tokuno-1894; @vollmer2015].
- Workshop: tracing-paper transfer (Boswell teaches this method [@boswell2019]) or carbon paper. Remember the image **reverses**.

## 5.3 Kento (registration)

McClain's instructions, read in full [@mcclains-kento-pdf]:

- ✅ Two marks along the **long bottom edge** of the block: the **kagi** ("key"), an L-shaped corner stop at the right, and the **hikitsuke** ("draw stop"), a straight stop about 40 mm in from the left. Both sit at least 6 mm from the block edges.
- ✅ Cut each stop about 2 mm deep with a kento-nomi or a 15 mm flat chisel held vertically. Overlapping the two cuts of the kagi by about 1 mm gives a clean corner.
- ✅ Then pare a **shallow ramp toward the stop**, only two or three paper-thicknesses deep at the mark, so the paper slides into place.
- ✅ The same kento positions go on **every block**, so each sheet can be laid against identical stops.
- ⚠️ Kento errors are the most discouraging beginner failure: misregistered keyblocks are "the kiss of doom" [@rospo-errors]. Cutting kento is a good task to supervise one-to-one. Some teachers pre-cut them 🔎.

## 5.4 Carving

- ✅ Japanese carving outlines every line on **both sides with the hangi-tō** (knife held like a pencil, pulled toward you), then clears the waste with the aisuki and gouges [@bull-carving-tools; @boswell2019; @highpoint-2025].
- ✅ Clear borders and large empty areas thoroughly, otherwise damp paper picks up stray ink or embosses unwanted marks [@rospo-errors].
- ⚠️ Keep cuts shallow in plywood (see chapter 3). Bevel the sides of lines outward for strength 🔎 (standard advice; see [@vollmer2015]).

## 5.5 Preparing paper: dampening

- ✅ Sized paper is **dampened** before printing and kept damp in a stack ("damp pack") for the whole run. This lets the fibres take up pigment under light pressure [@tanuki-process; @highpoint-2025; @rospo-errors].
- 🔎 Typical method: mist or brush alternate sheets, interleave with damp newsprint, wrap in plastic and leave for several hours or overnight so moisture evens out. Nam's students prepare their damp pack before day 2 [@highpoint-2025]. The exact timing varies with paper and climate, so test your paper at home first.
- ⚠️ Too wet: colours bleed and the paper tears. Too dry: blotchy colour, visible grain. Thin papers need a rest between colours [@rospo-errors].

## 5.6 Printing

- ✅ Basic cycle, repeated for each sheet and colour [@tanuki-process; @bm-hiroshige-blog]:
  1. Dampen the block lightly.
  2. Put a dab of nori and a few drops of pigment on the block, then brush them together and spread evenly with the printing brush.
  3. Lay the paper into the kagi first, then the hikitsuke, and let it fall onto the block.
  4. Rub with the baren in small overlapping circles or zigzags, pressing firmly.
  5. Peel, check, and return the sheet to the damp pack.
- ✅ Print order: usually the lightest and largest areas first, keyblock early or last depending on the design [@vollmer2015; @salter2001].
- ✅ Troubleshooting (Andrew Stone's list [@rospo-errors]):

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Unwanted embossing or marks | Borders or open areas not cleared | Clear the block further |
| Blotches or spots | Pigment picked up from uncleared wood; too much moisture | Clear, dry off, control water |
| Keyblock doubled | Paper not seated in the kento; paper or wood swelled | Seat carefully; keep moisture consistent |
| Colours out of register | Colour block kento don't match keyblock | Re-check kento before editioning |
| Bleeding | Printing too wet or with too much pigment | Less water and pigment; heavier paper |
| Blank or uneven patches | Pigment dried on the block before printing | Work faster; reprint (it darkens) |

## 5.7 Special effects (for demos or fast finishers)

- ✅ **Bokashi** (gradation): *ichimonji bokashi*, a straight band, is made by wetting the block and loading one side of the brush. *Ita-bokashi* abrades block edges. *Atenashi bokashi* is freehand. Hiroshige's skies are the classic example [@wiki-bokashi; @unfinished-print-schofield].
- ✅ **Goma-zuri** ("sesame printing": speckled texture from light pigment and pressure), **woodgrain printing**, **karazuri** (blind embossing), **kirazuri** (mica: analysis has now confirmed mica on an ukiyo-e background) [@schofield-mokuhanga; @heritage-kirazuri-2025; @openculture-bull-2020].
- ⚠️ Each effect takes practice. Pick **one** (bokashi) for day 2.

## 5.8 Drying and finishing

- ✅ Dry the prints flat between blotters or boards under light weight. Editioning, signing and aftercare are covered by Boswell [@boswell2019]. Keep finished prints out of strong light, as fugitive colours fade (chapter 3) [@bm-not-fade].
